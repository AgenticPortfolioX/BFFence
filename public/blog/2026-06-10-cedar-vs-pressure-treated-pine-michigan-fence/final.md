# Cedar vs. Pressure-Treated Pine: Choosing the Best Wood for Your Michigan Fence

*Published: June 10, 2026 | By BF Fence*

If you're a Michigan homeowner planning a new wood fence, you've likely narrowed it down to two choices: cedar or pressure-treated pine. It's the classic fencing debate, and for good reason—both are proven materials that have been enclosing yards across Oakland County for decades.

But here's the thing: Michigan's climate isn't like Georgia's or Colorado's or even Ohio's. Our winters dump 30 to 45 inches of snow on average, our summers push humidity into the 70-80% range, and we see 40 to 60 freeze-thaw cycles every single year. That's a gauntlet for any building material.

So which wood stands up best? Should you pay a premium for cedar's natural beauty, or does pressure-treated pine offer better value for your dollar? We've installed hundreds of fences across Auburn Hills, Rochester Hills, Troy, and Farmington Hills—both materials, in every kind of yard. Here's what we've learned.

---

## Why Wood Choice Matters More in Michigan

Before we dive into the specifics, let's talk about why this decision carries more weight here than it might in milder climates. Michigan's continental climate puts unique stresses on wood fencing:

**Freeze-thaw cycles.** When temperatures swing above and below freezing repeatedly, moisture trapped in wood expands and contracts. Over time, this leads to cracking, splitting, and boards pulling away from nails. The freeze-thaw action also affects fence posts set in the ground—frost heave can shift posts by an inch or more if they aren't set deep enough.

**Heavy snow loads.** Snow piled against a fence line means prolonged moisture contact at the base of every board. Wood that isn't naturally resistant to moisture will wick that water up, leading to rot that starts at the bottom and works its way up.

**Humid summers.** July and August in southeast Michigan aren't just hot—they're muggy. Consistently high humidity creates ideal conditions for mold, mildew, and fungal growth on wood surfaces. In shaded fence lines, this can become a real issue within a few seasons.

**UV exposure.** From late spring through early fall, Michigan gets plenty of intense sunshine. UV radiation breaks down wood fibers and strips natural color from untreated wood, turning rich reddish-brown tones into a weathered gray.

The fence you choose needs to hold up to all of this—not just look good on installation day.

## Meet the Contenders

### Pressure-Treated Pine: The Workhorse

Pressure-treated pine (typically Southern Yellow Pine) is the most common wood fencing material in the United States, and for good reason. It's affordable, widely available, and engineered to resist the elements.

The "pressure-treated" process works like this: lumber is placed in a giant vacuum chamber where air is pulled from the wood fibers. Then, a chemical preservative—typically ACQ (Alkaline Copper Quaternary) or Copper Azole—is forced deep into the wood under high pressure. This chemical infusion protects against rot, decay, and insects from the inside out.

**What you get:**
- **Lifespan:** 10–20 years with proper maintenance. Well-maintained PT fences can push toward 20 years or more in favorable conditions.
- **Cost:** $20–$30 per linear foot installed in Michigan (2026 pricing). That's roughly 30–50% less than cedar.
- **Appearance:** Fresh PT lumber has a greenish tint from the copper treatment. It fades to a light brown over 3–6 months but develops an uneven gray if left unstained.
- **Availability:** Readily available at every lumberyard in Oakland County. You can pick it up today.

**The trade-offs:**
- Pressure-treated lumber is delivered wet—really wet. It can take 2–6 weeks to dry to equilibrium moisture content. If you install it wet and stain it too soon, the stain won't penetrate properly and will peel within a year.
- It's more prone to warping, twisting, and checking (surface cracking) than cedar. This is especially true in the first 2–5 years as the wood dries and adjusts to Michigan's humidity swings.
- It requires regular maintenance—cleaning and resealing every 2–3 years—to prevent cracking and maintain its appearance.
- The chemical treatment means you need hot-dipped galvanized or stainless steel fasteners. Regular nails will corrode from the copper preservatives.

### Cedar: The Premium Natural Choice

Cedar—specifically Western Red Cedar—is the preferred material for homeowners who prioritize appearance and longevity. Its natural resistance to rot and insects comes from organic compounds called thujaplicins, which act as natural fungicides and insecticides. No chemicals needed.

**What you get:**
- **Lifespan:** 15–30+ years with proper care. Cedar's natural stability means it ages gracefully and maintains structural integrity far longer than treated pine.
- **Cost:** $30–$45 per linear foot installed in Michigan (2026 pricing). Premium-grade Western Red Cedar runs toward the higher end.
- **Appearance:** Rich reddish-brown tones with fine, straight grain. It weathers to a uniform silvery-gray patina if left unfinished—or you can maintain the warm color with regular staining.
- **Dimensional stability:** Cedar is significantly more stable than pine. It resists cupping, twisting, and warping, which means your fence lines stay straight and true over the years.

**The trade-offs:**
- Higher upfront cost. For a typical 150-foot fence line, cedar can cost $1,500–$4,500 more than pressure-treated pine at installation.
- Cedar's natural oils make it harder to stain evenly. If you want a uniform painted or dark-stained finish, it requires more preparation.
- Not all cedar is created equal. "Cedar fencing" can refer to several species with very different durability profiles. Western Red Cedar is the gold standard; Eastern White Cedar is less rot-resistant.
- Carpenter bees love cedar. In the 10-year Georgia study we referenced, 67% of cedar fences showed carpenter bee activity by year 7, versus 23% for pressure-treated pine.

## Michigan Climate Performance: What the Data Says

We've pulled data from the closest climate analog studies available—including a comprehensive 10-year Georgia study tracking 500+ fence installations and Ontario-based fence research—and translated the findings to Michigan's conditions.

### How Pressure-Treated Pine Holds Up in Michigan

PT pine performs well in Michigan's wet environment, but not without caveats. The chemical treatment makes the wood resistant to rot and insects—even in ground-contact applications. In the Georgia study, 95% of properly installed PT posts were still structurally sound after 10 years.

**The issue is dimensional stability.** Michigan's dramatic seasonal humidity swings cause PT pine to absorb and release moisture continuously. The Georgia study found that PT lumber warped **3x more than cedar** in years 2–5. This happens regardless of lumber quality or installation technique—it's a fundamental property of the wood.

What does that mean for your fence? Boards may cup, twist, or bow over time, creating irregular gaps between pickets. After several Michigan winters, you may notice some boards have pulled away from rails or developed surface cracks—a condition called "checking."

**The good news:** Proper installation mitigates much of this. Setting posts below the frost line (at least 42 inches in Oakland County), using correct fastener spacing, and allowing the wood to acclimate before sealing all help extend the life of a PT fence.

### How Cedar Holds Up in Michigan

Cedar's secret weapon is dimensional stability. The wood has a cellular structure that resists water absorption and dimensional change. When humidity spikes in July, cedar barely moves. When deep winter cold sets in, it doesn't contract and crack the way pine does.

**However—and this is important—cedar's natural protection degrades over time.** The natural oils that make cedar rot-resistant gradually leach out, especially in humid environments. The Georgia study found that cedar lost approximately 40% of its natural oils in the first 3 years when exposed to high humidity.

This means cedar isn't "set it and forget it." Without periodic sealing or staining, eventually the wood becomes vulnerable to the same issues affecting any untreated wood. The difference is that cedar starts from a much better place—it takes longer to degrade, and even degraded, it's more rot-resistant than untreated pine.

**The bottom line for Michigan:** Cedar outperforms PT pine in freeze-thaw stability and long-term appearance. But it still needs maintenance. And in our experience, the carpenter bee issue is real—we've seen more galleries in cedar fences throughout Oakland County than in treated pine.

## Maintenance: What You're Actually Signing Up For

Here's the honest truth about fence maintenance in Michigan:

### Pressure-Treated Pine Maintenance Schedule

**Year 1:**
- Allow the wood to dry for 4–8 weeks after installation before applying any finish
- Apply a high-quality water-repellent stain or sealant once the wood is dry
- Check for initial warping or nail pops after the first freeze-thaw cycle

**Every 2–3 years:**
- Power wash the fence to remove dirt, mildew, and loose stain
- Inspect for rot at ground level, loose boards, and protruding nails
- Reapply stain or sealant

**After 10 years:**
- Expect to replace 5–15% of boards that have warped or rotted at the bottom
- Check post integrity at ground level

### Cedar Maintenance Schedule

**Year 1:**
- Cedar can be left natural or stained immediately (no drying period needed)
- If staining, oil-based stains work best and require more prep work
- The natural color will begin fading within 3–6 months if left unstained

**Every 3–4 years (if stained):**
- Clean with a mild wood cleaner (avoid harsh pressure washing)
- Lightly sand and reapply stain
- Spot-treat any mildew-prone areas

**Every 5–7 years (if left natural):**
- Clean and apply a clear water repellent to extend life
- The silver-gray patina is attractive but doesn't protect against moisture

**After 15 years:**
- Some board replacement may be needed, typically far fewer than PT pine
- Posts should be checked structurally

## The Decision Guide: Which One Is Right for You?

We've installed both materials in yards across Auburn Hills, Rochester Hills, Troy, Farmington Hills, and beyond. Here's how we help our customers decide:

### Choose Pressure-Treated Pine If:

**You're on a tighter budget.** There's no shame in this. PT pine is a legitimate, durable fencing material that will protect your property for 15+ years with proper care. The savings let you invest in other home improvements.

**You're planning to move within 5–10 years.** You won't be around long enough to see the cedar's long-term advantages, and PT pine's lower upfront cost gives you a better ROI at sale time.

**You want a painted fence.** PT pine takes paint exceptionally well. If your HOA requires white fencing or you want a specific color, PT pine is the better canvas.

**You have carpenter bee problems already.** If your neighborhood has an active carpenter bee population, PT pine's chemical treatment offers better resistance.

### Choose Cedar If:

**You're staying put for 15+ years.** Cedar's longer lifespan and better aging characteristics pay off when you're living with the fence for the long term.

**Low maintenance is a priority.** Cedar needs less frequent attention. If staining every 2–3 years sounds like a chore you'd rather skip, cedar's natural silver-gray patina is a legitimate low-maintenance option.

**Appearance matters most.** There's no substitute for the warm, natural look of cedar. It adds curb appeal in a way that stained PT pine just can't match.

**You live in a shaded yard.** Cedar's natural resistance to mildew and rot gives it a meaningful edge in damp, shaded fence lines where PT pine can develop fungal growth faster.

## Installation Tips for Michigan Fences

Whichever wood you choose, these installation best practices are critical for Michigan's climate:

**Set posts deep.** Michigan's frost line reaches 42 inches in Oakland County. Your fence posts should be set a minimum of 36–42 inches deep with concrete below the frost line. Shallow posts will heave and shift within two winters.

**Use proper drainage.** Backfill around posts with gravel before concrete to allow water to drain away from the post base. Water pooling at the base of posts is the number one cause of premature rot—in both cedar and PT pine.

**Elevate bottom boards.** Keep fence boards 2–3 inches off the ground. Direct ground contact is the fastest way to rot any wood, regardless of treatment.

**Use correct fasteners.** For PT pine: hot-dipped galvanized or stainless steel only. For cedar: stainless steel prevents black staining from tannin reactions. We use only coated deck screws on every job—never staples or ring shanks.

**Allow for expansion.** Don't install fence boards tight against each other. Leave a 1/8-inch gap to allow for seasonal expansion and contraction. This prevents buckling in summer and cracking in winter.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does cedar last longer than pressure-treated pine in Michigan?

Yes—on average, cedar fences last 5–10 years longer than pressure-treated pine in Michigan's climate. Cedar's dimensional stability gives it a significant advantage through freeze-thaw cycles. However, the gap narrows if the PT pine is well-maintained and the cedar is neglected.

### Is pressure-treated pine safe for vegetable gardens?

Modern ACQ-treated pine is considered safe for use near vegetable gardens once the chemicals have fully cured (typically 6–12 months). The EPA no longer允许 CCA (chromated copper arsenate) for residential use. That said, if you're concerned, cedar is the safer choice for garden boundaries.

### Can pressure-treated pine be stained to look like cedar?

Yes. High-quality semi-transparent stains in "cedar tone" colors can give PT pine a very convincing cedar-like appearance. It won't have the same grain pattern or natural warmth, but from 10 feet away, most people won't notice the difference.

### How much does a cedar fence cost in Michigan in 2026?

Expect to pay $30–$45 per linear foot installed for a standard 6-foot cedar fence in southeast Michigan. The final price depends on lumber grade (clear vs. knotty), height, and site conditions. Premium Western Red Cedar runs toward the higher end; Eastern White Cedar is more budget-friendly but less durable.

### Which wood is better for a shaded fence line?

Cedar. Its natural resistance to moisture and fungal growth makes it the better choice for shaded areas where the fence stays damp longer. Pressure-treated pine in shaded areas tends to develop surface mildew and algae more quickly.

### Do I need to seal my cedar fence?

Sealing a cedar fence is optional but recommended. Unsealed cedar weathers to an attractive silvery-gray but loses some of its natural rot resistance faster. Sealing every 3–4 years preserves the warm color and extends the wood's lifespan. If you love the silver-gray look, you can skip sealing—just know you may need to replace some boards a few years sooner.

## The Bottom Line

Both cedar and pressure-treated pine can give you a beautiful, functional fence that protects your property and adds value to your home. The right choice comes down to your budget, your timeline, and your tolerance for maintenance.

**Pressure-treated pine** is the smart, practical choice for budget-conscious homeowners and those who plan to move within 5–10 years. It's durable, affordable, and proven—you just have to stay on top of the maintenance.

**Cedar** is the premium choice for long-term homeowners who want natural beauty with less frequent upkeep. It costs more upfront but often proves more economical over a 20-year horizon when you factor in reduced maintenance and longer lifespan.

Here's the most important thing to remember: **The installer matters as much as the material.** A poorly installed cedar fence will fail long before a well-installed pressure-treated pine fence. Deep posts, proper drainage, correct fasteners, and professional craftsmanship make the difference between a fence that lasts 10 years and one that lasts 30.
