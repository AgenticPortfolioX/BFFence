## What a Wooded Clarkston-Area Yard Actually Needs From a Fence

Most fence websites start with materials. We start with the problem, because on a wooded lot there are usually four of them at once.

**1. No visible boundaries.** On acreage, the mowed line is not the property line. Nobody ever measured it — it's just where somebody stopped mowing in 1998. Fence it as-is and you can end up with a fence on your neighbor's land, which is the most expensive mistake on this list because it means moving it.

**2. Sightlines from the road.** Rural-edge lots often have more road exposure than suburban ones, not less — the houses sit far back, the trees are bare half the year, and headlights sweep a long open frontage. That is a privacy problem in the summer and a *"who's out there"* problem in November.

**3. Deer, pets, and kids, all at once.** Independence Township has serious deer pressure. Independence Oaks County Park — the largest park in Oakland County at 1,286 acres — is right there, and the township runs an active deer management program because the herd is heavy enough to affect the habitat. A fence on a wooded lot is usually doing two jobs at the same time: keeping the dog in and keeping the deer out of the hostas and arborvitae.

**4. Grade, trees, and water.** Wooded lots are rarely flat. They drain somewhere, they have root systems you can't see until you dig, and they often have a low spot that turns into a swamp every March.

A fence that ignores any one of those four things gets ripped out, repaired, or resented inside of three years. So the first week of a project like this is not about fence panels. It's about reading the lot.

## Why the Survey Comes Before the Fence

On a wooded Independence Township lot, the fence line should be set from a survey, not from the mowed edge and not from where the old wire fence sagged.

Here is what we're looking for before anything gets ordered:

| What we check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Recorded property lines** | The fence goes on your side of the line. On acreage, the visible handshake line and the recorded line are frequently 3–10 feet apart. |
| **Utility and drainage easements** | An easement has to stay accessible. If a fence crosses one, you may need a gate the utility can open. |
| **Grade change along the run** | A run that drops 30 inches across 100 feet needs stepped panels, not a straight line of panels with daylight underneath. |
| **Root flares and large trees** | You can build near trees. You cannot trench through a 14-inch root flare with a machine and expect the tree to forgive you. |
| **The septic field, if there is one** | Nobody wants a post in the drain field. Rural-edge and older properties still have them. |
| **Where water sits after a hard rain** | Post footings in a seasonal wet spot need drainage gravel and, sometimes, a different post material entirely. |

We walked this lot twice before a single board was ordered. The line moved about three feet on the second walk after the survey was flagged — three feet that would have put the entire north run on the wrong side of the line. That is the whole argument for calling a surveyor first. A survey costs a few hundred dollars. Moving a finished fence costs thousands.

### Village of Clarkston or Independence Township? The Permit Split

This catches almost everybody, so let's be precise about it.

There are **two governments** in the Clarkston area:

- The **Village of Clarkston** — small, walkable, historic, with its own zoning ordinance.
- **Independence Township** — everything around the village, including the wooded acreage lots, with its own separate zoning ordinance.

Permitting for both runs through **one building department**: the Independence Township Building Department, at 6483 Waldon Center Drive, Clarkston, MI 48346, (248) 625-8111, open Monday through Thursday, 7:00 a.m. to 5:30 p.m. The Village of Clarkston's building permits are administered through the township office.

What you need to know before you call:

- **Michigan Residential Code** generally exempts standard residential fences under 7 feet from *building permit* requirements. That exemption does **not** exempt you from local **zoning** rules on height, placement, and materials.
- Independence Township's zoning ordinance defines a fence broadly — "any permanent partition, structure or gate erected as a dividing marker, barrier or enclosure" — and regulates fences, walls, and other protective barriers under **Article 10, Section 10.05** of the ordinance.
- The township's residential districts run from **R-1A** (single-family) through **R-1R** (rural residential). Which district your lot sits in affects setbacks and placement, and it is worth confirming that yourself rather than trusting a mailing address.
- Across Oakland County, the common pattern is 6 feet maximum in side and rear yards, shorter or restricted front-yard fencing, and visibility requirements on corner lots. Confirm the specifics for your lot — a five-minute phone call to the building department beats guessing.

If your lot also has deed restrictions or a subdivision association, those can be stricter than the ordinance and they still bind you. And if a fence disagreement is about cost or maintenance rather than the line itself, Michigan's **Fence Viewers Act (Act 34 of 1978)** exists for that: a written request plus $25 to the township treasurer gets a fence viewer involved, and they issue a binding decision within seven days. Fence viewers cannot settle boundary disputes. For that, you need a surveyor.

## The Plan: A Custom Cedar Privacy Fence With a Double Driveway Gate

Once the line is settled, the plan comes together quickly. Here's what this project called for.

| Detail | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Fence style** | Board-on-board privacy fence, panels stepped to grade |
| **Material** | Western red cedar pickets and rails; pressure-treated ground-contact posts |
| **Height** | 6 feet in the side and rear runs |
| **Run length** | Approximately 340 linear feet, with two step sections following the grade |
| **Gates** | One 14-foot double driveway gate; one 4-foot walk gate on the far side |
| **Post setting** | 8-foot posts, 48 inches in the ground, belled concrete footings below the frost line |
| **Road side** | Cedar board-on-board with a decorative top rail, finished side facing out |
| **Interior side** | Same construction, rails facing in for a clean neighbor presentation |

Two things stand out on a lot like this. First, the **run is long** — a suburban backyard is often around 200 linear feet, and this one was well past 300. Second, a long run on uneven ground needs **steps**, not slopes, or you get a fence that looks drunk from the road and leaves a gap at every low point for a small dog to find.

### Why Cedar — and Where Pressure-Treated Still Makes Sense

Cedar is not the cheap option. It is the option that makes sense on a long run in Michigan, and here is the honest reasoning.

| Material | Installed cost (2026) | Lifespan | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Western red cedar** | $35–$40/linear ft average; $45–$90 for high-grade clear boards | 20–25+ years | Long runs, visible sides, homeowners who want the fence to weather gracefully |
| **Pressure-treated pine** | $45–$90/linear foot for high-grade; lower-grade treated for less | 10–20 years with sealing | Posts (ground contact), budget-driven runs, utility areas |
| **Cedar pickets + treated posts** | Most common hybrid spec | 20+ years | The balance most Michigan homeowners land on |
| **Vinyl** | Varies; premium to cedar | 20–30 years | Homeowners who never want to reseal anything |
| **Composite** | $55–$85/linear ft | 25–30+ years | Newer planned communities with strict CC&Rs |

Cedar earns its money in Michigan for three reasons:

1. **It handles freeze-thaw.** Cedar moves with moisture instead of checking apart like untreated pine. Over 40–60 freeze-thaw cycles a year, that matters.
2. **It resists rot and insects naturally.** No chemical treatment needed to make the pickets last.
3. **It ages well.** Left to weather, cedar goes silver-gray. Maintained with a stain every few years, it stays warm and rich. Either way, it does not look cheap from the road.

Where we still use pressure-treated wood: **posts**. Ground-contact posts are the part of a fence that lives in wet soil, and pressure-treated lumber is the right material for that job. The best fence for a Michigan lot is usually a hybrid — cedar where you see it, treated where you don't.

### Why Board-on-Board, Not Standard Privacy

Standard privacy fence has pickets on one side of the rails with small gaps between boards. Board-on-board overlaps the pickets so the boards cover the gaps on the rails, closing the fence completely — no vertical light leaks, no sightline through seams.

On a wooded lot, board-on-board does two extra jobs:

- **It blocks sightlines from the road and from the neighbor's second story.** Overlap is the difference between "mostly private" and actually private.
- **It presents a real face on both sides.** Boards cover the rails on the neighbor-facing side too, so the fence reads as intentional construction rather than a fence with its back turned.

### What Changes When a Fence Is a Long Run

Long runs change the engineering, not just the material order:

- **More posts, more footings, more concrete.** A 340-foot run is roughly 60 line posts — plus gate and corner posts set deeper than the rest.
- **Terrain labor.** Industry calculators put a slight slope at about +10% labor, a moderate slope at +25%, and a steep stepped run at +50%. A wooded acreage lot is almost never the flat case.
- **Expansion and contraction.** Long continuous runs need room to move. We plan panel joints and bracing so a hard freeze does not pop fasteners down the line.
- **Access matters.** A long run means a long walk for every load of material. That is part of why real estimates vary so much on acreage.

## The Driveway Gate: The Part Most Estimates Get Wrong

A 14-foot double driveway gate is not two walk gates bolted together. It is a small structure, and it is the part of a fence that fails first when it is built casually.

What this gate required:

- **Gate posts sized up and set deeper.** Gate posts carry the cantilever load of two swinging leaves. We set them 4–6 inches deeper than line posts, in concrete, below the frost line.
- **Diagonal bracing on each leaf.** Without a diagonal in the frame, a wide wood gate sags. Period. It is not a matter of if, but of when.
- **Structural hinges, not decorative ones.** A 7-foot-wide cedar leaf is heavy, and it gets heavier after rain.
- **Ground clearance planned around freeze-thaw.** A gate that clears by half an inch in September will drag in February. We plan clearance so the leaves still swing after a Michigan thaw-lift cycle.
- **Swing vs. slide.** On a wooded lot, gate swing needs a clear arc. If the driveway is tight or the grade is steep, a sliding or rolling gate can be the better answer, and it costs more because of the hardware.

**Driveway gate cost in Michigan, 2026:** you are generally looking at roughly $800 for a simple 10-foot driveway gate, $300–$1,000+ for a basic double gate, and **$1,200–$3,500 or more** for a double driveway gate with automated openers. A walk gate runs about $150–$400. Automation adds electronics, safety sensors, and a power run — which is why "we'll add the opener later" often costs more than doing it at the same time.

If you want a driveway gate, say so on day one. Retrofitting gate posts on a finished fence line means tearing out finished work.

## Building Around Trees and Grade Without Gaps

This is the part of a wooded-lot project that separates a fence that lasts from one that gets patched every spring.

**Trees.** You can build near a tree. You cannot trench through its root flare. Roots within the fence line get cut by hand, cleanly, one at a time — not ripped out with a machine. Where a large tree genuinely sits in the line, the better move is usually to jog the fence around it and build a short return, giving the trunk root space and keeping the line intact.

**Grade.** Panels step. Where the ground drops, each panel sits level at its own elevation, with the step hidden at the post. That keeps the bottom of the fence tight to the ground — no daylight gap for a dog to work at, no visible slope that makes the whole run look crooked.

**Water.** Footings in low, wet spots get a gravel drainage layer under the concrete so the post isn't sitting in a bucket. Downspouts get redirected. A post that lives in standing water is a post with an expiry date, no matter what it is made of.

**Deer.** A 6-foot fence changes deer behavior at a garden, but it does not stop a determined deer on a hard winter. Where browsing pressure is real, the fence protects the perimeter and the plantings behind it still get protection for their first few winters. Anyone promising a 6-foot wood fence will eliminate deer is selling, not building.

## How Deep Do Fence Posts Go in Michigan?

This is the single most important number on a Michigan fence project, and it is the one most often gotten wrong.

**Michigan Residential Code R403.1.4 requires exterior footings and foundation systems to extend 42 inches below grade**, and Michigan frost depth runs about 42–48 inches depending where you are. Best practice is to set the bottom of the footing at least **6 inches below** the published frost line.

That means:

- **A 6-foot privacy fence** typically uses an 8-foot post with **48 inches in the ground**.
- The old "bury one-third of the post" rule of thumb works out to about 36 inches for a 6-foot fence — **that is not deep enough in Michigan clay.**
- Concrete gets **belled** at the bottom, wider than the post, so frost heave has to lift the whole bell to move the post. Unbelled footings lift and settle and lift again.
- The concrete gets **crowned and sloped away** from the post so water runs off instead of pooling at the base.
- A **rot barrier sleeve** at grade covers roughly the top 2 inches above ground through the top 6 inches of burial — the band where rot starts.
- **Gate and corner posts** go 4–6 inches deeper than line posts.

A post set 30 inches deep in Michigan clay is not a fence post. It is a temporary post. It will be leaning in three winters, and you can read exactly why in our guide to [leaning fence posts in Michigan](/blog/2026-09-12-leaning-fence-posts-michigan-repair).

## The Installation: What a Long-Run Project Looks Like

Every wooded lot is different, but the sequence holds:

| Phase | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Lot walk and survey review** | Confirm property lines, easements, grade, roots, septic, drainage. Line gets staked. |
| **2. Permit and HOA check** | Call the Independence Township Building Department; confirm district, height, and placement. Get HOA or deed-restriction approval in writing if it applies. |
| **3. Layout and staking** | Posts marked to the inch, with step points called out and gate openings checked against actual vehicle width. |
| **4. Hand clearing along the line** | Roots cut by hand, brush cleared, grade skimmed only where necessary. |
| **5. Post holes and footings** | 48-inch holes for line posts, deeper for gates and corners, gravel layer in wet spots, belled concrete. |
| **6. Posts set and braced** | Plumbed, braced, and left to cure before any load goes on. |
| **7. Rails and framing** | Rails set level per panel section, following the steps. |
| **8. Pickets and board-on-board overlap** | The long part of the job — roughly twice the pickets of a standard privacy fence. |
| **9. Gates and hardware** | Gate posts checked again, frames braced diagonally, hinges and latches installed, swing tested. |
| **10. Cleanup and walkthrough** | Site raked, materials hauled, every gate and latch demonstrated. |

**Timeline:** a typical suburban yard is a few days of build. A 340-foot run with grade change, tree conflicts, and two gates is meaningfully longer — plan on a working week plus, with footings curing in the middle of it. Anyone promising a two-day finish on a long wooded run is not planning to set posts properly.

Booking is the other timeline. Fall is the best window in Michigan, and September bookings typically run **1 to 3 weeks out**. The ground is still workable, concrete cures before the first hard freeze, and the footing gets locked in before freeze-thaw starts prying at it.

## The After: What the Yard Feels Like Now

The transformation on a wooded lot is quieter than on a suburban one, but it is bigger.

Before: a yard that worked if everybody behaved. The dog was on a long line or a radio collar that mostly worked. The garden had a fence ring around it that looked like an apology. Headlights swept the frontage all evening. The kids played anywhere they wanted, which meant everywhere, which meant the property line kept coming up in conversation with neighbors.

After: an edge. The dog runs the whole yard, off leash, inside the line. The garden behind it is planted like it's staying. The road side is a finished cedar face from the street instead of a view into the side yard. And the driveway can be closed off with a 14-foot double gate when the family is away or the trailer is parked in back.

That is what fencing actually buys on a wooded lot — not just privacy, but **a defined piece of ground that finally behaves like it belongs to you.** It's protecting what matters, and it's privacy you can see from every window.

## Mistakes We See on Wooded Lots

We have fixed enough fences to know the patterns. These are the ones that cost Clarkston-area homeowners the most.

1. **Fencing to the mowed line instead of the survey.** The most expensive mistake on this list. If the fence crosses the line, it moves — at your cost, after your neighbor notices.
2. **Setting posts shallower than the frost line.** The fence looks perfect for two winters, then starts to wander. Every post leaning in different directions is an underground problem, not a fence problem.
3. **Skipping the gate post engineering.** Gates sag when gate posts are the same as line posts. The gate is the highest-load point on the entire fence line.
4. **Building tight to the ground with no step plan.** Panels that slope with the grade leave gaps; panels that span low spots look like a fence floating over a ditch. Step them.
5. **Trenching through tree roots with a machine.** You save a day of labor and inherit a dying tree, a root crown that decays, and sometimes a hole under the fence where the roots rotted out.
6. **Forgetting the deer, then blaming the fence.** A 6-foot fence changes deer patterns. It is not a wildlife barrier. Plan plantings accordingly.
7. **Putting a post in the drainage easement.** Utility access has to survive your fence line. Plan for it, or plan to take the fence apart later.
8. **Waiting until the ground freezes.** Foundations set in frozen or saturated ground are unreliable. In Michigan, the window closes fast in November.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does a cedar privacy fence cost in Clarkston, Michigan?

A cedar privacy fence in the Clarkston area generally runs $35–$40 per linear foot installed in 2026, with high-grade western red cedar higher, from $45 to $90 per linear foot. A typical 200-linear-foot cedar project runs $3,700 to $9,300, averaging around $6,500. Wooded acreage lots usually need longer runs and include a terrain adjustment — a moderate slope typically adds about 25% in labor, and a steep stepped run up to 50% — so expect a 300-foot-plus run plus a driveway gate to land above the typical 200-foot benchmark. Get a written, itemized estimate for your specific lot.

### Do I need a permit for a fence in the Village of Clarkston or Independence Township?

Your zoning rules depend on which of the two you live in — the Village of Clarkston has its own zoning ordinance, and Independence Township has a separate one — but permitting for both is administered through the Independence Township Building Department at 6483 Waldon Center Drive (248-625-8111, Monday–Thursday, 7:00 a.m.–5:30 p.m.). Michigan Residential Code generally exempts standard residential fences under 7 feet from building permit requirements, but local zoning still governs height, placement, and materials, and the township's ordinance regulates fences under Article 10, Section 10.05. Confirm your district (R-1A through R-1R) and any deed restrictions before you dig.

### How deep should fence posts be set in Michigan?

Michigan Residential Code R403.1.4 requires exterior footings to extend 42 inches below grade, and Michigan frost depth runs 42 to 48 inches depending on the region. Best practice is to set the bottom of the footing at least 6 inches below the published frost line, so a 6-foot privacy fence commonly uses an 8-foot post with 48 inches in the ground. The old "one-third of the post in the ground" rule gives you about 36 inches on a 6-foot fence, which is not deep enough in Michigan clay. Gate and corner posts should go 4 to 6 inches deeper than line posts.

### What is the best fence material for a wooded lot in Michigan?

For the visible parts of the fence, western red cedar is the best balance of longevity, appearance, and Michigan weather performance; it weathers to silver-gray or takes a stain well and holds up through freeze-thaw cycles. For the posts, pressure-treated lumber is the right choice because it's the part in permanent ground contact. Vinyl and composite are legitimate alternatives, especially in newer subdivisions with strict CC&Rs, and both will cost more up front. The hybrid spec — cedar pickets and rails on pressure-treated posts — is what most Michigan homeowners end up choosing.

### Can you install a fence around trees without killing them?

Usually, yes, if the fence line is planned before any digging happens. Roots inside the fence trench get cut by hand, cleanly, rather than being torn out with a machine. Where a large tree sits directly in the line, the better approach is to jog the fence around the trunk and build a short return, which gives the tree root space and keeps the run looking intentional. Fencing right up against a large trunk is the pattern that causes decline, because the root flare stays wet and the trunk can't breathe.

### What does a double driveway gate cost in Michigan?

In 2026, a simple 10-foot driveway gate runs around $800, a basic double gate typically adds $300 to $1,000 or more to a fence project, and a double driveway gate with automated openers generally runs $1,200 to $3,500 or more depending on width, hardware, and whether a power run is needed. Gate posts have to be sized up and set 4 to 6 inches deeper than line posts, and each swinging leaf needs diagonal bracing, so gates should be planned at the start of the project rather than added to a finished fence line.

### How long does a fence installation take on an acreage lot?

A typical suburban yard can be fenced in a few days, but a long run on a wooded acreage lot takes longer — plan on a working week or more for roughly 340 feet with grade changes, tree conflicts, and two gates, since footings need time to cure before load goes on them. Material staging and access on a long run add time as well. The other timeline is booking: fall is the best install window in Michigan and September is typically booked 1 to 3 weeks out.

### Will a fence keep deer out of my garden in Clarkston?

A 6-foot fence changes deer movement and protects most of what's behind it, but a determined deer on a hard winter can still get through or over a standard residential fence, and Independence Township does carry heavy deer pressure given the habitat around Independence Oaks County Park. The realistic approach is a 6-foot cedar privacy fence as the perimeter, plus protection for young or especially palatable plantings inside it for their first few winters. Anyone promising that a 6-foot wood fence eliminates deer browsing is overstating what any fence can do.
