# How to Clean a Wood Fence in Michigan: Leaf Stains, Green Algae, Mildew, and the Gray That Isn't Dirt

Walk any Oakland County backyard in mid-November and you will find the same scene: a mat of wet maple leaves packed against the bottom rail, a green film creeping up the shaded north face of the pickets, and a homeowner standing there with a pressure washer they rented for the weekend. Sometimes that story ends with a clean fence. More often it ends with fuzzy, furrowed cedar and a call to us in March.

Cleaning a wood fence is not hard, but it is not one job either. What most people call "a dirty fence" is usually one of six different problems, and three of them get measurably worse if you attack them with too much water pressure. The good news is that cleaning is the cheapest thing you will ever do for a fence — a few hours and forty dollars of cleaner can add years to boards that would otherwise be rotting from the ground up by spring.

This is the guide we would give a neighbor: how to figure out what you are actually looking at, what to use, what numbers to hold, and what the Michigan calendar allows you to finish before the frost goes in.

## Six Problems, Not One

Before you buy anything, spend ten minutes looking instead of spraying. Algae, moss, mildew, lichen, tannin stains, and plain sun-bleached gray each need a different approach, and the difference matters because one of them is not dirt at all — it is the wood itself breaking down.

| What you see | What it actually is | Why it shows up | What fixes it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thin green film, slippery when wet | Algae | Shade, humidity, organic debris holding moisture | Oxygen bleach, soft brush, low-pressure rinse |
| Thick green cushion, holds water | Moss | Long-term damp, debris in board gaps, poor drainage | Scrape off, clean, fix the drainage |
| Black or gray-black spotting, blotchy | Mildew | Leaf mats, north-facing shade, sprinkler overspray | Oxygen bleach with 10–15 minutes of dwell time |
| Crusty gray-green or orange patches | Lichen | Established damp surface, older wood | Longer dwell, soft brush; may leave a faint outline |
| Brown leaf-shaped outline | Tannin stain | Water-soluble extractives from wet leaves | Oxalic acid brightener |
| Even silver-gray, no growth at all | UV degradation of lignin | Sun | Brightener (partial), sanding, or acceptance |

Probe the bottom of a picket with a screwdriver before you do anything. If the tip sinks in easily, the surface is not your problem — the board is finished, and no amount of cleaning brings it back. We will come back to that.

## Why Michigan Fences Go Green on One Side Only

Algae needs three things: moisture, shade, and a surface to sit on. In Oakland County we hand it all three. Our summers run humid, our mature subdivisions — the wooded lots in Clarkston, the tree-lined streets off Tienken in Rochester Hills, the older blocks in Bloomfield — throw heavy shade on the north and east faces of a fence for most of the day, and our clay soils hold water far longer than the sandy stuff you read about in national guides.

Add the fourth ingredient, food, and the fence becomes a habitat. That food is leaf litter, grass clippings, mulch, and the fine organic dust that accumulates in the gap between boards. It sits against the wood, holds moisture for days after the rest of the yard has dried, and feeds the growth you are trying to remove.

This is why the shaded side of a fence goes green while the sunny side stays gold, and why the two sides of the same fence age at completely different rates. It is also why cleaning without removing the moisture source is a yearly subscription instead of a fix. If a fence line sits under a downspout that dumps at the post base, or a sprinkler head that hammers the same six boards every morning, you can wash it every spring and it will be green again by August.

Two local wrinkles worth knowing. First, road salt. If you are on a corner lot or a through-street in Auburn Hills or Troy, plow spray lands on the bottom two feet of fence all winter, and salt pulls moisture into the wood. Second, snow pile. Shoveled snow stacked against a fence in January sits there until March, and that is the wettest eight weeks of the year for the bottom rail. We will cover both in the seasonal routine below.

## What Wet Leaves Actually Do to a Fence

Leaves look harmless. They are not.

A pile of wet leaves packed against a fence is a moisture blanket. It blocks airflow, so the wood underneath stays wet long after the surrounding boards have dried in the sun. Federal guidance on mold and moisture uses a rough rule of thumb that damp materials dried within 24 to 48 hours usually will not grow mold — a leaf mat keeps boards damp for weeks, which is well past that window.

It also stains. Leaves contain tannins, which are water-soluble extractives. When rain runs through a pile of leaves and sits against a board, those tannins bleed into the wood and leave a brown, leaf-shaped shadow that does not come off with soap. On a light-colored cedar fence, a single season of packed leaves can leave a visible outline that only an oxalic acid brightener will lift.

And underneath the stain, the wood is quietly losing its finish. A leaf mat holds water against the bottom rail and the lower twelve inches of every picket — the exact places where rot starts, because that is where water, soil contact, and splashing all converge. This is why a fence can look structurally fine from the street and be soft at the base when you push on it. If you have ever wondered why we tell people that a fence fails from the bottom up, this is the mechanism.

The practical takeaway is simple: leaf removal is not landscaping. It is maintenance, and in November it is the highest-value ten minutes you will spend on your property.

## The Order of Operations

Do these steps in order. Skipping ahead is how people end up sealing dirt into the wood or brightening a board that needed replacing.

1. **Inspect first.** Walk the line, push each post, probe the bottom of three or four pickets with a screwdriver. Anything soft gets flagged for repair, not cleaning.
2. **Clear the debris.** Rake or blow leaves, grass, and mulch away from the fence line. Clear the gaps between boards.
3. **Fix the water.** Redirect downspouts, adjust sprinklers, pull mulch back two inches from the wood.
4. **Clean.** Oxygen bleach, soft brush, low-pressure rinse. Work bottom to top so dirty water does not run over clean boards.
5. **Brighten if needed.** Oxalic acid on tannin and iron stains, and on gray wood you want to bring back.
6. **Let it dry.** Forty-eight hours minimum, and check moisture content before any finish goes on.
7. **Repair or finish.** Replace soft boards, then stain or seal — but only if the temperature cooperates.

Steps one through five you can do this month. Step seven is where Michigan's calendar starts making decisions for you.

## Oxygen Bleach, Not Chlorine

If you remember one thing from this guide, make it this: use oxygen bleach on a wood fence, not chlorine bleach.

Oxygen bleach — sold as sodium percarbonate powder or as a ready-made deck and fence cleaner — kills mildew and algae and lifts the gray layer off weathered wood without breaking down the wood itself. It breaks down into oxygen, water, and soda ash, which is why it is far easier on the grass and shrubs along your fence line. The Western Red Cedar Lumber Association names oxygen bleach as its preferred mildew cleaner for cedar, and that recommendation is worth more than any label claim on a bottle.

Chlorine bleach will absolutely kill mildew. It will also attack lignin, the natural polymer that glues wood fibers together. Strip lignin and the surface goes fuzzy and weak, cedar loses the warm color people paid for, and the wood becomes harder for stain to bond to. You win the algae battle and lose the finish.

For plain dirt with no mildew or algae, mild non-phosphate detergent and water is enough. Skip dish soap with a degreaser if you plan to finish afterward — residue interferes with stain penetration.

**How to apply it:**

- Mix according to the label in a pump sprayer or bucket. Do not improvise a stronger batch; dwell time does the work, not concentration.
- Wet the fence first, then apply from the bottom up, a few boards at a time. Bottom-up prevents the streaks you get when cleaner runs down dry wood.
- Let it dwell three to five minutes for light growth, ten to fifteen for black mildew. Do not let it dry on the surface — mist it if it starts to.
- Scrub stubborn spots with a soft nylon brush, working with the grain. Never a wire brush: it scars cedar and leaves steel particles behind that rust into the wood.
- Rinse top to bottom with a garden hose or a wide-fan pressure washer on its lowest setting.
- Wet nearby plants before you start and rinse them after.

If the growth will not come off after a proper dwell and a scrub, the answer is more dwell time or a proper wood cleaner — not more pressure. More pressure removes wood.

## Pressure Washing: The Numbers

Yes, you can pressure wash a wood fence. Most of the damage people blame on pressure washers is really damage from the wrong nozzle, held too close, at the wrong angle — and cedar is the least forgiving wood in the yard.

| Surface | Working PSI | Tip | Standoff | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New or soft cedar pickets | 500–800 | 40° (white) | 12–18 in | Start at the bottom of the range; fuzz appears fast |
| Weathered, sound cedar | 800–1,200 | 25° (green) or 40° | 12–18 in | Only on wood that is still solid |
| Pressure-treated pine | 800–1,200 | 25°–40° | 12–15 in | Grain raises easily; keep moving |
| Do not use | 1,500+ or a 0° (red) tip | — | — | Cuts permanent grooves in softwood |

The industry guidance ranges from roughly 500 PSI on the careful end to 1,200–1,500 PSI on the aggressive end for wood, with the lower numbers applying to soft species like cedar and pine. In practice, we never go above about 1,200 PSI on a residential cedar fence, and we spend most of the job in the 500–800 range.

Work with the grain, keep the wand moving, and never park it in one spot. Pausing is how you get the tiger stripes that no amount of sanding fully hides.

Here is the rule that matters more than any number on that table: **if the wood starts to look fuzzy, hairy, or splintered, stop.** That fuzz is fiber. You are no longer removing dirt — you are removing cedar. Back the wand off, widen the tip, and let the cleaner do the work. Test a hidden board first, near a gate or behind a shrub, and use that setting for the rest of the fence.

Two more cautions. Do not pressure wash a fence that still has failing stain or paint flaking off — you will drive water under the edges and blow off more finish than you intended. And do not pressure wash in the two days before a hard freeze. Water forced into board gaps and end grain has nowhere to go, and freezing water expands.

## Tannin, Iron, and Leaf Stains

Some stains are not growth and will not respond to bleach at all.

**Tannin stains** — the brown leaf outlines and the general darkening under debris — come from water-soluble extractives in the wood and the leaves themselves. The fix is oxalic acid, sold as a wood brightener. A two-percent solution applied to a damp surface, left fifteen minutes, and rinsed will lift most tannin and rust staining. Heavier cases take a second application. Oxalic acid is an acid: gloves, eye protection, and no metal containers.

**Iron stains** — the rust streaks running down from nails, screws, or a steel brush somebody used years ago — respond to the same brightener. If you are seeing them, check the fasteners. Coated or stainless fasteners are one of the small line items on a quote that decides whether a fence looks clean in year twelve; our [material spec guide](/blog/2026-09-23-wood-fence-materials-spec-guide-michigan) covers which hardware holds up in this climate.

**Mildew staining that has soaked in** sometimes needs both: oxygen bleach first to kill and lift the growth, then brightener to even out the color.

What you should not do is sand a whole fence to fix staining. Sanding removes the soft earlywood and leaves a surface that weathers unevenly. Spot-sand a stubborn mark, brighten the rest.

## The Gray That Isn't Dirt

This is the part almost every cleaning guide gets wrong.

When unfinished cedar turns silver-gray, that is not dirt sitting on the surface. Sunlight breaks down lignin in the outer cell walls, and the gray you see is largely fungal growth feeding on that degraded layer. The wood has changed. Scrubbing harder does not reverse it.

What that means practically:

- **Light gray on sound wood** — a brightener will restore a surprising amount of color, and a penetrating oil finish will hold it.
- **Deep gray with a rough, hairy surface** — the outer fibers are gone. Brightening helps a little; sanding helps more; neither restores new wood.
- **Gray that has gone soft** — that is decay, not weathering. Clean it if you like, then plan the replacement.

Gray is also, honestly, a legitimate look. Plenty of homeowners in Rochester Hills and Bloomfield deliberately let cedar weather to silver and never finish it. Cedar is durable enough to survive that treatment, and it requires nothing from you except cleaning. What it does not survive is gray plus wet leaf mats plus a soft bottom board — the combination that turns a cosmetic issue into a structural one.

If you do want to hold the color, understand the schedule: a clear sealer typically needs reapplication every one to two years, while a pigmented or solid-color stain buys you three to six. There is no product that does it once. Our [staining and sealing guide](/blog/2026-07-22-wood-fence-staining-sealing-michigan-guide) covers product choice and application step by step.

## Doing This in November: What the Weather Allows

Here is the question we get every year around this date: is it too late to clean and seal before winter?

Cleaning, yes — you can still clean. Finishing, mostly no, and pretending otherwise is how people end up with a stain that never cures.

| Task | Mid-November? | The condition |
|---|---|---|
| Debris removal, rinse, oxygen bleach | Yes | Air above 40°F, and 24 hours above freezing afterward |
| Careful pressure washing | Yes | Same, plus no hard freeze in the two-day forecast |
| Oxalic acid brightening | Yes | Best above 45°F; longer dwell when cold |
| Board replacement | Yes | Any temperature; finish it in spring |
| Post replacement | Tight | Ground freezes from the top down; frozen clay is brutal work |
| Oil-based penetrating stain | Maybe | Some brands apply down to 35–40°F; most want 50°F |
| Water-based stain or sealer | No | Needs 55°F and drying time |

Most deck and fence stains want 50°F and above to bond and cure, and the field is genuinely split by product chemistry: some oil-based formulas are rated down to 35–40°F, while water-based products below 55°F commonly develop a dimpled, uneven film. Add Michigan's short days and heavy dew, and a November application can sit wet overnight and never cure properly.

Two numbers govern whether a finish will work at all: **surface temperature and moisture content.** Stain applied to wood above roughly 12 percent moisture content traps water instead of repelling it. After washing, that means forty-eight hours of dry weather minimum — longer in shade, longer still after a wet November week.

Our honest recommendation for the second half of November: **clean now, brighten if the wood needs it, and plan the finish for the first warm dry stretch in April.** Bare, clean, dry wood goes into winter better than wood sealed wet. Trapped moisture under a late-season finish causes more damage than a winter spent unfinished. If you want to seal now anyway, read the actual can — not a blog, including this one — and respect its temperature floor and recoat window.

## When Cleaning Isn't the Answer

Cleaning is cheap and worth doing. It is also, sometimes, a waste of a Saturday. Five signs that the problem is structural, not cosmetic:

| Sign | What it usually means | What it actually needs |
|---|---|---|
| Screwdriver sinks into the bottom of a picket | Rot at ground line | Board replacement |
| Post moves when you push it | Failed footing or rotted post base | Post replacement — this is the frost-heave story |
| Rails flex or sag between posts | Rail-end rot or loose fasteners | Rail and fastener repair |
| Pickets checked, split, or cupping | End grain taking water | Targeted replacement |
| More than a quarter of the boards are soft | Widespread decay | Repair-versus-replace conversation |

That last row matters. When a quarter or more of the fence is failing, patching boards becomes a subscription, and the honest math usually points at replacement — the decision framework is in our [replacement guide](/blog/2026-08-01-fence-replacement-guide-michigan). A post that moves when you push it is usually a frost-heave or ground-line rot problem, which we wrote up separately in [Leaning Fence Posts in Michigan](/blog/2026-09-12-leaning-fence-posts-michigan-repair). If you are unsure which side of the line you are on, push on the fence at three points and look at what your hand comes away with.

## The Fall Leaf Routine That Prevents Most of This

Ten minutes a week from mid-October through Thanksgiving prevents the majority of the damage this article describes.

- **Blow or rake the fence line after every heavy drop,** and especially before rain. Leaves that get wet first and sit second are the ones that stain.
- **Clear the board gaps** once the trees are bare. Debris in the gaps holds water against the face of every picket.
- **Pull mulch back two inches** from posts and bottom rails, and keep soil below the bottom of the boards.
- **Check your downspouts.** A splash block pointed at a fence post is a rot machine running every rainstorm.
- **Trim back vines and shrubs.** Plants trap moisture and pull on boards in wind.
- **Adjust sprinklers** so no head hits the fence. This one alone solves a lot of green.
- **Do not stack snow against the fence.** Plow and shovel it away from the line.

If you do only one of those, do the leaves. Everything else on this list is secondary to keeping wet organic material off the wood.

## Local Notes for Oakland County Homeowners

A few things that are true here and not necessarily true in a national guide.

**Clay holds water.** Much of Auburn Hills, Troy, and the older parts of Rochester Hills sits on heavy clay that drains slowly. Fence lines in low spots stay wet for days after a rain, and that shows up first as algae on the shaded face and rot at the bottom board. If water stands along your fence after a storm, cleaning is a temporary fix and drainage is the real one — our [winter prep guide](/blog/2026-08-26-winter-fence-prep-michigan-guide) covers the grading, downspout, and mulch details.

**Shade changes everything.** A wooded lot in Clarkston or a backyard under mature maples in Bloomfield will need cleaning twice as often as a sunny lot in Novi. That is not neglect — it is exposure.

**Some subdivisions care about appearance.** Several Oakland County subdivisions and HOAs have deed restrictions that require fences to be maintained in good condition and, in some cases, finished rather than left bare. Read your restrictions before you decide to let cedar weather gray; it is easier to know now than to repaint under pressure.

**Winter is coming at a known time.** Ground in this part of the state typically starts locking up in December. Anything that needs digging — a post, a footing, a leaning section — is on a clock. Cleaning and board swaps are not. If you are planning work for spring, late fall is the right time to get on a schedule, because April fills up first.

**Salt and plows.** Corner lots and through-streets get a seasonal dose of road salt and the occasional plow-scarred bottom board. Rinse the lower two feet in spring, and check that bottom rail before you assume a stain failure is a product problem.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I get green algae off a wood fence?

Oxygen bleach (sodium percarbonate), applied bottom to top to already-wet wood, left to dwell three to five minutes, scrubbed with a soft nylon brush, and rinsed with a garden hose or very low pressure. Chlorine bleach also kills algae but attacks lignin and fuzzes the wood, so it is the wrong tool for cedar.

### Can I pressure wash a cedar fence?

Yes, carefully. Stay in the 500–800 PSI range on cedar, use a 40-degree fan tip, hold the wand 12–18 inches from the boards, and work with the grain. If the surface starts to look fuzzy, stop — you are removing wood fiber, not dirt. Never use a 0-degree tip on a wood fence.

### What is the gray on my old wood fence — is it dirt?

No. Gray on unfinished wood is UV-degraded lignin with fungal growth feeding on it. Cleaning removes the growth, and an oxalic acid brightener restores some color, but heavy gray with a rough, hairy surface means the outer fibers are gone and cannot be washed back.

### How do I remove leaf stains from a wood fence?

Leaf stains are tannin stains. Soap will not lift them. Apply an oxalic acid wood brightener to a damp surface, let it sit about fifteen minutes, and rinse. Heavier stains take a second application. Clear the leaves first, or the stain will simply come back with the next rain.

### Is it too late to clean and seal my fence in November in Michigan?

Cleaning is fine as long as air temperatures stay above about 40°F and no hard freeze is coming. Sealing is usually not: most stains need 50°F or warmer, some oil-based products go down to 35–40°F, and wood must be at 12 percent moisture content or below. Cleaning now and finishing in April is the better play.

### Will cleaning make a 15-year-old fence look new again?

It will look considerably better — growth gone, tannins lifted, some color restored. It will not look new. Weathering changes the wood itself, and once the outer fibers are worn away no cleaner brings them back. Cleaning buys appearance and time; it does not reverse age.

### How much does it cost to have a fence cleaned in Oakland County?

Professional cleaning typically runs about $1.50–$3.50 per linear foot for a single side, with most contractors carrying a minimum charge. DIY is usually $40–$90 in materials for a 150-foot run plus a pressure washer rental. Brightening and staining are priced separately.

### Should I clean both sides of the fence?

Usually yes, if you have access. Mildew grows through board gaps, the shaded side ages the sunny side, and a fence cleaned on one face only tends to look worse, not better. If access requires a neighbor's permission, ask before you start — and cover their plantings if you are spraying.
