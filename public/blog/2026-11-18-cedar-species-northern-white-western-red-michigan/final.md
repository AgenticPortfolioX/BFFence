# Northern White Cedar vs. Western Red Cedar vs. Eastern Red Cedar: Which Cedar Actually Belongs on Your Michigan Fence

Stand in the fence aisle of any lumber yard in Oakland County and you will find a stack of boards with the word CEDAR stamped on the end tag. Depending on the yard, that word can mean three different trees, from two different plant genera, grown in three different parts of the continent. It can also mean none of them.

That is not a small distinction. Cedar is the most specified fence material in this county and the least specified material on a quote. "Six-foot cedar privacy fence" is a sentence that sounds like a specification and contains almost no information. Two contractors can hand you that sentence, both be telling the truth, and install fences that behave differently for twenty years.

This is the conversation we have with homeowners across Auburn Hills, Rochester Hills, Troy, and Clarkston every week, usually at a kitchen table with two competing bids in front of us. What follows is the version we give when nobody is in a hurry: what the three cedars actually are, what the numbers say, where the durability really lives inside a board, and which cedar belongs on which part of your fence.

## The Quick Answer

Western red cedar is the premium board species: light, stable, long and clear, and the right choice for pickets, rails, and anything visible. Northern white cedar is Michigan's own tree: softer, knottier, cheaper, and excellent for posts, rails, and rustic fencing where strength is not the driver. Eastern red cedar is a juniper, not a cedar at all in the everyday sense: dense, hard, and the best of the three for a post in the ground.

If a quote says "cedar" and nothing else, you do not know which of those three you are buying. That is the whole problem in one sentence.

## "Cedar" Is a Common Name, Not a Specification

The trees sold as cedar in North American lumber yards belong to the cypress family. Western red cedar and northern white cedar are both *Thuja* — true botanical cousins, one from the Pacific coast and one from the Great Lakes. Eastern red cedar is *Juniperus virginiana*, a juniper that shares a family with the other two but not a genus.

None of the three is a true cedar in the botanical sense, which is *Cedrus* — the Himalayan and Atlas cedars you will find in arboretums and nowhere in a fence quote. The name is a trade word that got attached to aromatic, rot-resistant softwoods and stayed there.

Why does the naming matter to a homeowner writing a check? Because a common name is not a specification, and anything that is not a specification can be substituted. A bid that says "cedar" can be filled with whatever cedar is cheapest that month: white cedar from a northern Michigan mill, western red cedar seconds from a broker, or a load of something cedar-colored. All three will be described, accurately, as cedar on the invoice.

The fix is simple and it costs nothing: make the quote name the species, the grade, the actual thickness, and the component. We will get to exactly how to ask for that.

## Where Each Cedar Actually Comes From

### Western Red Cedar: Thuja plicata

Western red cedar grows from northern California to southeast Alaska, and the trees are enormous — the species reaches 165 to 200 feet with trunks 7 to 13 feet across. That size is the reason it dominates fence lumber: a big tree yields long, wide, clear boards with few knots, and a mill can cut a 16-foot rail without working around a branch.

The wood is light (about 23 pounds per cubic foot at 12 percent moisture), works easily, and holds its shape well. It is also soft — 350 pounds on the Janka scale — so it dents, scratches, and marks. Iron fasteners stain it, especially when the wood is wet, which is why stainless or coated fasteners are standard on a cedar fence that is meant to stay good-looking.

### Northern White Cedar: Thuja occidentalis

Northern white cedar — eastern white cedar, arborvitae, *Thuja occidentalis* — is one of only two cedars native to Michigan. It grows in swamps and wet lowlands from the Great Lakes to the Atlantic, and it is a much smaller tree than its western cousin: 50 to 65 feet tall, with trunks 16 to 24 inches across.

Small trunks mean short, narrow, knotty boards. That single fact explains everything about how white cedar is sold and used. It does not produce 16-foot clear rails. It produces fence posts, rails, shingles, and rustic boards with character, and it has been used for exactly those things in Michigan for a very long time.

Northern white cedar is lighter than western red (about 22 pounds per cubic foot), softer (320 on the Janka scale), and weaker in bending — its modulus of rupture is 6,500 psi against western red's 7,500, and it is roughly 28 percent less stiff. It also has poor screw-holding ability, which is a real design constraint on gates and on any joint that relies on fasteners rather than blocking.

Its decay resistance is not the problem. The ratings put it in the same class as western red, and it carries an added advantage: it is listed as resistant to termites and powderpost beetles, where western red cedar's insect resistance is rated as mixed.

One more local note worth knowing. Michigan's wild white cedar resource has shrunk dramatically since settlement — cleared for agriculture and development, browsed hard by white-tailed deer, and often converted to other forest types after harvest. The cedar you buy today from a northern Michigan mill is not the old-growth cedar that built the state's barns. That matters, and it is the subject of the heartwood section below.

### Eastern Red Cedar: Juniperus virginiana

Eastern red cedar is the outlier, and the numbers make the case immediately. Janka hardness: 900 pounds, roughly 2.6 times western red cedar. Dried weight: 33 pounds per cubic foot, about 43 percent heavier. Crushing strength 6,020 psi against 4,560 for western red. Modulus of rupture 8,800 psi.

It is also rated excellent against both decay and insects, and it is used for fence posts in direct soil contact with no treatment at all — which is the sentence that matters most here. Eastern red cedar is a post species. It is dense, resinous, aromatic, and it takes ground contact better than any of the three.

What it is not is a picket species. The trees are small and knotty in the sizes available, the wood is hard on cutting tools (high silica content dulls blades quickly), and its shrinkage pattern is less forgiving than either *Thuja* — 3.1 percent radial and 4.7 percent tangential against 2.2 and 4.9 for white cedar, with higher total volumetric movement. Boards check and move more.

## The Numbers, Translated Into Fence Behavior

Here is the head-to-head, using values from The Wood Database, which is the standard reference in the trade. All figures are at 12 percent moisture content.

| Property | Western red cedar | Northern white cedar | Eastern red cedar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Janka hardness | 350 lbf | 320 lbf | 900 lbf |
| Dried weight | 23.0 lb/ft³ | 22.0 lb/ft³ | 33 lb/ft³ |
| Modulus of rupture | 7,500 psi | 6,500 psi | 8,800 psi |
| Modulus of elasticity | 1,110,000 psi | 800,000 psi | 880,000 psi |
| Crushing strength | 4,560 psi | 3,960 psi | 6,020 psi |
| Shrinkage (radial / tangential) | 2.4% / 5.0% | 2.2% / 4.9% | 3.1% / 4.7% |
| Decay resistance | Durable to very durable | Durable to very durable | Excellent |
| Insect resistance | Mixed | Termites, powderpost beetles | Excellent |
| Typical fence role | Pickets, rails, boards | Posts, rails, rustic fence | Posts in ground contact |

Read the table like a contractor and not like a woodworker, because the numbers mean different things at a fence than they do at a workbench.

Janka hardness is dent resistance. It tells you how a board handles a lawn mower, a ladder, a dog's shoulder, and a baseball. It tells you nothing about how long the board lasts in the ground. A 900-pound eastern red cedar picket can rot exactly as fast as a 350-pound western red picket if both are sapwood sitting in wet leaf litter.

Modulus of rupture and modulus of elasticity are bending strength and stiffness. Those numbers decide whether a 2x4 rail sags across an 8-foot span and whether a gate stays square. Western red cedar is the stiffest of the three and the most reliable rail stock; white cedar is noticeably softer and should not be asked to span the same distance without support.

Shrinkage is what the fence looks like in August. Cedar milled and installed wet will shrink across its width, and a fence built tight in May will show gaps by late summer. Both *Thuja* species are unusually stable for softwoods, which is part of why they dominate fence pickets; eastern red cedar moves more and checks more.

Density is fastener performance and weight. Denser wood holds a screw better. This is the quiet reason white cedar needs pre-drilling at board ends and why a gate frame built from it will loosen faster than one built from western red or treated pine.

One caution on all of these numbers: they are species averages, and individual boards vary by ten percent or more in either direction. At the fence, growth ring width and heartwood content will move a board's real-world life expectancy further than the species label on the tag.

## Heartwood, Sapwood, and the Part of the Board Nobody Checks

Everything above is a species-level conversation. The board-level conversation is more important, and it almost never appears on a quote.

A cedar tree has two kinds of wood. Heartwood is the dark core: dead, structurally stable, and loaded with extractives the tree manufactured while it was alive. In the *Thuja* species those extractives include compounds called thujaplicins, which are antifungal and insect-repellent. That chemistry, not some inherent property of "cedar," is what resists rot.

Sapwood is the pale ring just under the bark: living wood, full of starch and sugar, and it has almost none of those extractives. Sapwood is not durable. It is food.

Now look at a fence picket. On a typical fence-grade cedar board, a substantial share of the face can be sapwood — often the entire edge, sometimes half the board. A picket that is 40 percent sapwood has 40 percent of its width doing nothing to resist rot, and the bottom six inches of that picket is where it counts, because that is where leaf litter, splash, and soil keep it wet.

### Second growth is not old growth

Old-growth western red cedar came from trees centuries old, with tight growth rings, high density, and a large heartwood core. That wood is essentially gone from the commercial supply, and the cedar sold today is second growth: faster grown, wider-ringed, lighter, and with a higher sapwood percentage.

This is the honest version of a trade secret. The species has not changed; the average board has. A 1965 cedar fence and a 2026 cedar fence can carry the same name and age differently, and the difference is measured in rings per inch and heartwood share, not in the word on the invoice.

### How to check before it gets nailed up

You can inspect a delivery in about ten minutes, and it is the highest-value ten minutes in the whole project.

Look at the end grain. Heartwood is darker — reddish brown in western red, light reddish brown in white cedar, violet-brown in eastern red — and sapwood is nearly white or pale yellow. Estimate the proportion. On a load of pickets, pull ten boards at random and look at both ends.

Look at ring width. Tight rings mean slow growth and denser wood. Wide rings — a quarter inch or more — mean a fast-grown board that will be lighter, softer, and more sapwood-heavy.

Check the moisture. Kiln-dried cedar installs tighter and shrinks less. Green or wet stock will open gaps as it dries, and in a Michigan November it will not dry quickly.

Then check the tag and the paperwork. Species, grade, actual thickness, and drying method should all be written down. If they are not, the delivery is a surprise waiting to happen.

## Is Northern White Cedar as Durable as Western Red Cedar?

This is the question we get most, usually from someone who has been told that red cedar is the premium wood and white cedar is the budget compromise.

On decay resistance, the ratings do not support the premium story. Both species are classified durable to very durable in heartwood, and wood scientists at the Forest Products Laboratory have treated the heartwood of red and white cedar as roughly equivalent for decades. Northern white cedar also carries the better insect rating of the two.

So what actually differs? Four things.

Strength and stiffness: western red cedar is meaningfully stronger in bending and about 28 percent stiffer. On rails, gates, and long spans, that matters.

Fastener holding: white cedar is soft and weak, and its screw-holding ability is rated poor. It needs pre-drilling near ends and mechanical reinforcement at joints.

Board quality and supply: western red cedar comes in longer, clearer, more uniform boards. White cedar arrives short, narrow, and knotty, with more culling required on site. Our crews sort white cedar deliveries and set aside the checked and knot-heavy pieces; with western red that sorting takes minutes.

Weathering appearance: white cedar starts pale and straw-colored and goes silver-gray. Western red starts reddish-brown and, unfinished in Michigan weather, darkens toward near-black before it grays. Neither is better; they are different looks, and the one you want should drive the decision as much as the rating does.

**The verdict: white cedar is a genuinely good fence wood, and the claim that it is a lesser species is marketing rather than wood science.** Where it loses is strength and fastener holding, not rot. Specify it where the board is not carrying load — pickets, short rails, rustic and ranch-style fencing — and expect to cull more of the delivery.

## Why Eastern Red Cedar Is a Post Species, Not a Picket Species

If the post is the part of the fence that fails first — and in Oakland County it usually is — then eastern red cedar deserves a serious look. It is the only one of the three that is routinely used in direct soil contact without treatment, and its decay and insect ratings are the best of the group.

A 4x4 eastern red cedar post in clay soil will outlast a cedar picket fence around it. That is not a controversial claim in the trade; it is why the species has been used for farm fence posts for a century.

The catch is everything else about it. It is hard and dense, so it is heavier to handle and harder to fasten. It has high silica content, which dulls saw blades and drill bits quickly — a crew cutting eastern red cedar all day will notice. Its movement is greater, so long boards check and cup more than *Thuja*. And the supply is not there: you cannot walk into a suburban yard and buy a unit of eastern red cedar pickets, because the trees do not produce the clear, long stock a picket fence wants.

Where it wins: posts, especially in the wet spots of a yard where a treated post's cut end is a liability. Where it does not: picket runs, long rails, and anything where appearance consistency across a hundred boards matters.

## The Impostors: Cedar-Tone and Other Things Sold Under the Word

### Cedar-Tone is pine

Cedar-Tone is pressure-treated southern yellow pine with iron-oxide pigment infused into the wood under pressure, along with the micronized copper azole (MCA) preservative that does the actual rot protection. The color runs through the piece, so a fresh cut end matches the face. Manufacturers describe it exactly that way: a cedar-like color on treated pine, and the color does not fade the way natural cedar does.

It is not cedar. It contains no cedar. It has no cedar extractives, no cedar aroma worth the name, and no cedar grain pattern.

Is that bad? Not automatically. Treated southern yellow pine is a legitimate fence material: it is ground-contact rated when labeled as such, it holds fasteners better than any cedar, it is dimensionally more consistent than a knotty white cedar board, and it costs less. For a homeowner who wants a wood fence at the lowest responsible price and intends to stain it, cedar-tone pine is an honest answer. For the species-by-species version of that comparison, see [cedar versus pressure-treated pine](/blog/2026-06-10-cedar-vs-pressure-treated-pine-michigan-fence).

What is not honest is a bid that says "cedar fence" and delivers cedar-tone. That happens, and it is hard to catch after the fact if you never looked at the tag.

### The other word-borrowers

Alaskan yellow cedar is not a *Thuja* at all — it is *Callitropsis nootkatensis*, another cypress-family wood, harder and more decay-resistant than western red cedar, and considerably more expensive. It is a real premium product, rarely used for residential fencing because of price.

The rest is labeling: cedar-colored stains on pine, box-store fence packages described as "cedar" when only the pickets are cedar and the posts are treated, cypress sold as a cedar alternative. Most of it is not fraud. All of it is a reason to read the tag.

### How to tell in ten seconds

The end tag will usually say it: SYP, southern yellow pine, or "cedar tone." Cut a piece and look — cedar-tone is uniform in color through the cross-section because the pigment is infused, while real cedar shows a dark heartwood core and a pale sapwood ring. Cedar-tone is also heavier than western red cedar and smells like treatment, not like a cedar chest.

## Grades, Thickness, and What "Fence Grade" Actually Buys You

Even after you have picked a species, the grade decides what you get.

Fence-grade cedar is sold in tiers — typically a #1 or premium picket, a #2, and an economy or rustic grade — and the difference is knots, checks, wane, and how much of the board is usable. A #1 picket is sound and tight-knotted with limited defect. An economy picket can carry large knots, splits, and mill glaze. Both are cedar; only one is a good fence board.

Thickness is where buyers get surprised. A nominal 1x6 fence picket is not an inch thick. Fence-grade boards are commonly milled at about 5/8 inch actual, while a select or "5/4" board runs closer to a full inch. That difference is strength, fastener holding, and how the fence looks in year eight. It is also invisible on a quote that says "1x6 cedar pickets."

Grain orientation matters as much as grade, and it is the least discussed. Flat-sawn boards — the ones where the growth rings run diagonally across the face — cup as they take on and lose moisture. Vertical-grain or quarter-sawn boards stay flat. On a south- or west-facing run that bakes in August sun, flat-sawn pickets cup toward the sun and pull fasteners. You cannot fix that with a better stain.

Drying method matters too. Kiln-dried cedar is more stable at installation and shrinks less in the first season. Green or air-dried stock can be fine, but it needs to be installed with shrinkage in mind, and in November it will not dry down before the snow.

What a good specification looks like: species, grade, actual thickness, grain orientation for visible runs, drying method, fastener material, and the post specification. Six lines. Any contractor who resists writing them is telling you something. ## Matching the Cedar to the Part of the Fence

This is where the whole discussion becomes useful, because a fence is not one material. It is a system of components with different jobs, and the right cedar changes from one to the next. For the wider question of wood against vinyl, aluminum, and composite, the [material comparison guide](/blog/2026-08-15-fence-material-comparison-guide-oakland-county) covers that ground.

| Component | First choice | Acceptable | Avoid | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Posts in soil | Ground-contact treated pine; eastern red cedar | Cedar on a pier or bracket above grade | Untreated cedar directly in wet clay | The post is the failure point; ground contact is brutal in Oakland County clay |
| Rails | Western red cedar 2x4 | Treated pine; white cedar on short spans | White cedar on long spans | Stiffness controls sag; white cedar is 28% less stiff |
| Pickets | Western red cedar #1 or #2 | Northern white cedar, culled | Eastern red cedar, economy grades | Appearance consistency, workability, availability |
| Caps and trim | Western red cedar | White cedar, clear | Knotty economy stock | Visible, handled, and the first place checking shows |
| Gate frames | Treated pine or western red cedar with a diagonal brace | White cedar with steel reinforcement | White cedar alone on a wide gate | Screw holding and stiffness; gates take abuse |
| Rustic or ranch fence | Northern white cedar round rail | Eastern red cedar | Western red cedar (waste of money) | Local supply and cost fit the job |

The pattern is straightforward once you see it. Posts want density and ground-contact durability. Rails want stiffness. Pickets want stability and appearance. The species that wins on one component is not the species that wins on the next, and a contractor who uses one wood for everything is either selling you a premium product where you do not need it or a budget product where you cannot afford it.

The reverse is also true. A quote that comes in thousands below the others on a "cedar" fence is almost always one of three things: a lower grade, a thinner board, or a different species than you think you are buying. Sometimes it is all three at once.

## Six Questions That Turn "Cedar" on a Quote Into a Specification

Ask these before you sign anything. A straight answer takes five minutes and tells you more than the rest of the bid combined.

1. **Which species, on which component?** Posts, rails, and pickets can be three different woods, and often should be. "Cedar" alone is not an answer.
2. **What grade, and what sapwood percentage will you accept?** If the answer is "we use whatever the yard has," you have your answer.
3. **What is the actual thickness?** Not nominal — actual. Five-eighths and three-quarters are different fences.
4. **Kiln-dried or green?** This determines how the fence looks in August, not how it looks on installation day.
5. **What fasteners?** Stainless or coated for cedar. Bare steel will streak it, especially where the wood stays wet.
6. **What happens at the posts?** Depth, footing, species, and whether the post is set below Michigan's frost line — 42 inches in this part of the state. This is where fences fail and where bids quietly differ.

If a contractor answers all six in writing, you are dealing with someone who builds fences on purpose. If the answers are vague, the price is probably the least of your problems.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is northern white cedar as good as western red cedar for a fence?

On rot resistance, yes. Both heartwoods are rated durable to very durable, and northern white cedar carries the better insect rating. Western red cedar is stronger, stiffer, and available in clearer, longer boards, so it is the better choice for rails and gates. For pickets and short spans, white cedar performs well at a lower cost.

### Does cedar sapwood rot?

Yes. Sapwood has almost none of the extractives that protect heartwood, so it decays like any untreated softwood, especially at the bottom of a picket where soil and leaf litter hold moisture. Look at the end grain before installation: dark core, pale ring. The more pale ring, the shorter that board's life in a wet location.

### Is cedar-tone the same as cedar?

No. Cedar-tone is pressure-treated southern yellow pine with iron-oxide pigment infused into the wood. The preservative — micronized copper azole — provides the rot resistance; the pigment provides the color. It is a legitimate budget fence material and holds fasteners better than cedar, but it is pine, and it should never be priced or described as cedar.

### What is the hardest cedar for fence posts?

Eastern red cedar, by a wide margin. It rates 900 pounds on the Janka scale against 350 for western red and 320 for northern white cedar, and it is about 43 percent heavier. It is the one cedar routinely used in direct soil contact without treatment, which makes it a strong post material and a poor picket material.

### Which cedar is best for fence pickets?

Western red cedar is the standard for good reason: it is stable, works cleanly, comes in consistent lengths, and takes a finish predictably. Northern white cedar is a solid, less expensive alternative if you accept more knots and more culling. Eastern red cedar is a poor picket choice because of supply, hardness, and movement.

### How long does a cedar fence last in Michigan?

A well-built cedar fence with heartwood-heavy boards, posts set below the frost line, and reasonable maintenance typically gives 15 to 25 years in this climate; we regularly see western red cedar fences past 20 with only posts replaced. Sapwood-heavy boards in wet locations, or shallow footings, will cut that life substantially regardless of species.

### Should cedar posts be set in concrete?

For a privacy fence in Oakland County clay, posts should be set below the 42-inch frost line, and a properly sized concrete footing is the most reliable way to hold them there through freeze-thaw. The footing is not the whole story: the post material, the cut end, and drainage around the base determine how long the post survives. Ground-contact rated material or eastern red cedar is the safer choice in soil.

### Do I need stainless steel fasteners for a cedar fence?

For western red cedar, yes — or a coated alternative rated for exterior use. Iron-based fasteners react with cedar extractives and leave black streaks, particularly when the wood is wet, and the staining is permanent. Stainless costs more up front and is the difference between a fence that ages and a fence that looks damaged.
