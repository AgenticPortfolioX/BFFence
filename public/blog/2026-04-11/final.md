## The Ground Is Ready â And So Is Your Fence

Michigan soil is no joke. We live on clay-heavy ground that freezes solid in winter and turns into a swamp in early spring. But there's a sweet spot â usually mid-April through May â when the frost line drops below the post depth and the ground firms up enough for clean, stable digging.

Here's what that means in practical terms:

**Frost heave is the enemy.** Every winter, Michigan experiences between 60 and 100 freeze-thaw cycles. Water in the soil freezes, expands, and pushes against everything in its path â including your fence posts. If a post isn't set below the frost line (42 inches in Oakland County), it will move. Maybe not this year, maybe not next year, but eventually you'll come out one morning and find a section of fence leaning like it had a rough night.

In spring, the ground is thawed but still firm. That's the ideal condition for digging post holes to the correct depth and setting them in concrete. The soil compacts properly around the footing, creating a stable base that resists frost heave for years.

**Fall is decent** â the ground is workable and schedules lighten up. But fall installation leaves your new fence exposed to a full Michigan winter with no time to cure and settle. That's asking for trouble.

Spring gives you the best of both worlds: perfect working conditions and months of warm weather for your fence to settle in before it faces its first real test.

## Choosing the Right Wood for Michigan's Climate

Not all wood is created equal, and what works in Arizona or California won't necessarily survive a Rochester winter. Michigan throws four seasons of punishment at your fence â humid summers, wet springs, windy falls, and winters with 60 to 100 freeze-thaw cycles. You need wood that can handle all of it.

### Cedar: The Gold Standard

If we had to recommend one wood for Michigan fences, it's Western Red Cedar. Here's why:

- **Natural rot resistance.** Cedar contains thujaplicin, a natural compound that kills fungi and bacteria. No chemicals needed.
- **Insect repellent.** The same oils that prevent rot also repel termites, carpenter ants, and other wood-boring insects.
- **Dimensional stability.** Cedar shrinks and swells less than other woods when moisture levels change. That means fewer warped boards and less gapping between pickets.
- **Looks great.** Cedar weathers to a handsome silver-gray if left untreated, or holds stain beautifully if you want to maintain the original color.

The trade-off is cost. At $30â$45 per linear foot installed, cedar costs more upfront than pine. But when you factor in lower maintenance costs and a 20â30 year lifespan, most homeowners come out ahead over the long run.

### Pressure-Treated Pine: The Budget Contender

Pine gets a bad reputation sometimes, but it's a solid choice if you're watching your budget. Modern pressure treatment infuses the wood with preservatives that resist rot and insects â similar protection to cedar's natural oils, just achieved differently.

The key with pine is **maintenance**. You need to seal it within the first year (ideally within 3â6 months) and reseal every 2â3 years. Skip the maintenance and you'll see warping, cracking, and early deterioration â especially in Michigan's humidity.

At $20â$35 per linear foot, pine saves you 30â40% over cedar on materials. For many families, that's the difference between getting a fence now and waiting another year.

### Redwood: Premium but Worth It (If You Can Find It)

Redwood is the Cadillac of fencing woods. It's more durable than cedar, naturally more resistant to decay, and has a rich, warm color that's unmatched. The problem? Availability in Michigan is limited, and costs run $40â$65 per linear foot. If budget isn't a concern and you can source it, redwood will outlast almost anything else.

## 5 Signs You Need a New Fence This Spring

Not sure if you need a full replacement or just some repairs? Here's what to look for:

### 1. Leaning Posts

If one or more fence sections are tilting, it's usually a sign that the posts have been compromised by frost heave. A leaning fence doesn't fix itself â it gets worse with every freeze-thaw cycle. If more than two or three posts are affected, replacement is usually more cost-effective than repair.

### 2. Rot at the Base

Get down and look at the bottom of your fence posts and boards. If the wood is soft, spongy, or crumbling, rot has set in. This is especially common with older pine fences that weren't sealed properly. Rot weakens the structural integrity and will spread over time.

### 3. Widespread Warping and Splitting

A few warped boards are normal â Michigan's humidity swings are brutal. But if half your fence looks like a funhouse mirror, the wood has reached the end of its useful life. Wider gaps between boards also mean less privacy and more exposure to wind.

### 4. Loose or Missing Boards After Winter

Walk your fence line this spring. If you're picking up boards off the ground or noticing sections that wobble when you push on them, winter took its toll. Individual boards can be replaced, but if the damage is widespread, the underlying structure is probably failing too.

### 5. Your Fence Is 15+ Years Old

Even well-maintained fences have a lifespan. Pressure-treated pine lasts 15â20 years. Cedar lasts 20â30. If your fence is approaching or past those marks, a spring replacement is a smart move â especially if you're planning to sell your home in the next few years. A new fence adds curb appeal and can return 50â70% of its cost at resale.

## Protecting Your Investment: Maintenance Tips for New Fence Owners

Once your new fence is up, a little maintenance goes a long way. Here's our simple care guide:

### Year 1: Let It Settle

- **Don't stain immediately** â if you chose cedar, let it weather for 3â6 months before staining. This allows the wood's pores to open and accept the stain more evenly.
- **Seal pressure-treated pine** within the first 6 months to lock in the preservatives.
- **Inspect after the first hard freeze** â check for any movement in the posts or settling in the concrete footings.

### Every Spring: Annual Checkup

- **Walk the fence line** and look for loose boards, popped nails, or sagging gates.
- **Clean the fence** with a gentle pressure wash (under 1500 PSI) to remove dirt and mildew.
- **Trim vegetation** â bushes and vines growing against the fence trap moisture and accelerate rot.

### Every 2â3 Years: Refinish

- **Restain or reseal** the wood to maintain UV protection and moisture resistance.
- **Tighten gate hardware** â hinges and latches take a beating from wind and daily use.
- **Check post bases** for any signs of rot or erosion.

This routine takes a Saturday morning every spring and keeps your fence looking sharp and standing strong for decades.
