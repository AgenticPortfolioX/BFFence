# Commercial Fence Installation in Oakland County: The Business Owner's Guide to Perimeter Fencing, Screening, and Property Demarcation
*Published: September 26, 2026 | By BF Fence*
---
Most commercial fence projects don't start with a business owner deciding they want a fence. They start with something else.

A letter from the city about open storage visible from the road. A neighbor complaint about a dumpster. A tenant asking why the back lot isn't screened. A theft over a weekend. An insurance renewal with questions attached. A lease requiring the property manager to maintain "an opaque screen" around the waste enclosure. A site plan review comment with a deadline.

That's the moment a business owner in Auburn Hills, Rochester Hills, Troy, Novi, or anywhere else in Oakland County picks up the phone — and where the details start to matter, because a commercial fence isn't a residential fence with more boards. It has a different approval path, a different duty cycle, and a different set of people who can tear it back out.

This guide covers the jobs businesses actually order, what local ordinance really requires, what the site plan reviewer looks at, where wood wins and where it doesn't, and how the Michigan calendar shapes your schedule.

## What Counts as a Commercial Fence Project (And Why It's Not Just a Bigger Backyard Fence)

A commercial fence project is any fence serving a non-residential use — retail, industrial, office, warehouse, restaurant, multi-family, church, daycare, municipal, or mixed-use. In zoning terms, that's the line between a residential district and everything else. In practical terms, it's the difference between three realities.

**Approval is plan-based, not neighbor-based.** Residential fence permits are usually a simple administrative review. Commercial fences are frequently reviewed as part of, or in conformance with, an approved site plan — which means the municipality may care about parking, drainage, landscape buffers, fire access, and adjacent residential districts, not just height.

**The fence has a duty cycle.** A residential privacy fence opens a gate a few times a week. A commercial fence may see a roll-off truck backed into the same gate twice a week for fifteen years. Post size, footings, frame design, and gate hardware have to be chosen for that life.

**There are more decision-makers.** The owner may be a landlord, the tenant the one generating the storage, the caller a property manager, the budget a corporate facilities cycle. Sorting out who approves the work and who signs is step one — and usually the slowest one.

## The Eight Commercial Fence Jobs Oakland County Businesses Actually Order

Nearly every commercial inquiry we get in Oakland County falls into one of eight categories. Each has its own logic for height, material, gates, and screening.

### 1. Perimeter Fencing and Property Demarcation

A perimeter fence defines where a property begins and ends. For industrial and office sites, it's the difference between a yard that looks managed and one that looks open to the world. Perimeter runs are typically straight-line work — often the most economical part of a commercial project per linear foot — and are usually combined with two or three access gates rather than many.

Demarcation isn't only aesthetic. A visible, maintained boundary is the first layer of a security posture: it tells a would-be intruder the property is defined and observed, and it frames the area where lighting and cameras can actually be effective.

### 2. Dumpster and Waste Enclosures

This is the most common commercial fence job in Oakland County, and the most regulated. Most Michigan municipalities require commercial dumpsters to be screened from public view — in practice, an opaque enclosure on three sides with a gate on the fourth.

The enclosure is a package, not just panels. It needs a pad under the container, screening at or above container height (commonly six to eight feet), bollards inside to stop the truck and container from pushing through the back wall, and a gate clear opening wide enough for the hauler to service the container without a ten-minute maneuver. Most front-load and rear-load haulers want somewhere in the range of twelve to fifteen feet. When an enclosure is ordered without hauler input, the gate ends up too narrow and the hauler invents a service method that damages the fence.

### 3. Open-Storage Screening

If a business stores equipment, vehicles, materials, pallets, or containers outdoors, most Oakland County communities require that storage to be hidden from public view, the street, and adjoining properties. This is where commercial fencing stops being optional and becomes a compliance item with a citation attached. Auburn Hills, for example, requires screening no less than the height of the items stored — meaning a fence designed to hide a dumpster won't hide a forklift.

### 4. Mechanical, Utility, and Generator Screening

HVAC units, generators, transformers, meters, and mechanical pads all need to exist somewhere, and most businesses prefer that "somewhere" to be visible only to a maintenance crew. Short screening runs and enclosure walls solve this while keeping service access and code-required clearances intact. Small by footage, disproportionate by impact on how a property reads from the parking lot.

### 5. Loading Dock and Service Yard Separation

A dock area is where pedestrians and vehicles share space with bad sight lines. Fencing is often the cheapest way to separate them — a defined walk path, a controlled entry, a yard securable at night when the site is empty. On these projects, gate location matters more than material: put a fence in the wrong place on a dock and you've created a daily operational problem.

### 6. Parking Lot and Customer-Area Screening

Commercial sites use fencing as a visual buffer — a screen between the customer entrance and the service side of the building, a privacy run beside a patio or outdoor seating area, a defined edge between parking and a residential neighbor. This is the category where the fence does double duty: operational screening plus property relations.

### 7. Multi-Family, Condo, and HOA Community Fencing

Apartment communities, condominium associations, and subdivisions with common area manage a mix of needs: amenity screening, trash enclosures, pool and playground enclosure, and boundary fencing between the community and adjacent commercial or roadway uses. These projects usually involve a board, a property manager, a budget cycle, and a preference for one consistent look across multiple enclosures. The right approach is often a specified standard built in phases.

### 8. Outdoor Dining, Daycare, and Church Play Areas

Enclosed outdoor areas concentrate children, food service, and equipment in controlled space. For restaurants, a patio fence is a licensing and ambience issue. For daycares, churches, and schools, an enclosure is a supervision and containment issue — sightlines from the building, self-closing gate hardware, and finger-safe spacing all matter. These jobs reward a site walk with the person who actually supervises the space.

## Three Oakland County Municipalities, Three Real Requirements

Oakland County doesn't administer local zoning. Each city, village, and township writes and enforces its own fence ordinance, and the differences between neighbors are real. Here's how three of the busiest commercial corridors in Oakland County read.

### Auburn Hills — Zoning Ordinance No. 372, Article XVIII

Auburn Hills regulates screening aggressively, and the language is specific: open storage of industrial or commercial equipment, vehicles, and materials — expressly including wastes — must be screened from public view, from a public street, and from adjoining properties by an obscuring wall or obscuring fence no less than the height of the items stored.

If that storage sits adjacent to a residential zone or mobile home park — front, side, or rear relationship, or even across a right-of-way — the required screen rises to at least six feet, or the height of the stored materials, whichever is higher. The ordinance also includes a maintenance enforcement provision: a required wall or fence that falls into disrepair can trigger escrow of funds for repair, which is worth understanding before installing a fence you don't plan to maintain. Where required screening exceeds six feet, or site conditions are unusual, a landscaped greenbelt may substitute after Planning Commission review and City Council approval. Required walls and fences are generally located on the lot line, except where underground utilities interfere or a residential front yard setback applies.

### Rochester Hills — Zoning Ordinance § 138-10.107.D

Rochester Hills has among the most explicit non-residential fence standards in the area. Erecting, constructing, or altering any fence, wall, or protective barrier on non-residential property requires approval from the Building Official — confirming it conforms to both the zoning district and the fence chapter itself — before work begins.

The height cap for non-residential fences is eight feet, with one carve-out: security fencing of anodized aluminum or similar approved ornamental material may be permitted up to ten feet at the Planning Commission's discretion. Inward-facing barbed wire cradles are allowed only at the joint discretion of the Planning Commission and City Council.

The detail that surprises most business owners: chain link is generally prohibited in Rochester Hills unless the Planning and Economic Development Department determines the fence will not be visible from an abutting roadway. On a visible commercial frontage, that effectively removes the cheapest material option — and it's the strongest local argument for a wood or ornamental screen.

### Troy — City Code Chapter 83

Troy requires a Building Department permit before any fence is constructed, with an application showing the proposal meets ordinance and zoning requirements. Two provisions deserve attention early. First, permit issuance can be blocked until final lot grading is approved, so a site that hasn't been finalized can't simply add fence. Second — and more commonly encountered on commercial property — a fence proposed inside an easement requires sign-off from the Director of Public Works.

Troy also draws material lines worth knowing. Electrically charged fences are prohibited citywide. Barbed wire is prohibited in residential zones, with a narrow exception for blocking attractive nuisances at a minimum of six feet above grade. On non-residential lots, barbed wire or sharp material may only be used above five feet.

## The Commercial Permit Path, Step By Step

The sequence varies by municipality, but the path on a typical Oakland County commercial fence project looks like this:

1. **Confirm the zoning district and the applicable fence standard.** This sets maximum height, material restrictions, and whether screening is mandatory for the use.
2. **Verify property lines.** Most municipalities will tell you plainly that they do not locate property lines — that's the applicant's responsibility. On a site with an unmarked boundary, a survey is a cheap step that prevents an expensive one.
3. **Request utility locates through 811 before any digging.** Commercial sites are dense with private and public utilities; a fence line often crosses irrigation, gas service, fiber, and electric runs.
4. **Submit zoning approval and site plan materials.** Typically a site plan showing property lines, fence and gate locations, existing easements and structures, plus fence specifications — height, materials, construction type, and which face is finished.
5. **Apply for the building permit.** In many communities the zoning hurdle is administrative and review takes roughly a week; projects with site plan implications take longer.
6. **Schedule the inspection.** Most jurisdictions require a completed-fence inspection.
7. **Coordinate any easement sign-off separately.** If part of the fence crosses a utility or drain easement, that approval runs on its own timeline.

The practical advice: bring your fence contractor in before you submit anything. A plan rejected on height or setback costs a month. A plan drawn to the ordinance the first time usually clears quickly.

## The Three Things That Get Fences Torn Out

We've watched a lot of fence projects get expensive after the fact. Almost always it's one of these three — and all three are avoidable with a site walk before the first post is dug.

**Fire lanes, hydrants, and apparatus access.** A fence cannot block a fire apparatus route, a hydrant, or a fire department connection. If a perimeter line eliminates a turn-around or narrows required access width, the fire authority will require it removed — and no zoning approval protects you from that. Confirm the access plan before finalizing the line.

**Utility easements.** Easements are corridors someone else has the right to dig up. Fence panels inside one can be removed at your cost when the utility works there, and some municipalities require public works or utility sign-off before the fence can even be permitted — as Troy's code demonstrates directly. The usual fixes are removable sections spanning the easement or rerouting the line along it.

**Unapproved screening heights and materials.** Screening that's too short is a compliance failure. Screening over the district's height limit without the required discretionary approval is also a compliance failure. The fix — as in Auburn Hills, where screening above six feet may be substituted with a landscaped greenbelt subject to review — is a design conversation, not a purchase-order conversation.

## Where Wood Wins on a Commercial Site

Commercial material choices get discussed in cost terms, and that's covered in our breakdown of [what a commercial fence installation actually costs for Michigan businesses](/blog/2026-08-22-commercial-fence-cost-pricing-guide). This section is about where wood is genuinely the right call.

**Screening and enclosure.** For dumpster enclosures, open-storage screening, mechanical screening, and multi-family trash areas, wood delivers an opaque surface at a competitive price in a maintenance-replaceable form — repairable panel by panel after plow or truck damage instead of replacing a section of steel.

**Aesthetics where the fence is customer-facing.** Wood reads as intended architecture on a restaurant patio, a multi-family amenity area, or a screened service entrance beside a storefront. It also matches the residential neighborhoods many Oakland County commercial properties sit beside — which is privacy you can see, handled the way the neighbors will appreciate.

**Communities and associations.** Multi-family and association properties generally want one consistent look across trash enclosures, amenity areas, and boundary fencing. Wood satisfies a board's aesthetic vote and a municipality's screening requirement at the same time.

**Where wood is the wrong call:** high-security applications requiring climb resistance, sites where anything but open mesh will obstruct surveillance, and locations where the ordinance effectively mandates a material — as in Rochester Hills, where chain link is restricted and ornamental material is contemplated for taller security fences.

For many commercial sites the best answer is a hybrid: steel posts set in concrete, wood infill panels, and hardware sized for daily commercial use. You get the look of wood, the structural predictability of steel in the ground, and a fence that's repairable in pieces.

## The Specifications That Matter More on a Commercial Job

A commercial fence carries the same Michigan risks as a residential one — rot, frost heave, wind, fastener failure — with less tolerance for downtime. The specifications that matter most:

- **Ground-contact rated posts.** Posts in soil should be rated for ground contact (UC4A or equivalent). This is not a place to save money: the post is the part you cannot replace without rebuilding the fence.
- **Footings below the frost line.** Michigan frost depth runs roughly 42 to 48 inches depending on location, and standard practice — consistent with code commentary adopted by Michigan, Wisconsin, and Minnesota building departments — is to set the bottom of the footing a minimum of six inches below the published frost line. Shallow footings are why posts tilt three winters after installation.
- **Fasteners matched to the wood.** Hot-dip galvanized (G90) or stainless, sized for the load, with the correct coating for treated or cedar material. Fastener failure is the most common "why is my fence coming apart" call we get on commercial screening.
- **Wind load on solid panels.** An eight-foot solid panel catches a lot of wind — on an open industrial site that's a structural design condition, not an aesthetic. Post spacing, post size, rail count, and bracing all follow from it.
- **A real pad for enclosures.** Dumpster and equipment enclosures should sit on a pad, with bollards at the container and truck turning points.
- **Commercial-grade gate hardware.** Hinges, latches, and frames that survive a hauler backing into them for a decade, with a clear opening sized to the equipment servicing the site.

## What a Fence Actually Prevents, and What It Doesn't

It's worth being honest about the security case, because it's why many of these projects get funded.

Nationally, property crime is falling — FBI 2025 data shows property crime down 12.4 percent and burglary down 15.8 percent. Construction and jobsite theft has moved the other way, driven by commodity prices rather than crime rates: industry security reporting put metal theft up 77 percent in 2025, with jobsite theft roughly doubling between 2024 and 2025, and multiple sources estimate 30 to 40 percent of copper theft incidents occur at construction and industrial sites. The last comprehensive national benchmark, published in 2016, estimated $300 million to $1 billion in annual equipment theft losses with a recovery rate near one in five. No current national count exists — but the trend reporting is consistent.

A fence doesn't stop a determined thief. What it does is raise the effort required, channel the points of entry, and create a lit, camera-covered perimeter where detection can actually work — plus satisfy the site-level expectations insurers and property managers increasingly attach to coverage and leases. The strongest commercial setups pair a defined perimeter with controlled gates, lighting, and cameras; fencing is the frame that makes the rest function.

For open storage, screening is a compliance function first and a security function second. The business case that gets approved is usually simpler than security: a screened, orderly, maintained yard changes how customers, tenants, and municipal inspectors perceive the property — and it removes the citation risk entirely.

## Gates, Haulers, Plows, and Salt: The Daily Reality of a Commercial Fence

The failure points on a commercial fence are rarely mid-run. They're where people and equipment meet the fence every week.

**Gate width and swing.** Size gates to the equipment, not to the fence line. Confirm the hauler's truck dimensions and approach before drawing the opening. A gate that swings into a snow storage area in January will not open in January.

**Snow storage and plow damage.** Every commercial site needs somewhere to push snow. If that somewhere is against a fence line, expect a winter of panels taking hits. Designating snow storage away from fence corners — and choosing a design that's repairable in sections — costs less than annual repairs.

**Salt and deicer exposure.** Commercial lots get salt applied near entrances and service lanes. Fasteners and hardware near those areas need coatings that survive it, and wood that stays wet and salty in one spot all winter needs to be able to dry. Keeping soil and mulch below the bottom of the boards extends the service life of any fence in a commercial lot.

**Access and visibility.** Never fence off a fire department connection, a hydrant, or the only path a service truck can use — and leave a maintenance access point that doesn't require walking a quarter mile with a ladder.

## Why Late September Through First Freeze Is Prime Commercial Season

Here's the practical argument for scheduling a commercial fence project right now, in the last week of September.

**Digging gets harder, not easier.** Once Oakland County ground freezes — often late November into December, deeper through January and February — augering footings requires equipment and patience, and frost works against the concrete cure window. A commercial footing that has to reach forty inches plus six below grade is a warm-weather project by nature.

**The calendar is in your favor.** Spring and summer are peak season for every fence company in Michigan. Fall is when crews have room and material lead times shorten. A project quoted today is more likely to be built in October than a project quoted in April is to be built in May.

**A secured site is a winter-ready site.** Businesses that store anything outdoors, or leave equipment on-site, want a screen and a closed gate before the snow flies — not a project lingering into December.

**Winter damage is easier to prevent than repair.** A fence built with proper footings before freeze-up goes into spring thaw with concrete fully cured and posts plumb. Every fence rushed into frozen ground in December is a candidate for the leaning-post call in April.

If your project needs site plan review, sequencing matters: start approvals now so the build lands in October and November, rather than starting approvals in November and building in a frozen January.

## What to Ask Before You Sign

1. Are you licensed and insured, and can you provide a certificate of insurance with general liability limits?
2. Will you write the fence specification to my zoning district's requirements and pull the permit, or do I need to?
3. How are posts specified for ground contact, and how deep will footings go below Michigan's frost line?
4. Which fasteners and coatings are you using, and why?
5. Can I see references from commercial projects with similar gates and haulers?
6. Who disposes of the old fence, and is that in the price?
7. What is the gate clear opening dimension, and has it been checked against the equipment servicing this site?
8. What does the warranty actually cover?
9. How do you handle a footing that hits ledge rock or a utility conflict mid-install?
10. What happens if the municipality requires a change after inspection?

## A Typical Commercial Project, Start to Finish

Every site is different, but the shape of a mid-sized commercial project is consistent. What follows is a composite of the kind of job we see most often in Oakland County — a light-industrial property with roughly 900 linear feet of perimeter, two drive gates, and a dumpster enclosure.

**Week 1 — Site walk and scope.** Confirm the zoning district and screening obligations, walk the line with the property manager, and note every place the fence meets a fire lane, easement, or service entrance. Confirm the hauler's truck and required clear opening. Deliver a written specification and a quote separating perimeter, gates, and enclosure.

**Week 2 — Approval.** Submit zoning approval and permit materials with a site plan showing the fence line, gates, easements, and specifications. Resolve any property line question now.

**Weeks 3–4 — Scheduling and locates.** Utility locates through 811, material order, crew scheduling, and tenant coordination on access — including the hours the loading dock must stay clear.

**Weeks 4–6 — Construction.** Remove and haul out the old fence, auger and set footings in concrete below the frost line, build the gates and enclosure, install bollards and hardware, clean the site, and walk it with the owner.

**Week 6 — Inspection and closeout.** Municipal inspection, punch items, warranty documentation, and one maintenance note: keep soil and mulch below the bottom board, and keep the snow plow off the fence line.

The point is the sequencing. Most of the value in a commercial fence project is created before anyone digs a hole — in the fence line, the height, the gates, and the material your ordinance and your operating reality actually call for.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a business need a permit to install a fence in Oakland County, Michigan?**
Yes, in nearly every case. Oakland County doesn't administer local zoning — each city, village, and township enforces its own fence ordinance, and non-residential fences typically require zoning approval plus a building permit. Some communities, like Rochester Hills, also require explicit Building Official approval of the fence itself before work begins. Confirm with the local building department before ordering material.

**How tall can a commercial fence be in Oakland County?**
It depends on the municipality and zoning district. A common standard is a maximum of eight feet for non-residential fences, with some communities allowing taller fences for security by discretionary approval — Rochester Hills caps non-residential fences at eight feet while allowing anodized aluminum security fencing up to ten feet at the Planning Commission's discretion. Some industrial districts allow more. There is no single county-wide number.

**Are dumpster enclosures required by ordinance in Michigan?**
For most commercial properties, yes — screening waste containers from public view is standard in Michigan municipal zoning codes, and many communities require opaque screening on three sides. Height, materials, gate requirements, and pad requirements vary. In Auburn Hills, screening of open storage including wastes must be no less than the height of the items stored, with a minimum of six feet when adjacent to residential property.

**Can I put a fence inside a utility easement?**
It's possible but usually conditional, and sometimes it's a hard stop before permitting. Easements give utilities the right to access and excavate, and a fence in that corridor can be removed at the owner's expense. Some municipalities require public works or utility approval before issuing a fence permit at all — Troy's code blocks permit issuance for a fence in an easement until the Director of Public Works signs off. The practical fixes are removable sections spanning the easement or rerouting the line.

**What's the difference between property demarcation fencing and security fencing?**
Demarcation is boundary definition: a visible, maintained line establishing where the property starts and ends. Security fencing is engineered for resistance — taller, stronger, climb-resistant, and usually paired with controlled gates, lighting, and cameras. On most Oakland County commercial sites the perimeter is demarcation plus controlled access, with security-grade fencing reserved for high-value or high-risk areas.

**How long does a commercial fence installation take?**
Construction on a mid-sized project — a few hundred to about a thousand linear feet with gates and an enclosure — usually runs two to four weeks of on-site work once materials are staged and approvals are in hand. The permit and approval phase is often the longer part, typically a couple of weeks and longer with site plan review or easement sign-off. Material lead times can stretch in peak season, which is another reason fall scheduling helps.

**Do commercial fences have to be screened from residential property?**
Often yes, and it's frequently why the fence is being built. Where a non-residential use abuts a residential zone, many ordinances require an obscuring wall or fence — Auburn Hills requires at least six feet, or the height of the stored materials, whichever is higher, when open storage abuts a residential zone or mobile home park. Height limits, materials, and maintenance obligations are set by the individual municipality.

**What material is best for a commercial fence in Michigan?**
It depends on the job. Wood is the best value for opaque screening, dumpster enclosures, restaurant patios, and multi-family aesthetics, and it's repairable panel by panel after plow or truck damage. Steel posts set in concrete with wood infill add structural predictability. Where ordinances restrict chain link or require ornamental material, wood and ornamental steel are the practical alternatives, and security-rated applications may require a specific material approved by the municipality.

**Can a fence be installed after the ground freezes?**
Technically yes, with the right equipment, but it's slower, more expensive, and concrete cure conditions in frozen ground are less forgiving. Because footings must reach below Michigan's frost line — roughly 42 to 48 inches, plus a six-inch margin — a freeze-up install means breaking through frost to dig. Scheduling between late September and first freeze is the practical answer.

**How much does commercial fencing cost per linear foot?**
Commercial pricing varies widely with material, height, gate count, site conditions, and access. Rather than repeat ranges here, we published a full breakdown of commercial fence pricing, quote drivers, and value in our [commercial fence cost guide for Michigan businesses](/blog/2026-08-22-commercial-fence-cost-pricing-guide). The short version: get a written quote that separates perimeter, gates, and enclosures, and compare line by line.

A commercial fence in Oakland County is a permit project, a compliance project, a security decision, and a maintenance commitment — in that order. The municipalities around Detroit's northern suburbs are specific about heights, screening, materials, and easements, and a fence that ignores those details tends to get rebuilt rather than adjusted.

Get the ordinance right, put the footings below the frost line, size the gates to the trucks that will use them, and put the project on a schedule that beats the freeze. That's how a fence does its job for decades instead of becoming next spring's problem.
