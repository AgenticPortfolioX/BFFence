# What a Wood Fence Actually Costs in Oakland County: A 2026–27 Line-by-Line Breakdown, From the Post Holes to the Permit

Every November, the same three phone calls happen in the same order. A homeowner in Rochester Hills, or Auburn Hills, or Troy gets a quote, gets a second quote, and gets a third — and the three numbers don't agree. Not by a little. By three or four thousand dollars on what is supposedly the same fence.

Most of the time, nobody in that story is lying. What's happening is that "a fence" isn't a product with a price, it's a construction project with a scope. Two bids for "160 feet of six-foot cedar privacy fence" can describe genuinely different objects: different post spacing, different board thickness, different foundation depth, different hardware, and one of them includes pulling the old fence while the other quietly doesn't.

So here is what the number actually is. Below is what wood fencing costs in Oakland County right now, in the 2026–27 pricing cycle, built from line items instead of a single per-foot figure — because the per-foot figure is what everybody publishes and it's the one number that can't answer your question.

A note on where these numbers come from: they're ours. They're what we quote and what we build, checked against 2026 contractor data for Metro Detroit and against national ranges to make sure we're not living in our own bubble. Your job will land somewhere in these bands, and the sections below explain exactly which way it moves and why.

## Why the Per-Foot Number You Were Quoted Isn't a Price

A per-foot price is a division problem, not a quote. It's the total divided by the linear footage — which means the number carries no information about what's inside it. A $29-per-foot bid and a $41-per-foot bid for the same 160 feet can both be internally honest. They're just pricing different fences.

Seven variables move that number, and every one of them is a real decision somebody has to make:

**Height.** A six-foot fence uses roughly 35% more picket than a four-foot fence and needs a third rail. Height is the single biggest driver after material choice, and it's the one most often glossed over — "privacy fence" can mean four feet, six feet, or eight feet, and only one of those actually blocks a sightline from a neighbor's second-story window.

**Style.** Side-by-side dog-ear is the cheap, standard build. Board-on-board overlaps two rows of pickets so the fence looks finished from both sides and closes the gaps that open as boards dry — it uses roughly 1.6 to 1.7 times the pickets and costs 20–30% more. Shadowbox costs more than dog-ear and delivers less privacy. Lattice top, cap rail, and trim each add material and labor.

**Species.** Eastern white cedar, western red cedar, and pressure-treated pine are three different price tiers, and in Michigan the difference matters for how the fence ages, not just what it costs. We covered the species question in detail in [our cedar comparison](https://bffence.com/updates/2026-11-18-cedar-species-northern-white-western-red-michigan); the cost version is that western red costs meaningfully more per board foot than eastern white, and both cost more than treated pine.

**Post spacing and post size.** Six-foot spacing with 4x4 posts is a different fence than eight-foot spacing with 4x4 posts, which is a different fence again from eight-foot spacing with 6x6 posts. Fewer posts is cheaper today and more expensive in year eight.

**Foundation.** In Michigan, posts set to 48 inches in concrete are the standard for a six-foot privacy fence, because the state's code frost depth is 42 inches. Posts tamped into gravel at 30 inches cost a fraction as much and are the number one reason fences lean after a hard freeze.

**Gates.** Gates are their own construction problem. A gate is framed, braced, and hung on hardware that has to survive a hundred freeze-thaw cycles a year. It is not a section of fence with hinges on it, and it is not priced like one.

**Site.** A flat, open backyard with trailer access prices one way. A fence line that steps down two feet of grade, runs through a stand of maples, and sits forty yards from the nearest driveway prices another way entirely.

That's the whole argument for reading a quote line by line. The per-foot number is the output. The lines are the input, and the lines are where you find out whether you're comparing two fences or two ideas of a fence.

## Where Your Money Actually Goes: A 160-Foot Cedar Fence, Line by Line

Here is a real-shaped job: 160 linear feet, six-foot eastern white cedar, dog-ear pickets, one four-foot walk gate, existing fence removed and hauled away, permit pulled, north Oakland County.

| Line item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Cedar pickets, 1x6x6 dog-ear, ~385 pieces including waste and gate infill | $1,100 |
| Cedar posts, 4x4x8, 24 pieces | $515 |
| Cedar rails, 2x4x8, 66 pieces (three per bay) | $700 |
| Concrete, 60 bags of 80-lb mix, two to three per post | $390 |
| Stainless ring-shank fasteners, hinges, latch, gate frame hardware | $245 |
| Removal and disposal of 160 LF of existing fence | $560 |
| Permit fee and application time | $145 |
| Labor, two-person crew, roughly 3.5 days | $2,050 |
| Equipment: auger, trailer, small tools | $180 |
| Overhead, insurance, and margin | $620 |
| **Total** | **$6,505 — about $40.66 per linear foot** |

Look at the shape of that. Materials are about 45% of the job. Labor is about 31%. Removal is 9%. Overhead and margin — the part homeowners are most suspicious of and the part that pays for the truck, the insurance, the warranty, and somebody still being in business in year five — is under 10%.

That 10% is the whole reason the low-bid conversation gets confusing. If a company is operating on 8% margin and another is operating on 22%, the second one can afford to send somebody back in year three when a gate sags, and the first one can't. You are not only buying boards. You're buying the existence of the company that will answer the phone.

### The board-foot math, if you want to check it

Material prices in 2026 are not subtle. Pressure-treated pine has been running roughly $4.50–$7.00 per board foot at retail; western red cedar $6.00–$9.00. A 1x6x6 picket is about 2.25 board feet, which is why picket price dominates the material line: at 385 pieces, every dollar of picket price is $385 on the quote.

This is also why the market context matters. In the summer of 2025 the U.S. Department of Commerce raised combined antidumping and countervailing duties on Canadian softwood from about 14.4% to 35.19%. A 2026 review trimmed the components, but a separate 10% Section 232 tariff layered on top holds the effective burden near 35% — and most of what goes into a Michigan wood fence is Canadian softwood, whether it's the treated pine rails or the cedar pickets. Framing lumber sat around $872 per thousand board feet in January 2026.

Practically, that means two questions are worth asking any contractor in the 2026–27 season: is this a firm price, and how long is it firm for? A quote that's good for 30 days is a normal quote. A quote with no expiration during a period of 35% duties is a quote that will get renegotiated at the worst possible moment — usually after the old fence is already down.

## The Seven Things That Move a Fence Quote 10–40%

These are the adders. None of them are unusual in Oakland County; several are the default condition. Each figure below is priced against the 160-foot baseline above.

| Condition | Typical adder | On the 160 LF job |
|---|---|---|
| Existing fence removal and disposal | $3 – $5 per LF | $480 – $800 |
| Grade change requiring stepped panels | 10–25% of labor | $205 – $510 |
| Heavy clay or glacial till, hand-digging or rock bar | $15 – $40 per post | $360 – $960 |
| Tight access, material hand-carried past a house or garage | flat $300 – $900 | $300 – $900 |
| Trees and root systems along the line | $150 – $600 | $150 – $600 |
| Corner lot or front-yard run subject to setback review | extra footage plus possible survey | $200 – $1,200 + footage |
| Frozen ground, December through February | 5–12% | roughly $250 – $600 |

**Removal** is the most common and the most frequently omitted. A 22-year-old treated pine fence comes out faster than a cedar fence that's been in the ground since the 1990s with posts set in concrete collars. Hauling is not free — a 160-foot fence is roughly a trailer load and a half of material, and the disposal fee is real.

**Grade** is the one that catches people. A backyard that drops two feet across the run means either stepping the panels or racking them, and racking a six-foot cedar panel more than a few inches looks wrong and stresses the joints. We wrote a full walkthrough of [stepping a fence down a two-foot grade change](https://bffence.com/updates/2026-10-28-rochester-hills-sloped-lot-cedar-fence-grade-change) if you want to see what that actually involves.

**Soil** matters more here than most homeowners expect. Oakland County has heavy clay in a lot of it, sitting on glacial till, and a lot of it is full of fieldstone. An auger that walks through sandy loam will bog down in wet clay and stop dead on a rock shelf. That's hand work, and hand work is hours.

**Access** is invisible on a quote and obvious on a job site. If the fence line is behind a detached garage and the only way in is through a four-foot gate, every post, rail, and bundle of pickets moves by hand.

**Trees** are a design constraint as much as a cost. Posts go where the fence needs to go, and roots go where roots go. Root pruning, post relocation, and occasionally a short jog in the line all cost money.

**Setbacks** are a permitting issue with a footage consequence. Corner lots in Oakland County often have two front yards for setback purposes, and the rules differ by city — [our guide to fence rules by city](https://bffence.com/updates/2026-10-31-oakland-county-fence-rules-by-city-guide) covers the height limits, front-yard lines, and corner clearance for Auburn Hills, Rochester Hills, Rochester, Troy, Novi, and Bloomfield Township.

**Winter** is worth separating from the rest, because it's the one that cuts both ways, and we'll come back to it.

## How a Cheap Bid Gets Cheap: Eight Substitutions and What They Cost Later

This is the section that answers the four-thousand-dollar question. When a bid comes in at $28 per foot against a $41 per foot bid, the difference is almost always some combination of these eight moves.

| Substitution | Saves on the quote | What it costs later |
|---|---|---|
| Posts at 8 ft spacing instead of 6 ft | ~$260 in posts and concrete | Racking panels, sagging between posts, full re-frame in 8–12 years |
| Posts tamped in gravel instead of set in concrete to 48 in | ~$390 concrete plus ~6 labor hours | Frost heave; 6–10 posts reset in year 5–8, typically $1,800–$2,600 |
| Two rails instead of three on a 6 ft fence | ~$230 | Mid-panel sag in 8–10 years, pickets cup as they dry |
| Bright common nails instead of stainless ring-shank | ~$120 | Rust streaks down pickets, nail pop, board replacement |
| 5/4-in pickets or #2 grade instead of 1-in #1 grade | 20–25% of picket cost, ~$250 | Cupping and splitting; 10–15% board replacement by year 10 |
| Staining left out of scope | $480 – $800 | $2.25 per LF every three years, starting sooner than it should |
| Permit not pulled | $50 – $175 | Removal order, disclosure problem at sale, no inspection of the post depth you paid for |
| Builder-grade gate hardware | $60 – $120 | Gate sag and latch misalignment within two or three winters |

Add those up and you get roughly $2,090 on a 160-foot job — about $13 per foot. That is precisely the gap between the $28 bid and the $41 bid. The low bid didn't find a cheaper way to build the same fence. It built a different fence and called it the same name.

The post foundation line is the one to take seriously in Michigan specifically. State code sets frost depth at 42 inches, and the standard for a six-foot privacy fence here is a 10-to-12-inch hole taken to 48 inches with concrete. That's the difference between a fence that's still plumb after fifteen winters and one that starts heaving in year five. It's also $390 in concrete and half a day of labor, which is exactly why it's the first thing to disappear from a low bid.

We've built both kinds of repair. Resetting a line of heaved posts costs more than the concrete would have, and it never quite looks the same again.

## Gates: The Line Item That Decides Your Budget

Gates deserve their own section because they're the most consistently under-quoted item in residential fencing, and the most common source of mid-project change orders.

A four-foot walk gate runs roughly $450–$900 installed in cedar, depending on frame construction and hardware. A five-foot double drive gate runs $1,100–$2,600. A six-foot privacy gate with a matching arch or cap runs higher. Extra walk gates, self-closing or self-latching hardware, and anything automated moves the number again.

Why the spread is so wide: a gate is a rigid frame hung from two posts that move independently, carrying its own weight plus wind load plus whatever a kid swings on. Good gates are built with a diagonal brace, three hinges rather than two, and a latch that has enough adjustability to be re-trued in year four when the hinge post shifts a sixteenth of an inch. Cheap gates are two-by-four frames with two hinges and a barrel bolt, and they show it by the second winter. [Our guide to fence gates](https://bffence.com/updates/2026-10-10-fence-gates-guide-michigan) goes deeper on hardware selection.

Two practical notes. First, ask how many gates are in the bid before you compare totals — a fence with two gates versus a fence with one gate is a $500 swing that has nothing to do with the fence. Second, plan gate placement in the estimate, not on install day. The best gate location is usually where people already walk, and that's rarely where the fence line starts.

## Permits, Licenses, and the Paperwork That Protects Your Money

A fence permit in most Oakland County communities runs somewhere between $50 and $250, and the review is typically one to two weeks. Rochester Hills publishes a fence permit band of $50–$175 with roughly a two-week turnaround. For comparison, Detroit charges $270–$680 for fences above seven feet. The fee is the small part. The inspection is the point: somebody from the city looks at your post depth, your setbacks, and your height before the job closes out.

Who pulls it matters. In Michigan, a residential contractor is responsible for obtaining required permits, and a contract that doesn't say so is a contract missing a required element.

Which brings up the licensing piece, and this is worth knowing because it's cheap to check. Under the Michigan Occupational Code, a contractor doing work totaling $600 or more in labor and materials must hold a Residential Builder or Maintenance & Alteration Contractor license through LARA. Every fence job clears $600. Ask for the license number, then verify it in LARA's public license database or by calling the Residential Builders section at 517-241-9309. A contractor who can't produce a license number is not a contractor who is saving you money.

Michigan also requires a written contract above $600, and the required contents are specific: the company name, address, and phone; the license number; a detailed description of materials including brand, grade, and style; a total price; start and completion dates; a statement that the contractor will obtain permits; insurance and workers' compensation coverage; warranty terms; cleanup; and the payment schedule. If you're solicited at home and sign there, you have three business days to cancel in writing.

Two more items. Michigan sets no statewide cap on deposits — which means the deposit is a negotiation, not a rule, and a reputable contractor does not need the full job paid up front. LARA's own homeowner guidance is blunt about it: never pay for the entire job in advance, and pay by check to the company name. And on larger projects, the owner records a notice of commencement with the county register of deeds before work begins; that's the document that keeps a subcontractor's unpaid bill from becoming a lien on your house.

None of that is fence-specific. All of it is the difference between a project and a problem.

## The 15-Year Cost, Not the Day-One Cost

Here's the comparison that actually matters, because the day-one number rewards whichever material is cheapest to install and ignores everything after.

| Fence | Installed per LF | Upkeep over 15 years | 15-year cost per LF | Expected service life |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pressure-treated pine, 6 ft | $30 avg | 7 stain cycles at ~$2.00 | ~$44 | 15 – 18 years |
| Eastern white cedar, 6 ft | $39 avg | 5 stain cycles at ~$2.25 | ~$50 | 20 – 25 years |
| Western red cedar, 6 ft board-on-board | $51 avg | 5 stain cycles at ~$2.50 | ~$64 | 25 – 30 years |
| Vinyl privacy, 6 ft | $48 avg | wash only, ~$1.00 | ~$49 | 25 – 30 years |
| Composite privacy, 6 ft | $62 avg | wash only, ~$1.00 | ~$63 | 25 – 30 years |
| Ornamental aluminum, 5 ft | $49 avg | wash only, ~$1.00 | ~$50 | 30+ years |

Read that table honestly and it says something a fence contractor isn't supposed to say: over fifteen years, vinyl and cedar land in nearly the same place on cost. Vinyl's upfront premium is roughly cancelled by the staining you don't do. If the only question were money over fifteen years, the two are close enough that it comes down to preference.

So here's where they actually differ, and it's the part the table can't show.

Vinyl is a closed system. When a panel cracks — and in a Michigan cold snap, impact cracks happen — you replace a manufactured section that has to match a product line that may or may not still exist in that profile and color. Cedar is an open system. A damaged board comes out with a flat bar and gets replaced with a board from the lumberyard for the price of a board.

Cedar also takes repairs invisibly and vinyl doesn't. And cedar earns the "privacy you can see" reputation for a reason: it looks like a boundary made of trees rather than a boundary made of product, which in a neighborhood of 1970s and 1980s subdivisions is usually what people are actually after.

Where vinyl genuinely wins: a homeowner who will not stain anything, ever, and knows it. That's a legitimate answer, and if it's your answer, buy vinyl and stop apologizing for it. The worst outcome is buying cedar and then not maintaining it, because an unstained cedar fence in Michigan goes gray, checks, and starts losing boards years earlier than it should. Bare cedar isn't a low-maintenance choice. It's a deferred-maintenance choice with a deadline.

## Why a Late-November Quote Looks Different From a May Quote

There's a real seasonal spread in this trade, and late November is the good side of it.

From roughly December through February, contractors in Oakland County have capacity. The spring backlog hasn't formed, crews are working through smaller jobs, and scheduling a fence is a matter of days rather than weeks. That shows up in pricing as an off-season discount in the 5–10% range across the industry — not because the work is worth less, but because steady winter work beats an idle truck.

The tradeoff is ground conditions. Frozen ground doesn't stop a fence; it changes how the holes get dug. We run augers, thaw with equipment where needed, and set posts in concrete that has to be protected from a hard freeze for a day or two. Cedar and treated pine both work fine in cold. What doesn't work in cold is staining and sealing, which needs sustained temperatures above roughly 50°F and dry wood — so a December install typically means staining the following spring or fall, on a fence that's had a season to dry out. That's actually the correct sequence anyway.

There's a second reason to price in the off-season this particular year. Material pricing has been driven by duty policy through 2025 and 2026, and a quote written in late November with a 30-day firm window lets you commit against today's board prices instead of whatever they are in April when everyone in the county is calling the same three contractors. If you know you're building in spring, locking material pricing in the winter is the single cheapest decision available to you.

One honest caveat: a winter install on a site that needs significant grade work or drainage correction is better done in spring, when the ground can be worked and the water table is where it actually sits. We tell people that when it's true.

## How to Compare Three Bids on One Page

The reason three quotes feel incomparable is that they're organized differently. Normalize them. Put every bid through the same list:

| What to check | Why it decides the comparison |
|---|---|
| Total linear feet, measured the same way | A 12-foot difference in measured run is a $500 difference in price |
| Height, and whether that height is to the top of the picket or the top of the post | Post height versus picket height changes both material and setback compliance |
| Species and grade, named specifically | "Cedar" is three species and at least two grades |
| Picket dimension: 1x6 or 5/4x6 | The thinner board is a straight downgrade at a similar-looking price |
| Post size, spacing, and embedment depth | 4x4 at 8 ft in gravel to 30 in is not the same fence |
| Rail count | Two rails versus three on a 6 ft fence |
| Fastener type | Stainless ring-shank versus bright common nails |
| Gate count, gate width, and hardware | The line most often silently dropped |
| Removal and disposal: included or excluded | $480–$800 on a 160-foot job |
| Permit: pulled by contractor, fee included | A required contract element in Michigan |
| Staining: included, excluded, or priced as an option | $480–$800, and it changes the maintenance clock |
| Payment schedule and deposit | Michigan has no cap, so read it |
| Warranty: what's covered, for how long, labor included | Labor coverage is the part that matters |
| Firm price and expiration date | Material pricing is volatile in this cycle |

Any two bids that survive all fourteen lines are genuinely comparable, and the difference in price will be a difference in margin or overhead — which is a legitimate thing to pay for or not, but it's an honest difference. Any two bids that differ on four or five of those lines aren't comparable at all, no matter what the per-foot number says.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does a 6-foot wood fence cost per linear foot in Oakland County?

Eastern white cedar runs $34–$44 per linear foot installed and pressure-treated pine runs $26–$34, for a standard dog-ear privacy build on level ground with good access. Board-on-board western red cedar runs $44–$58. Gates, removal, permits, and staining are priced separately, and on a typical job those add roughly 15–25% to the total.

### Is cedar worth the extra cost over pressure-treated pine in Michigan?

Usually yes, if you're staying in the house more than eight years. Treated pine costs about $8 per foot less installed, but it moves more through Michigan's freeze-thaw cycles and typically needs its first staining sooner. Cedar's natural rot resistance means it holds up longer through wet-dry cycling, and it takes repairs invisibly. If you're selling in three years, pine is the rational buy. If this is your last fence, cedar is.

### Does removing the old fence cost extra?

Yes — roughly $3–$5 per linear foot, or $480–$800 on a 160-foot run, including hauling and disposal. It's frequently left out of a low bid, which is one of the reasons two quotes for the "same" fence can differ by several hundred dollars before anything else is compared.

### How much does a gate add to a fence?

A four-foot cedar walk gate runs about $450–$900 installed. A five-foot double drive gate runs $1,100–$2,600. Cost depends on frame construction, hinge count, hardware quality, and whether the gate has to be self-closing or self-latching. Ask how many gates are in each bid before comparing totals.

### Do I need a permit for a fence in Oakland County, and who pulls it?

Most Oakland County communities require a fence permit, typically $50–$250 with a one-to-two-week review; Rochester Hills publishes a $50–$175 band. The contractor is responsible for obtaining required permits, and a Michigan residential contract above $600 is required to state that. Confirm the specific rules for your city, since height limits and setback lines differ municipality to municipality.

### What deposit should I pay a fence contractor in Michigan?

Michigan sets no statewide cap on deposits, so treat it as a negotiation. A deposit covering materials and mobilization is normal and defensible; paying the full job up front is not, and LARA's own homeowner guidance says never to do it. Pay by check to the company name, get a receipt, and tie progress payments to completed milestones.

### Is it cheaper to have a fence installed in winter?

Often by 5–10%, and scheduling is faster because the spring backlog hasn't formed. Frozen ground changes how holes are dug but doesn't prevent installation. The one thing that has to wait is staining and sealing, which needs sustained temperatures above about 50°F, so the finish typically happens the following spring — which is the right sequence anyway.

### How much does it cost to stain a new wood fence, and how often?

Roughly $1.50–$3.00 per linear foot per application, so $240–$480 on a 160-foot fence, every two to three years. A new cedar fence should get its first finish within six to twelve months of installation once the wood has dried, then on a two-to-three-year cycle. Skipping it doesn't save the money; it moves it to board replacement.
