## Why Property Line Conflicts Happen (And Why They're Costly)

Most fence disputes start with assumptions. "The old fence was here for 20 years — that must be the line." Or "My deed shows the boundary, so I'm good." Both can be wrong.

Old fences often followed convenience, not accuracy. Deeds reference monuments that may be gone. Surveys from the 1970s used different standards. And in older Oakland County subdivisions like those in Royal Oak, Berkley, or Ferndale, lot lines frequently drift from what hedges, tree lines, or existing fences suggest.

The cost of guessing wrong? Moving a 6-foot wood privacy fence averages $1,200-$1,800 in labor alone — not including potential damage to posts, concrete footings, or finished boards. If you've stained or sealed the fence, you're looking at a full redo. Worse, if the dispute escalates to court, legal fees can easily exceed the fence cost itself.

Michigan law provides clarity, but only if you act before the first post hole is dug.

## Michigan's Fence Laws: What Actually Applies to You

Michigan is a **Home Rule state**. This means Oakland County itself sets almost no fence regulations — your city, village, or township controls height limits, setbacks, permit requirements, and material rules.

### Height Limits: Check Your Municipality
While Oakland County has no county-wide fence height ordinance, incorporated cities within it do — and they vary significantly:

- **Royal Oak**: 6 ft max rear/side, 4 ft max front yard (§323-3)
- **Troy**: 6 ft rear/side, 30 inches front yard (Ch. 83)
- **Farmington Hills**: 8 ft rear/side ( ornamental only), 3 ft front yard (Ch. 11)
- **Birmingham**: Typically follows 6 ft rear/side, 4 ft front
- **Bloomfield Township**: 6 ft rear/side, 4 ft front with stricter corner-lot rules
- **West Bloomfield**: 6 ft rear/side, 4 ft front

Corner lots face additional sight-triangle restrictions — typically limiting fences to 2.5-3 feet within 10 feet of intersections to maintain driver visibility. These aren't suggestions; violating them can result in daily fines until compliance.

**Action step**: Before finalizing your fence height, call your local building or zoning department. Have your address ready. Ask: "What are the maximum fence heights for rear, side, and front yards at my property?" Get it in writing if possible.

### Permit Requirements: When You Need One
Permit rules vary wildly across Oakland County:

- **Farmington Hills**: Requires permit *regardless of size* — $100 fee, needs scaled plot plan showing property lines, fence location, height, materials, and setbacks.
- **Royal Oak**: Permit required for fences over 30 inches tall — $25 fee, online submission.
- **Troy**: Similar to Royal Oak — permit over 30 inches, fee varies by valuation.
- **Pontiac**: Generally requires permit for any new fence construction.
- **Waterford**: Permit required, fee based on fence value.

Many townships (like Orion, Oxford, or Springfield) follow similar patterns but always verify — some exempt fences under 6 feet, others don't.

**Critical note**: HOA rules can be stricter than municipal codes and override them. If you live in a subdivision with a homeowners association, get written approval *before* applying for a municipal permit. HOAs frequently regulate materials, colors, heights, and even require architectural review.

**Action step**: Contact your municipal building department *and* your HOA (if applicable) to confirm permit requirements, fees, and required documentation. Don't assume — verify.

## The Setback Myth: Michigan Doesn't Require One (But Your City Might)

Here's where most homeowners get tripped up: **Michigan state law sets no universal setback requirement** for fences. The Michigan Residential Code exempts standard residential fences under 7 feet from building permits, but that's the *only* statewide standardization.

Setbacks, height limits, and material rules are **entirely local**. In practice:

- Most West Michigan municipalities (and many Oakland County communities) recommend placing fences 2 to 6 inches *inside* the property boundary to avoid encroachment disputes.
- Some communities require specific setbacks — check your local ordinance.
- Building directly on the line without a written neighbor agreement risks future disputes if a survey reveals even slight encroachment.

**Action step**: Ask your municipality: "Is there a required setback from the property line for fences in my zoning district?" If yes, get the exact measurement. If no, strongly consider placing the fence 2-4 inches inside your line as a precaution.

## When to Get a Property Survey: The $500 Question That Saves Thousands

A boundary survey isn't just for property sales. For fence projects, it's cheap insurance.

### What a Boundary Survey Does
A licensed surveyor:
1. Researches your deed, subdivision plat, and historical records
2. Locates or re-establishes your property corners using legal descriptions
3. Sets physical markers (iron pins, concrete monuments) at your boundaries
4. Provides a signed, sealed survey map showing exact lines, easements, and encroachments

### Cost vs. Risk
In 2026, a residential boundary survey in Oakland County typically ranges from **$450 to $1,200**, depending on:
- Lot size and complexity
- Availability of existing subdivision plats
- Terrain and access
- Need for additional research due to unclear records

Compare that to:
- Moving a fence post-install: $1,200-$1,800+ labor
- Legal fees if dispute escalates: $3,000-$10,000+
- Lost time and stress

### Clear Signs You Need a Survey
Get one if:
- You're in an older subdivision (pre-1980) where lot lines may have drifted
- Your deed description is vague or references gone monuments (e.g., "stone wall," "old oak tree")
- There's any uncertainty about where your line sits relative to existing fences, hedges, or landscaping
- Your neighbor has questioned the line in the past
- You're installing near a corner lot, easement, or potential visibility triangle
- You plan to build very close to where you believe the line is

**Action step**: For most Oakland County fence projects in established neighborhoods, budget $600-$800 for a survey. Get quotes from 2-3 LARA-licensed surveyors. Confirm they're insured and provide a stamped map suitable for legal proceedings if needed.

### How to Prepare for the Survey
1. **Clear access**: Trim vegetation around suspected corners and along fence line
2. **Gather documents**: Have your deed, title insurance, and any prior surveys ready
3. **Talk to neighbors**: Inform adjoining owners — surveys often benefit both parties
4. **Mark improvements**: Note existing structures, fences, trees, or landscaping near the line
5. **Schedule MISS DIG 811**: Required before any digging for fence posts — more on this below

## Michigan's Fence Viewers Act: Your Low-Cost Dispute Resolution Tool

Even with perfect preparation, disagreements happen. Maybe your neighbor remembers the line differently, or a new survey reveals a discrepancy. Before you think about court, know about **Michigan's Fence Viewers Act (Act 34 of 1978)**.

This isn't widely known, but every Michigan township *must* appoint at least one fence viewer — a local official trained to resolve boundary fence disputes quickly, affordably, and without court.

### How It Works
1. **You submit a written request** to your township treasurer, describing the fence issue (location, nature of disagreement, desired outcome)
2. **You pay a filing fee** (typically $25, though some townships vary)
3. **The fence viewer notifies your neighbor** in writing at least 5 days before inspection
4. **They inspect the property**, review deeds/surveys if available, and issue a binding decision on:
   - Responsibility for construction/maintenance costs
   - Fence placement relative to the line
   - Required repairs or modifications
5. **You receive written notification** of the decision within 7 days
6. **Either party can appeal** to circuit court if dissatisfied

### What Fence Viewers *Can't* Do
Importantly, fence viewers **cannot determine property boundaries**. If the core dispute is *where the line actually is*, they'll refer you to get a survey. Their strength lies in resolving disagreements about *existing fences* — who pays for repair, whether a fence can stay as-is, or what modifications are needed.

### Real Oakland County Example
In a 2025 case from Waterford Township, two neighbors disagreed over a 20-year-old wood fence. One believed it was on the line; the other's recent survey showed it 4 inches onto their property. Instead of suing, they used the fence viewer process:
- Homeowner A filed request ($25 fee)
- Fence viewer inspected, reviewed both deeds and the recent survey
- Decision: Fence could remain but Homeowner A would pay 40% of any future repair costs (reflecting the encroachment percentage) and grant an easement for the encroached portion
- Both parties agreed, avoiding court costs exceeding $8,000 estimated

### When to Use It
Consider the fence viewer process if:
- You and a neighbor disagree over maintenance or repair of an existing fence
- There's a question about cost-sharing for a fence on or near the line
- You've built a fence and your neighbor claims it encroaches (but you want to avoid court)
- You need a quick, affordable resolution to keep neighborly relations intact

**Action step**: Save your township clerk's contact information. If a fence dispute arises, call first: "How do I initiate a fence viewer request?" Most townships have simple forms or letter requirements.

## Critical Pre-Build Steps: What Every Oakland County Homeowner Should Do

Here's your actionable checklist — follow these in order before signing any contract or digging the first post hole.

### 1. Verify Your Boundary (Strongly Consider a Survey)
- If your lot is older than 1980 or deed description is vague, budget $600-$1,000 for a boundary survey
- Get quotes from 2-3 LARA-licensed surveyors in Oakland County
- Confirm they'll provide a stamped map with corners marked
- Schedule MISS DIG 811 for any digging (required by law — see below)

### 2. Confirm Local Regulations
- Call your city/township building/zoning department
- Ask: maximum heights (rear/side/front), setback requirements, permit needs, fees
- Get specifics for your address and zoning district
- If in an HOA, get written approval *before* municipal permit

### 3. Document Everything
- Save survey map (if obtained)
- Print or save email confirmations of height limits and permit rules
- Take dated photos of the proposed fence line area showing existing conditions
- Keep a notebook of conversations with municipal officials and neighbors

### 4. Use MISS DIG 811 — It's Free and Required by Law
Michigan law (PA 174 of 2013) requires contacting MISS DIG 811 **at least 3 full business days** before digging for fence posts, poles, or any underground work.

- Submit online at [missdig811.org](https://www.missdig811.org/) or call 811
- Utility companies (gas, electric, telecom, water, cable) mark lines at no charge
- Colors: yellow=gas, red=electric, orange=comm/catv, blue=water, green=sewer
- White marks your proposed excavation area
- **Never guess** — hitting a gas line risks explosion, electrocution, or costly repairs

### 5. Get It in Writing
Before work begins:
- Ensure your contract includes: exact fence location (referencing survey if available), materials, height, linear footage, gate count, post size/depth, concrete specifications, removal/haul-away, permit handling, cleanup, and warranty
- If building near the line, consider a simple written agreement with your neighbor acknowledging the planned location (not required, but helpful for rapport)
- Confirm who handles permits and MISS DIG 811 — typically the contractor should

### 6. Understand Michigan's Frost Line Requirement
For longevity in Michigan's freeze-thaw cycles, fence posts should be set **below the frost line** — typically 36-42 inches deep in Oakland County, depending on microclimate. This prevents heaving and premature failure. Reputable contractors will specify this in quotes.

## Seasonal Timing: When to Build Your Fence in Michigan for Best Results

Michigan's climate adds another layer to fence planning that many homeowners overlook. The timing of your installation affects everything from post-setting quality to material longevity and neighbor convenience. Understanding our seasonal rhythms helps you schedule for optimal results.

### The Golden Window: Late Summer to Early Fall
For Oakland County homeowners, late August through mid-October often provides ideal fence-building conditions:

- **Soil conditions**: Ground is workable but not waterlogged from spring rains or frozen from winter
- **Frost line consideration**: Posts set in September have time to settle before the first hard freeze, reducing heaving risk
- **Material stability**: Wood has acclimated to summer humidity, minimizing sudden warping or twisting after installation
- **Contractor availability**: Summer rush has passed, but crews are still booking before winter shutdown

This window allows footings to reach proper depth (typically 36-42 inches below grade in our area) while the soil is still cooperative. A fence installed in early September has 6-8 weeks to settle before winter dormancy.

### Spring Considerations: Wait for the Right Conditions
Spring brings enthusiasm for outdoor projects, but timing matters:

- **Early spring (March-April)**: Soil is often too wet or frozen solid. Digging in saturated ground leads to poor post support and muddy worksites
- **Mid-spring (May)**: Better conditions, but watch for:
  - Late frosts that can heave freshly set posts
  - Rapid grass growth that obscures property lines
  - Neighbor sensitivity to yard disruption during prime outdoor season
- **Late spring (June)**: Generally good, but beginning of summer contractor rush

If you choose spring, aim for mid-to-late May when soil has dried and warmed but before peak heat arrives.

### Summer: Possible with Precautions
July and August installations work but require attention to:

- **Heat stress on materials**: Direct sun can make wood more prone to splitting during handling
- **Water needs**: Freshly pressure-treated posts benefit from consistent moisture as they acclimate
- **Neighbor consideration**: Longer daylight means work hours extend, increasing potential disruption
- **Contractor availability**: Peak season means higher prices and less scheduling flexibility

Summer builds succeed when contractors take extra care with material handling and schedule work for cooler morning hours.

### Winter: Generally Avoid for New Installation
December through February presents significant challenges:

- **Frozen ground**: Increases digging time and cost, may require specialized equipment
- **Concrete issues**: Standard mixes may not cure properly in freezing temperatures
- **Material brittleness**: Wood becomes more susceptible to cracking in extreme cold
- **Safety concerns**: Ice, snow, and reduced daylight create hazardous worksites

The exception is late winter (late February) when an early thaw allows ground preparation, but even then, frost depth remains a concern.

### Weather-Related Delays: Build Flexibility Into Your Plan
Michigan weather demands scheduling flexibility:

- **Rain delays**: Heavy saturation makes digging ineffective and damages lawns
- **Wind considerations**: Gusty days pose safety risks for handling large fence panels
- **Extreme heat**: Safety regulations may limit work hours during heat advisories
- **Severe weather**: Thunderstorms, high winds, or blizzard conditions stop work entirely

Discuss weather policies with your contractor up front. Reputable teams build buffer days into schedules and communicate proactively when conditions warrant postponement.

### Material Considerations by Season
Timing affects how materials behave:

- **Pressure-treated lumber**: Best installed when humidity allows even drying to prevent warping
- **Cedar**: More stable but still benefits from moderate humidity during installation
- **Concrete footings**: Require temperatures above freezing for proper curing (typically 40°F+ for 24-48 hours)
- **Staining/sealing**: Should wait 4-8 weeks after installation for pressure-treated wood to dry sufficiently

### Neighbor Timing Courtesy
As Guardians, we recognize that fence projects impact more than just your property:

- **Weekday vs. weekend**: Some municipalities restrict weekend work hours; check local noise ordinances
- **Holiday sensitivity**: Avoid major holiday weekends when neighbors plan gatherings
- **School schedules**: Consider timing around school start/end if your neighborhood has many children
- **Communication**: Give neighbors a clear timeline including start date, expected duration, and completion date

**Action step**: When getting quotes, ask contractors: "What's your preferred installation window for Oakland County, and how do you handle weather delays?" Their answer reveals experience with our local conditions.

By respecting Michigan's seasonal rhythms, you set your fence up for decades of service rather than fighting against the climate from day one.
