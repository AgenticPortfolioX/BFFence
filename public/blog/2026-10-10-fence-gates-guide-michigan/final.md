## The Gate Is the Part You Actually Use

Think about how you actually live with a fence. You look at it, sure, and a good one is a pleasure to look at. But you *use* the gate. It's the door in the wall — the one moving part in a system that's otherwise entirely static. Everything that comes in and out of your yard passes through it: you, your kids, your dogs, the mower, the wheelbarrow, the delivery driver.

That changes the engineering. A fence panel only has to hold up its own weight and resist wind. A gate has to hold up its own weight, resist wind, *and* carry the repeated load of swinging on a pivot that's anchored in the ground, in a climate that freezes and thaws the ground several times a year. The gate is where a fence becomes mechanical, and mechanics wear out.

So the gate deserves more thought than it usually gets — not less. It's the piece where "Oakland County's Trusted Choice" either earns the phrase or doesn't, because it's the piece you touch every single day.

## Size It Right the First Time

The single most common gate mistake we see is simple: the gate is too narrow. Homeowners picture a gate as a person-sized opening, order a 3-foot gate, and then discover that the mower won't fit, the wheelbarrow won't fit, and the leaf of the gate catches the fence post every time it swings because there's no clearance built into the opening.

The width you need depends entirely on what has to pass through. Here's the working chart we use:

| What goes through | Recommended gate width (clear opening) |
|---|---|
| People only (pedestrian/walk gate) | 36–42 inches |
| People plus wheelbarrow, garden cart, garbage can | 42–48 inches |
| Riding mower / small tractor | 60 inches (5 feet) |
| Single-vehicle driveway gate | 10–12 feet |
| Double (two-leaf) driveway gate | 12–16 feet total, split across two leaves |

Two rules matter more than the chart itself. First, **size the opening, not just the gate leaf.** The clear opening is the space between the posts, minus the space the hinges need and minus the thickness of the leaf as it swings. A 4-foot gate leaf hung in a 4-foot opening will bind. You want the opening at least a few inches wider than the gate, and you want to confirm the *swing arc* clears the fence, a tree, a downspout, or a grade change.

Second, **don't size to today's needs — size to the next five years.** If a riding mower is even a maybe, build the mower gate now. Widening an opening later means moving posts, which means digging up concrete footings, which is the most expensive way to save an inch.

## Walk Gates, Drive Gates, and Double Gates

Almost every residential gate falls into one of three families, and each has its own rules of thumb.

**Walk gates (also called pedestrian gates)** are the everyday gates — 36 to 48 inches wide, hung to match the fence. A walk gate is what your family uses to get to the mailbox, the trash, or the neighbor's yard. If pets use it, think about height and the gap under the bottom: a small dog can slip under a gate with a 6-inch ground gap, so we hang pet-side walk gates low to grade and check the bottom clearance.

**Drive gates** span a vehicle opening — a driveway, a side yard where a trailer parks, or a spot where you want to pull a boat or camper behind the fence. They run roughly 10 to 16 feet. At that width, weight and physics take over: a leaf that big wants to sag, so drive gates are almost always built as **two leaves** rather than one giant panel, and they need **heavier posts** and **armored hardware** — drop rods and heavier hinges — to stay true.

**Double gates** are two leaves that meet in the middle. They're the standard answer for wide or sloping driveways, because each leaf is half the weight of a single wide gate. The catch is the meeting point: if the ground under the driveway leaf is uneven, or one leaf's post settles more than the other, the two leaves stop lining up. That's why double gates get a **drop rod** (a steel rod that drops into the ground or a receiver to hold the far leaf in place) and a **cane bolt** (a rod that anchors the free leaf to the ground when it's closed).

One more type worth naming: **the "man-gate" in a big double gate.** A lot of homeowners add a small pedestrian leaf built into one side of a large double drive gate, so you don't have to open the whole 16 feet just to walk the dog out.

## Match the Gate to the Fence — and Build the Frame Right

A gate should look like it belongs to the fence it's hung in, but it can't be *built* like a fence panel. A fence panel hangs on three fixed points and never moves. A gate is a moving frame, and it has to be rigid enough to stay square for decades while its weight pulls it out of square every time it swings.

That means the **frame** does most of the work. A good wood gate is built as a squared frame — two vertical stiles, top and bottom rails, and a **diagonal brace** running corner to corner — with the fence boards attached over it. That diagonal is not decoration. It's what keeps the frame from racking into a parallelogram the first time the gate slams. A gate built like a flat slab of fence boards, with no frame behind it, is a gate that will sag, guaranteed.

On material:

- **Cedar** is the classic Michigan choice for wood gates — naturally rot-resistant, lighter than treated pine, and beautiful with a stain.
- **Pressure-treated pine** is heavier and stronger, and it's a fine frame material, but treated wood moves more as it dries and takes up moisture. Built and sealed correctly, it's a durable, budget-friendlier wood gate.
- **Ornamental aluminum** makes the lightest, longest-lived gates of all. If you don't want to think about the gate again for thirty years, aluminum is hard to beat, especially for drive gates where weight is the enemy.
- **Vinyl and composite** gates are low-maintenance and never need staining, but vinyl gates can get heavy and flex in temperature swings, so the frame and hinges have to be sized for the load.

Whichever you choose, the rule is the same: **the gate is where the lightest material and the heaviest load meet.** Match it to the fence for looks, then overbuild the frame a little for the life it's going to lead.

## Hardware Is Where a Michigan Gate Lives or Dies

If we could point a homeowner at one thing that determines whether a gate is still swinging smoothly in ten years, it would be the hardware. Paint and stain are cosmetic. Hardware is structural. And hardware is where cheap installs cut the corner a homeowner can't see — until the gate is dragging and the latch won't catch.

**Hinges.** Fixed hinges (strap hinges on wood, or T-hinges on lighter gates) hold the gate on its pivot. **Self-closing hinges** — usually a spring-loaded or adjustable mechanism — return the gate to closed on their own, which is required for pool gates and handy anywhere you don't want a gate left open. **Self-latching** hardware engages automatically as the gate closes, again required at pools and a smart upgrade anywhere.

**Latches.** A thumb-latch or gravity latch is the standard for a driveway gate. A pool gate needs a compliant self-latching mechanism, and the latch has to be positioned so a small child can't reach it — which the code measures precisely (more on that below). For security, a keyed latch or a hasp you can lock gets you a real barrier rather than a gesture.

**Drop rods and cane bolts.** These aren't the latch; they're the anchor. On a double gate, the drop rod holds the active leaf so you can open just the one you need, and the cane bolt pins the far leaf down so both leaves stay put. Without them, a double gate wobbles and its leaves stop meeting cleanly.

**Fasteners.** This is the quiet one that matters most in Michigan. Use **hot-dip galvanized or stainless steel** screws and bolts. Plain steel rusts and bleeds black streaks down your fence. Electro-galvanized coatings, the shiny silver screws in the big-box bins, are not enough for a gate that lives outdoors for twenty years.

| Hardware job | What to use | Why it matters in Michigan |
|---|---|---|
| Hinge (wood gate) | Hot-dip galvanized or stainless strap/T hinge | Uncoated steel rusts, stains wood, and freezes solid |
| Self-closing | Adjustable spring hinge | Required for pool gates; keeps gates from being left open |
| Self-latching | Compliant gravity/spring latch | Pool code and child safety; no manual latch to forget |
| Double-gate anchor | Drop rod + cane bolt | Holds leaves in place so they keep meeting cleanly |
| Fasteners | Hot-dip galvanized or stainless | Ordinary steel corrodes fast in wet/freeze-thaw cycles |

## Built for the Freeze: Gate Posts, Footings, and the Frost Line

Ask a contractor what kills fences in Michigan, and you'll hear one word more than any other: **heave.** Our ground freezes, thaws, refreezes, and thaws again all winter. Water in the soil expands when it freezes, and it lifts anything sitting shallow in the ground. A fence post that's set too shallow will rise a half inch, then an inch, then two — and the fence along it goes out of line.

A gate post takes that problem and multiplies it. A regular line post only has to hold a fence panel that's nailed to its neighbors. A **gate post holds the entire weight of the gate on a lever arm**, plus the repeated force of every swing, plus the gate's own momentum when it's caught by the wind. If a gate post heaves even a little, three things happen at once: the gate leaf drops and starts dragging, the gap at the latch changes, and the hinge geometry skews so the gate swings crooked. For a **self-closing pool gate**, a post that heaves an inch can stop the gate from latching at all — which turns a safety feature into a liability and a failed inspection into a phone call.

So the post and footing for a gate need to be built for the load:

- **Depth below frost.** Michigan's residential code requires exterior footings to extend well below grade — on the order of three and a half feet — for buildings, and fence posts in this county are commonly set at least 36 inches deep in concrete. Gate posts, which carry far more load, deserve to go deeper, in the 42-to-48-inch range, fully below the frost line, in a properly sized concrete footing.
- **Post size.** A 4x4 is the minimum for a light walk gate. For a drive gate, a double gate, or anything heavy, step up to a **6x6** post. A post that's undersized for the gate will bend and lean no matter how good the hinge is.
- **Keep wood out of the dirt.** Use a ground-contact-rated post, set it so the wood doesn't sit in pooled water, and make sure the grade around the footing sheds water away rather than toward it. Rot at the base of a gate post is what turns a leaning post into a broken one.

This is exactly the kind of thing that's invisible in a finished fence and impossible to fix cheaply later. Getting the footings right on day one is what makes a gate "built to last" instead of "built to look good for three years."

## If There's a Pool: The Michigan Pool-Gate Code, in Plain English

If your property has a pool, spa, or hot tub, your gate stops being a convenience and becomes a **life-safety device**, and it's regulated in a way the rest of your fence is not. Michigan applies the pool-barrier rules through the **Michigan Residential Code** (based on the 2015 International Residential Code), which points to **IRC Appendix G**, and that state code **preempts local amendments** under the Stille-DeRossett-Hale Single State Construction Code Act.

The core rules a residential pool barrier has to meet:

- The barrier stands at least **48 inches** tall, measured on the side **facing away from the pool**.
- No opening anywhere in the barrier lets a **4-inch sphere** pass through.
- The gap between the bottom of the barrier and the ground is no more than **2 inches** on a non-solid surface (4 inches if it rests on a solid surface like a deck).
- Chain-link openings are no wider than **1.25 inches** unless slats reduce the openings to 1.75 inches or less.
- Where the barrier uses horizontal and vertical members and the horizontal members are less than 45 inches apart, the horizontal members go on the **pool side**, and vertical spacing stays at 1.75 inches or less.
- Where the house itself forms part of the barrier, doors opening directly to the pool area get **alarms**, or the pool has a compliant **power safety cover**.

And the gate itself — this is the part inspectors love to test:

- Pool gates must **open outward, away from the pool**, never toward it.
- They must be **self-closing**, so they return to closed on their own from any position, and **self-latching**, so the latch engages automatically as the gate closes.
- The latch release is either at least **54 inches above grade**, or, if it sits lower, on the **pool side of the gate at least 3 inches below the top**, with no opening larger than **half an inch** within 18 inches of the latch — so a small child can't reach over or through to release it.

Two practical notes. First, **city rules vary on the edges** even though the barrier height is state code. Some communities, like White Lake, do not accept a powered safety cover as a substitute for a fence; others set minimum distances from the pool to the lot line. Second, **the gate post is the weak point of the whole system.** A self-closing gate only works if the post holding it stays plumb. A gate post that heaves in a January thaw stops latching, and a pool gate that doesn't latch is the exact failure the code exists to prevent. Build the pool gate post like it's carrying a car, because in terms of responsibility, it is.

## Security and Privacy: What a Gate Actually Controls

A fence draws a line; **the gate decides who crosses it.** That's why we think of the gate as an access-control feature, not just a hole in the fence.

**A lock is only as good as the gate and the posts around it.** A keyed latch bolted to a gate hung on a wobbly post protects nothing. For real security, you want a solid gate, a lockable latch, hinges a person can't lift the gate off of, and posts set deep enough that nobody can shove the whole assembly out of line. On a privacy fence, a full-height gate that matches the fence line keeps the visual barrier intact and gives you a lockable access point for the yard, the side gate, or the driveway.

**Privacy, on the other hand, is about sight lines.** A solid gate preserves the privacy line your fence draws — "privacy you can see," the kind that lets you enjoy your backyard without feeling watched. An ornamental or open gate in the middle of a solid privacy run breaks that line; where the front of the yard is concerned, many Oakland County cities require exactly that kind of open, ornamental fencing and won't allow a solid gate in front of the building line at all.

**Watch for the visibility triangle.** On a corner lot, every city in the county enforces a sight-triangle rule at the intersection so drivers can see pedestrians. Troy, for instance, caps a fence — and any gate in it — at 30 inches high within a 25-foot sight triangle at an intersection, and limits fences ahead of the front building line to 30 inches generally. If you're putting a gate on a corner lot, that gate has to respect the triangle just like the fence does. It's also why some cities, like Bloomfield Township, keep solid fencing to the side and rear only, with anything taller or front-facing needing extra approval.

The gate is where security, privacy, and code all meet — which is why it's worth planning with your installer rather than improvising after the fence is up.

## Why Gates Sag — and How to Stop It

Sag is the classic gate problem, and it's almost always one of four causes:

1. **A failing hinge.** Hinges wear, loosen, or rust, and a hinge that's lost its bite lets the gate drop at the latch end.
2. **A leaning or heaved post.** If the post moves, the hinge moves with it, and a gate is only as square as the posts it hangs between.
3. **A frame with no diagonal brace.** Wood without bracing racks into a parallelogram over time.
4. **An overloaded leaf.** A gate that's too wide for its posts and hardware will sag no matter how well it's hung, because the weight simply wins.

**Prevention is cheaper than repair.** A framed gate with a diagonal brace, hung on adequate posts set below the frost line, with real hardware, simply doesn't sag the way a slab-and-two-hinges gate does. If you want extra insurance on a wide or heavy gate, an **anti-sag kit** — a cable and turnbuckle that runs from the bottom corner on the hinge side to the top corner on the latch side — lets the frame hold its own weight and can be tensioned later if it starts to drop.

**If your gate is already sagging,** the fix depends on the cause, and you should always diagnose before you grab a wrench:

- Tighten or replace the hinges and re-check the swing.
- Square the frame — measure both diagonals; if they're not equal, the frame has racked and needs to be pulled back square before it's braced.
- Add an anti-sag kit or a diagonal brace if the frame is flexing.
- If the post is leaning or heaving, the real fix is the post, not the hinge. Sometimes that means resetting or replacing the post, which is why the deep-certified-footing approach at install time pays for itself.

We tell homeowners the same thing we tell each other: a gate that's worth hanging once is worth hanging right. Patching symptoms — a washer under a hinge here, a shim there — buys months, not years.

## What We See in the Field

After years of this, we've stopped being surprised by any particular gate problem, but a few patterns come up again and again across Oakland County yards:

- **The gate that drags every February.** Almost always a post that has taken on water at the base or heaved in a thaw. The homeowner blames the hinge; the hinge is fine. The ground moved.
- **The double drive gate where the leaves stopped meeting.** One post settled, and the two leaves that used to line up now fight each other. A drop rod and a cane bolt would have kept them honest.
- **The pool gate that passed inspection and stopped latching two winters later.** It latched fine on day one. Then the post heaved, and a self-closing gate on a tilted post doesn't self-close anymore.
- **The beautiful gate on cheap hinges.** The wood is gorgeous; the hardware was the shiny bin at the big-box store; the gate is rust-stained and stiff within two seasons.

None of these are exotic failures. They're all the same lesson: on a gate, the invisible choices — footing depth, post size, hardware quality, bracing — are the choices that decide how the gate behaves in year fifteen.

## The Local Rules That Touch Your Gate

Michigan is a **Home Rule** state: Oakland County itself has no countywide fence ordinance, and each city and township writes its own rules. In practice, a gate is handled as part of the fence project, so the rules that apply to your fence apply to your gate. A few gate-relevant points worth knowing:

- **Permits.** Many communities require a permit or zoning approval before a fence and gate go up. Some, like Rochester (the city), require a zoning permit and keep fences and gates in the rear and side yards, with the good side facing the neighbor. Some, like Bloomfield Township, allow only side and rear fencing up to 4 feet without extra approval, with anything taller or front-facing needing an appeal. Troy requires a fence permit generally, and treats **fences 7 feet and taller, plus all commercial fence and gate projects of any height**, as building-permit work.
- **Where gate posts can go.** Perimeter fences — and any gate within a few feet of the lot line — often trigger extra property-line verification. The Village of Lake Orion, for example, asks for a staked boundary survey or notarized neighbor consent for a perimeter fence, and enforces a clear-vision triangle near drives and intersections.
- **Shared-boundary questions.** If your gate or fence sits on or near a boundary and a neighbor uses it, Michigan's **Fence Viewers Act** gives townships a formal way to settle cost and animal-damage disputes. It's not the tool for boundary disputes — those need a survey — but it settles the "who pays" question.
- **HOA overlays.** Most newer Oakland County subdivisions add their own covenants on top of the city rules, and HOAs are frequently stricter — on fence height, gate style, colors, and whether a driveway gate is allowed at all. Get written HOA approval before you build, because an HOA can require a compliant gate to come out even if the city is fine with it.

If you take one thing from this section, make it this: **your gate is a permitted structure in most of this county, and rules change from city to city.** We check the rules for your address before we quote, and you should never build a gate on the assumption that what worked two streets over will work on your lot.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How wide should a fence gate be?**
For a walk-through gate, 36 to 42 inches is the standard, and 42 to 48 inches if you need to move a wheelbarrow, garden cart, or garbage can through. For a riding mower, plan on 60 inches (5 feet). For vehicles, single drive gates run about 10 to 12 feet and double drive gates about 12 to 16 feet. Always size the clear opening, not just the gate leaf, and leave room for the swing.

**What are the pool fence gate requirements in Michigan?**
Michigan applies the residential pool-barrier rules through the Michigan Residential Code (based on the 2015 IRC) and IRC Appendix G, which is state code and preempts local amendments. The barrier must be at least 48 inches tall, with no opening a 4-inch sphere can pass through. Pool gates must open outward away from the pool and be self-closing and self-latching. The latch release must be at least 54 inches above grade, or on the pool side at least 3 inches below the top with no opening larger than half an inch within 18 inches of the latch.

**Why does my wooden fence gate sag?**
Almost always one of four causes: a failing or rusting hinge, a post that has heaved or leaned, a frame with no diagonal brace that has racked out of square, or a gate leaf that's simply too heavy for its posts and hardware. Diagnose the cause before you fix it — tightening a hinge won't help if the post underneath it has moved.

**How do I fix a sagging gate?**
Check and tighten or replace the hinges, square the frame by comparing its diagonals, and add an anti-sag kit (a cable and turnbuckle running corner to corner) or a diagonal brace if the frame is flexing. If the post is leaning or heaved, the real fix is the post — which is why proper footing depth at install time matters so much.

**How deep should a gate post be set?**
Deeper than a fence post, because it carries the gate's full weight and swing load. Michigan exterior footings extend roughly three and a half feet below grade for buildings, and fence posts here are commonly set at least 36 inches in concrete — gate posts should go deeper, often 42 to 48 inches, fully below the frost line and in a sized concrete footing, so freeze-thaw can't heave them out of line.

**Do I need a permit for a fence gate in Oakland County?**
It depends on your city. Many communities require a fence permit or zoning approval, and the gate is usually covered by that permit. Some cities, like Troy, also trigger a building permit for fences 7 feet and taller and for all commercial fence and gate projects. We verify the rules for your specific address before quoting.

**What's the best gate material for a Michigan fence?**
Match the gate to the fence so it looks right, then build the frame to handle the load. Cedar is the classic wood choice for its rot resistance and light weight; aluminum makes the longest-lived, lightest gate; pressure-treated pine and vinyl are strong options when framed and hung correctly.

**Can a gate open onto a sidewalk or driveway?**
It can, but you have to plan the swing. Check the swing arc against the fence, grade, trees, and downspouts, and on a corner lot respect the city's visibility-triangle rule.
