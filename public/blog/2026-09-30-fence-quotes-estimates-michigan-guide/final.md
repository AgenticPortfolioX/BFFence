# How to Read a Fence Quote in Michigan: Estimates vs. Quotes, What Drives the Price, and How to Compare Bids Without Getting Burned
*Published: September 30, 2026 | By BF Fence*
---
Here is the uncomfortable part of shopping for a fence: the number at the bottom of the page is the least reliable thing on it.

Two quotes for the same backyard can land $1,500 apart, and the higher one can be the honest one. A quote can say "$6,400 for a cedar privacy fence" and mean eleven different jobs depending on post depth, board thickness, gate hardware, and whether anyone hauls away the old fence. It can look tidy and leave out the permit, the concrete, and the locates. Or it can be the lowest of the three and quietly skip the one thing Michigan will punish you for — a footing set above the frost line.

So comparing totals first is the wrong move. You compare scope, then price — against a written checklist. This is that checklist: what "estimate" and "quote" actually promise, what Michigan law requires a fence contract to contain, every line item a complete bid should name, what drives the price per linear foot, the three shortcuts that make a low bid low, and how to sit down with three pieces of paper and pick the right one.

If you're in Oakland County and you want a number you can trust, it starts with understanding what you're reading. Let's read it.

## Why Two Honest Quotes for the Same Fence Don't Match

Start with a truth that saves a lot of frustration: legitimately, honestly, two good contractors can hand you very different prices for what sounds like the same fence.

A fence is not a product with a sticker. It is a project, and a project is a stack of decisions: How deep are the posts? What grade of wood, and which species? Board-on-board or stockade? How many gates, and how wide? Does the old fence come out, and does the dirt go somewhere? Is the ground level, or does it slope and need stepping? Every one of those questions moves the price, and two contractors who price them differently are both telling the truth — about different jobs.

That is the trap. When you compare "$5,900" to "$7,200," you are not comparing two prices for the same fence. You are comparing two scopes that happen to share a yard. The $5,900 bid may assume a haul-away you're doing yourself, exclude the gate, and set posts shallower than Michigan wants. The $7,200 bid may include the removal, the gate, the permit, and footings dug below the frost line. Same yard. Different fences. One number is bigger and one is a better deal.

So the first rule of reading a fence quote in Michigan is this: **the total is the conclusion, not the evidence.** Read the scope first. The number only means something once you know what it covers.

## Estimate vs. Quote vs. Bid vs. Proposal: What Each Word Promises

Contractors use these words loosely in conversation, and homeowners reasonably assume they all mean "the price." They don't. In construction, they mean different things, and one of them can bind a contractor while another can't.

An **estimate** is an educated, nonbinding projection — a planning number. It is meant to help you budget, and a well-prepared estimate states its assumptions and its accuracy range, typically landing within roughly 10 to 25 percent of the final price when those assumptions hold. A ballpark estimate that names no assumptions is not an estimate you can rely on; it is a guess with a dollar sign.

A **quote** is a fixed, client-facing price tied to a defined scope. Once you accept it — usually by signing — it generally functions as a binding price agreement. Because material prices move, most contractors put an expiration on a quote, commonly around 30 days, so the date at the top matters as much as the number.

A **proposal** bundles scope, qualifications, approach, and price together, common on larger or commercial work. A **bid** is a competitive price submission — the word you hear in formal procurement where several contractors price the same defined scope.

For a residential fence, you'll usually meet two of these: a preliminary estimate after the first visit, and a formal quote once measurements and material are confirmed. The practical takeaway: if a contractor calls it an estimate but it's the only number you ever get in writing, treat it as an estimate and ask for a quote with a defined scope and a validity date before you sign anything. And in Michigan, there's a legal floor under all of this that most homeowners have never heard of.

## What Michigan Law Actually Requires in a Fence Contract

Michigan does not leave this to a handshake, and the rules are specific enough to use as your checklist.

First, the **$600 licensing line.** Under Michigan's Residential Builders and Maintenance & Alteration Contractors act (Public Act 299 of 1980, Article 24), a contractor doing work totaling **$600 or more in labor and materials must be licensed by the Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs (LARA)** — the threshold is written into the law at MCL 339.2403. A real fence project clears that line without trying, so the company building your fence should hold a residential builder or maintenance-and-alteration license and be able to show it. You can verify a license through LARA, or ask to see the license and the pocket card licensed contractors are required to carry.

Second, the **written-contract rule.** Michigan's Home Improvement Finance Act (MCL 445.1101 and following) requires that a home-improvement contract **above $600 be in writing and signed by both parties**, and include the contractor's **name and license number**, a **description of the work**, the **total price**, and a **payment schedule**. Here is the part that should get every homeowner's attention: a home-improvement contract over $600 that is **not put in writing is not enforceable by the contractor** under MCL 445.1108. In plain terms, the law rewards the homeowner who insists on paper.

Third, your **right to cancel.** If a contractor solicited you and you signed a home-improvement contract in your home, Michigan gives you a **three-business-day right to rescind** under MCL 445.1116. High-pressure, in-home pitches are where most of the trouble starts.

Fourth, the **contents the state tells you to demand.** Michigan's own homeowner guidance lists what a complete home-improvement contract should contain: the company's **name, address, phone, and license number**; **detailed job specifications** (brand names, colors, grades, styles, and model numbers — not "cedar fence," but *which* cedar, *what* grade); **a list of all costs spelled out clearly**; **start and completion dates**; a statement that **all required permits and variances will be obtained by the contractor** before work begins; a **provision for credits** if large amounts of material are left over; **clearly stated payment terms**; a **right-to-cancel clause**; and **both signatures and the date**, with any changes initialed.

Notice how much of that is about *transparency*, not price. That is the point. A contractor who follows the state's own guidance is one whose quote you can actually read. When a bid arrives as a single line — "fence, $6,800" — it fails the state's standard before you even get to the arithmetic.

## The Line-Item Checklist: What Every Written Fence Quote Should Name

Now take the state's requirements and make them fence-specific. A complete, honest fence quote is not a paragraph and a number. It is a short specification document, and it should name each of these:

- **Material and species.** Which wood, and what grade. "Cedar" is not specific enough — Western red cedar is a different board than the pine some companies quietly substitute. Get species, grade, and board dimension in writing.
- **Height and total linear feet.** The height of the fence and the measured footage. This is the number the price per linear foot rests on, and it should be a real measurement of your yard, not a rounded guess.
- **Post size, spacing, and footing depth.** This is where quotes hide. Ask for the post dimension, the on-center spacing, and the footing depth. The technical standard: posts set at least **one-third of their total length below grade** — for a six-foot fence on an eight-foot post, 24 to 30 inches minimum — and in Michigan, frost depth governs and pushes footings deeper still, toward the state's roughly 42- to 48-inch frost line. A quote that says nothing about footing depth is telling you something.
- **Concrete.** Is concrete specified, and roughly how much per hole? Posts set in concrete require **48 to 72 hours of cure** before the fence is loaded, which is why a contractor who hangs panels the same day they pour is one to walk away from.
- **Gates.** How many, what widths, and what hardware — hinges, latch, and whether gate posts are set in larger holes (they should be). Gate hardware is a frequent "we'll figure it out later" item that becomes an added cost.
- **Corners, ends, and terminal posts.** Every corner and end needs a post that carries structural load. Count them.
- **Old-fence removal and haul-away.** Two items that get quietly merged into "included" or quietly dropped. If the old fence is coming out, the quote should say so — and say what happens to it.
- **Permits.** Who pulls them. The answer should be the contractor: the permit is what puts an inspection and the correction responsibility on the builder's side.
- **Utility locates.** The quote should state that 811 will be called before any digging — it's the law in Michigan, and it's what keeps an auger out of a gas line.
- **Property lines and setbacks.** Who confirms where the line is, and how setbacks and easements are handled. Get it in writing, because the costliest surprise in fencing is a fence that has to move.
- **Cleanup and site restoration.** What the yard looks like when the crew leaves, and who repairs the grass.

If you can check those boxes, you are holding a document you can actually compare. If half are missing, you are not comparing quotes — you're comparing guesswork, and the low number is low for a reason you can't yet see.

## What Actually Drives the Price Per Linear Foot

Once the scope is clear, the price stops looking arbitrary. Fence pricing is not a secret formula; it is a small set of real inputs. Understand them and you can sanity-check any quote in seconds.

- **Material.** The largest variable. Cedar commands more than treated pine; vinyl, aluminum, and ornamental steel each carry their own bands.
- **Height.** An eight-foot privacy fence uses more material and takes more wind load than a six-footer, which changes post sizing. Height is a structural decision, not just a look.
- **Total footage.** Price scales with length, but not perfectly — long runs move faster per foot than short, fussy ones.
- **Gates.** Each gate adds hardware, a reinforced frame, and often a larger footing. Gates are among the most under-estimated line items in residential fencing.
- **Corners and end posts.** Every corner and end is extra structure. A yard with many turns costs more than a straight run of the same footage.
- **Slope and terrain.** A sloped yard has to be stepped, racked, or contoured, which changes post counts and labor. This is where two quotes for "the same yard" legitimately diverge.
- **Soil, roots, and rock.** Rocky or root-choked ground slows digging and can require different tools. A contractor who has walked your line will price it; one who priced from a satellite photo may not.
- **Access.** A yard a truck and equipment can't reach costs more to build.
- **Old-fence removal and staining.** Tear-out, disposal, and haul-away carry real dump fees, and staining is often optional and quoted separately. Decide what's in and what's out before comparing.

When a quote itemizes these, you can see why it costs what it costs. When it doesn't, the number is doing work you can't audit.

## What a Fair Price Looks Like in Michigan Right Now

Reference bands are useful as a sanity check and dangerous as a promise, so take these as the first word, not the last. For Michigan in 2026, a 150-linear-foot wood privacy fence runs a state average around **$3,570**, with a range of roughly **$2,678 to $4,998** — about **$33 per linear foot** for wood at six feet, chain-link around **$18**, vinyl around **$41**, aluminum around **$46**, and steel or ornamental iron around **$56**. ## The Three Low-Bid Shortcuts (and What They Cost You Later)

When a bid is dramatically lower than the others, the difference almost always hides in one of three places. Learn to look.

**Shortcut one: shallow posts.** The footing is the one part of a fence you never see and the part most expensive to fix. A bid that sets posts shallow — above the frost line, or without adequate concrete — saves the contractor a little digging and costs you a fence that heaves, leans, and needs repair after a couple of Michigan winters. If you've seen a fence line go wavy in spring, you've seen shallow footings. (We wrote about that failure and what it costs to fix in [leaning fence posts in Michigan](/blog/2026-09-12-leaning-fence-posts-michigan-repair).)

**Shortcut two: thin or undersized material.** A cheaper grade of board, a thinner picket, a lighter rail, a smaller post. It looks similar on day one and ages faster, sags sooner, and takes wind worse. Vague material descriptions are how this hides.

**Shortcut three: missing scope that becomes a change order.** The bid doesn't include removal, or the permit, or the haul-away, or the gate hardware — and none of it is free once the job starts. The "cheaper" quote quietly becomes the expensive one, at the moment when you have the least leverage. It is entirely avoidable if you compare scope, not totals.

None of this means the low bid is always wrong. A low bid that names every item and still comes in low — on a simple, flat, accessible job — can be a perfectly good bid from a hungry, efficient company. The test is never "is it low." The test is "is it complete."

## Why the Cheapest Bid Is Usually Not the Cheapest Fence

A fence is not a one-time purchase. It is a structure you live with for 15 to 30 years, and the number that matters is not the day-one price — it is the lifetime cost. A fence built with proper footings, real material, and complete scope stays put; one built to the lowest number generates its own bills — a post that heaves, a gate that sags, a section that rots early. Add those up and the "cheaper" fence usually isn't.

There's also the resale angle: a well-built, maintained wood fence is an asset, and a failing one is a liability a buyer prices into an offer. Built to last is not a slogan in Michigan — it is the difference between an investment and an expense. So when you compare bids, ask the second-order questions. Which bid will still look right in ten winters? Which contractor will still answer the phone? The cheapest fence and the best value are rarely the same fence.

## How to Compare Three Bids Apples-to-Apples

Here is the method, in order.

**Step one: get three written quotes.** Three is the practical minimum; four or five gives you a clearer market picture. Get them *written* — under Michigan's Home Improvement Finance Act, a fence contract over $600 belongs in writing anyway.

**Step two: normalize the scope.** Before you look at a single total, build a comparison table with each item as a row and each contractor as a column, and fill in what each bid actually says. Mark the blanks honestly — a blank is not "included." A blank is unknown.

| Line item | What to record for each bid |
|---|---|
| Material, species, grade | Species and grade named? |
| Height | Feet, stated? |
| Total linear feet | Measured footage? |
| Post size and spacing | Dimension and on-center spacing? |
| Footing depth | Inches, and below frost line? |
| Concrete and cure | Concrete specified? Cure noted? |
| Gates | Count, widths, hardware? |
| Corners / end posts | Counted? |
| Old-fence removal | In or out? |
| Haul-away / disposal | In or out? |
| Permits | Contractor pulls? |
| 811 utility locates | Stated? |
| Property line / setback | Responsibility stated? |
| Cleanup / restoration | Stated? |
| Warranty | Material + workmanship, length? |
| Quote type and validity | Estimate or quote? Expires when? |

**Step three: price the gaps.** If one bid omits removal and another includes it, don't compare their totals — compare their scope. The bid that "wins" on price may be winning by leaving out work you'll pay for anyway.

**Step four: look for the outlier, not the average.** The middle of three bids is not automatically right; what matters is *why* a bid sits apart. A bid 30 to 40 percent below the others is either cutting something or misunderstanding the job. A bid well above should justify itself with scope, material, warranty, or reputation — or it's just expensive.

**Step five: check the contractor, not just the paper.** Verify the license. Ask to see the certificate of insurance — general liability and workers' compensation — because if a crew member is injured on your property and the contractor isn't covered, that exposure can come back to you. Ask for photos of recent, similar work and references from those jobs.

**Step six: read the contract against Michigan's checklist.** Name, address, phone, and license number present? Detailed specifications? Costs broken out? Start and completion dates? Permits on the contractor? Right to cancel? Payment schedule? Signatures and date? If any of it is missing, ask before you sign — a good contractor won't mind.

Do that, and you'll be choosing between three known quantities instead of three mysterious numbers.

## Deposits and Payments: What's Normal, What's a Warning

Money is where the guidance is most concrete. Michigan's Attorney General publishes plain rules for home-repair payments, and they apply directly to fencing.

- **A deposit should not exceed one-third of the total project cost.** That is the state's own guidance, not a contractor preference. A demand for half, or the full amount up front, is a warning — and "100 percent up front" is a hard stop.
- **Schedule payments as the work progresses.** Payment should track the job, not run ahead of it; a schedule that pays most of the money before most of the work is done hands your leverage away.
- **Do not pay in cash.** Cash leaves no record, which is exactly why cash-only contractors prefer it. Pay by check or card.
- **Hold the final payment until the job is done to your satisfaction — and until you have proof that subcontractors and suppliers are paid.** That protects you from a lien surprise where a supplier who was never paid comes looking to you.

These four rules cost nothing to follow and eliminate the most common way a fence project goes wrong.

## Red Flags in a Fence Quote: The Attorney General's List, Applied to Fencing

Michigan's consumer-protection guidance names the warning signs of a bad home-improvement contractor. Read them with a fence in mind:

- **High-pressure urgency or a limited-time offer.** A real fence company doesn't need you to decide tonight.
- **Cash-only payment.** No record, no recourse.
- **Door-to-door solicitation**, especially right after a storm. Storm-chasers install fast and cheap and vanish.
- **"We have extra material from a nearby job."** A classic opener — and a sign the price is being invented on your porch.
- **Work must start "now."** Legitimate contractors are usually booked weeks out.
- **Payment in advance** beyond a normal deposit.
- **A contractor who leaves the job to "get more help"** and doesn't come back on schedule.
- **A small job pitched, then upsold** into substantially more work.
- **Out-of-state plates after a storm**, or a claim of being "FEMA-approved." FEMA does not approve contractors.
- **A "free inspection" that conveniently discovers a serious problem.**
- **A contractor who asks you to pull the permit.** Permits belong to the contractor.
- **A price with no written scope**, or blank spaces you're rushed to sign.

One more, specific to fencing: **no mention of footing depth, concrete, or 811.** Those three absences are the quiet signatures of a bid that plans to cut the parts you can't see.

## Ten Questions to Ask Before You Sign

Bring these to every quote review. The answers tell you more than the totals do.

1. **Are you licensed in Michigan, and what's your license number?** Confirm it with LARA.
2. **Can I see your certificate of insurance** — general liability and workers' compensation?
3. **How deep will the posts be set, and how much concrete per hole?** Look for a frost-line answer, not a generic one.
4. **What species and grade of wood**, down to brand or mill if it matters?
5. **How many gates, what widths, and what hardware?**
6. **Who pulls the permit?** The answer should be the contractor.
7. **Will you call 811 before digging?** The only good answer is yes.
8. **What's your payment schedule?** Expect a deposit no greater than one-third, progress payments, and a final payment on completion.
9. **What does your warranty cover, and for how long** — labor as well as material?
10. **What's not included?** Ask directly. The gaps a contractor names out loud are the ones you'll never be surprised by.

If a contractor answers all ten clearly and in writing, you have found someone worth doing business with. If they can't, that is your answer, and it arrived before you spent a dollar.

## What a Real Workmanship Warranty Looks Like

A fence quote should carry two warranties, and homeowners often conflate them.

The **material warranty** comes from the manufacturer — the mill or the vinyl or metal supplier — and covers defects in the product itself. Read its length and limits; material warranties rarely cover installation problems.

The **workmanship warranty** comes from the contractor, and it speaks to how the fence was built. Professional fence companies typically offer one to five years on installation, covering failures that come from how the fence was set: posts that shift, gates that sag, panels that loosen. A contractor who stands behind their work will name a number and put it in writing.

Ask the follow-up: *What happens if a post shifts two winters from now?* The right answer is "we come back and fix it, at no charge, inside the warranty." The wrong answer is "that's just settling." In Michigan, with our freeze-thaw cycles, a post that shifts is usually a footing decision, not weather.

## Timing: Why Fall Bids Land Differently in Michigan

There is a seasonal dimension to fence pricing most homeowners never consider, and it is relevant right now.

Michigan's frost line is the reason. To keep a fence from heaving, footings have to sit below the frost depth — roughly 42 to 48 inches across the state, deepest in the north. Digging those holes is straightforward in open ground and much slower and costlier once the ground locks up and you're breaking through frost. In Oakland County, the practical window runs from late September through first freeze — typically late October into mid-November.

That has two effects on a quote. Material demand eases after the summer rush, so scheduling is smoother. And a fall-built fence gets its footings into good ground before winter, which is exactly the condition it needs to survive its first freeze-thaw cycles. All else equal, a fall bid for a fall install lands more comfortably than the same job waiting for spring — and a crew that can start in a couple of weeks in the fall, because the calendar opened up, is worth a conversation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much should a fence deposit be in Michigan?

The Michigan Attorney General's consumer guidance says a deposit should not be more than one-third of the total project cost. A one-third deposit at signing, with the balance tied to progress and a final payment on completion, is the norm for a legitimate Michigan fence contractor. Anything approaching half or more up front — or a demand for cash — should stop the conversation.

### Is a fence quote legally binding?

It depends on the wording, the signatures, and how both sides treat the document. A signed, itemized quote with a defined scope generally functions as an enforceable contract; a ballpark estimate usually does not. In Michigan, a home-improvement contract over $600 that is not put in writing is not enforceable by the contractor under the Home Improvement Finance Act (MCL 445.1108).

### Do I need a licensed fence contractor to build a fence in Michigan?

If the job costs $600 or more in labor and materials, yes. Michigan law (MCL 339.2403) requires a LARA-issued residential builder or maintenance-and-alteration license above that threshold. You can verify a license with LARA, or ask to see the contractor's license and the pocket card licensed contractors are required to carry.

### What should a written fence quote include?

At minimum: material species and grade, height, total linear feet, post size and spacing, footing depth and concrete, gate count, width, and hardware, corner and end-post treatment, old-fence removal, haul-away, permits, 811 utility locates, property-line responsibility, cleanup, and warranty. Under Michigan's Home Improvement Finance Act, contracts over $600 must also include the contractor's name and license number, a description of the work, the total price, and a payment schedule — in writing and signed by both parties.

### How many fence quotes should I get?

Three written quotes is the practical minimum, and four or five gives you a clearer market range. The goal is not to average them — it's to spot the outlier. If one bid is 30 to 40 percent below the rest, ask exactly what it leaves out before you assume it's a deal.

### Is the cheapest fence quote the best value?

Usually not. The lowest number is often low because it omits scope — removal, haul-away, permits, concrete, or gate hardware — or because it cuts what you can't see: post depth, material grade, or footing size. The real comparison is the full scope over the fence's full lifetime, not the total at the bottom of the page.

### How do I know if a fence quote is too low?

A quote that sits well below the others and stays vague on specifics is the warning sign. Look for shallow post depths, no concrete specified, thin or undersized material, no mention of 811 locates or property lines, and a price with no written scope. A low bid that names everything and still comes in low is a legitimate bid; a low bid that names nothing is a shortcut you'll pay for later.

### Does the homeowner or the contractor pull the fence permit?

The contractor should. Michigan's consumer guidance specifically lists "contractor asks the homeowner to pull the permit" as a red flag. A licensed, insured contractor pulls the permit because it keeps the inspection and the correction responsibility on their side of the table. If the quote doesn't say who handles permits, ask before you sign.

### What warranty should a fence quote include?

Two warranties: a manufacturer's warranty on the material and a workmanship warranty from the contractor on the installation. Professional fence companies typically offer one to five years on workmanship, covering posts that shift, gates that sag, and panels that come loose. Get both the length and the coverage in writing before you sign.

### Is it cheaper to build a fence in the fall in Michigan?

Often it's smoother, and sometimes it's cheaper. Late September through first freeze is a strong digging window before the ground locks up, material demand eases after the summer rush, and crews can schedule without the spring backlog. Given Michigan's frost line, getting footings into good ground before winter is a real advantage for how the fence performs through its first freeze-thaw cycles.

A fence quote is a document you are supposed to be able to read, and in Michigan the law says as much. The number at the bottom is the last thing to look at, not the first. Compare scope.
