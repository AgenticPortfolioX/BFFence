# A Tree Fell on Your Fence: Who Pays, What Insurance Covers, and What the Repair Actually Costs in Oakland County
*Published: November 11, 2026 | By BFFence*

---

The November storms here do a specific kind of damage. Wet snow lands on trees that are still holding their leaves, the canopy catches the weight, and a limb the size of a canoe comes down across a fence line that has stood quietly for fifteen years.

What follows is not really a fence problem. It is three questions, and homeowners ask them in the same order every time we get called out.

Who is responsible for this? Will insurance pay for it? And what is it actually going to cost me?

The honest answers are more specific than most of what you will find online, because Michigan answers all three with statutes and case law, and Oakland County answers the last one with real numbers. So let's take them in order.

One note before we start: this is general information about how Michigan law and Michigan policies have handled these claims — not legal advice and not insurance advice. If you are in an active dispute with a neighbor, or a claim has been denied, a Michigan property attorney is the right call. Everything else here is what we see on jobs, with the citations attached so you can check us.

## The First Hour: What to Do Before You Move Anything

Before the insurance call and before the tree service, do four things in this sequence. They take about twenty minutes and they protect the claim.

**1. Make sure the scene is safe.** Assume any limb under tension is loaded like a spring — a branch pinned under the trunk can release when you cut the wrong end. If a limb is resting on a power line, or the line is down anywhere on your property, stop and call DTE or Consumers before anyone touches that fence. No fence is worth a burn.

**2. Photograph everything before you move anything.** Adjusters can tell fresh storm damage from old rot, but only if there is evidence. Take six specific shots: a wide shot showing the whole damaged run and its relation to the house, the point where the tree crossed the fence line, the broken posts at the ground line, the panel or rail that failed, the tree's stump or the point where it snapped, and a dated close-up of any damage that looks like it predates the storm. Then walk the rest of the fence — most storms damage more than the obvious section.

**3. Keep the debris where it is until you have spoken to your insurer.** Hauling the limb to the curb feels productive and destroys your evidence. If a limb is blocking a driveway, move it, but photograph it in place first.

**4. Stop the damage from spreading.** If a bay is hanging by one rail, brace it. If a gate will not latch, tie it. If a post is cracked at grade and leaning toward a neighbor's yard, do not leave it. Michigan policies expect you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage, and those steps are reimbursable — keep the receipts.

Only then call the insurer, and call us for an estimate in the same week. The estimate is what tells you whether filing a claim makes any sense at all, which is a question we will get to.

## Who Pays When a Tree Takes Out a Fence in Michigan

Here is the short version, and it surprises almost everyone: **in Michigan, a healthy tree that comes down in a storm is nobody's fault.** The law treats it as an act of God. The tree's owner is not automatically liable for your fence, and you are not automatically liable for theirs. Each of you files with your own insurance company for the damage on your own side of the line.

That is the default, and it is the answer in the majority of the calls we get. But the default has real exceptions, and the exceptions all turn on one fact: **what the tree's owner knew before the storm.**

The controlling Michigan case is *Stevens v. Drekich*, 178 Mich. App. 273 (1989), where the Court of Appeals found no liability for a tree that had shown no prior signs of decay or danger. No notice of a problem, no duty to prevent the harm. Michigan applies ordinary negligence principles to falling trees, which means the whole fight becomes a fight about documentation: did the owner know, or should they reasonably have known, that the tree was a hazard?

| What happened | Who is generally on the hook | What decides it |
|---|---|---|
| Healthy tree, storm failure, lands on your fence | You, through your own policy | No negligence, so no liability on the tree's owner |
| Visibly hazardous tree the owner knew about | The tree's owner, potentially | Prior knowledge of dead limbs, trunk cracks, fungal growth at the base, a lean that got worse |
| You warned the owner in writing and nothing was done | The tree's owner, more strongly | A dated email or letter converts "nobody could have known" into documented notice |
| Tree's trunk sits on the boundary line | You and your neighbor, jointly | Both owners have an interest; neither can act alone |
| Tree falls and hits nothing | Whoever owns the trunk | Cleanup is the owner's problem, and insurance usually will not pay for it |

### When the tree owner is on the hook

The warning signs that establish knowledge are not subtle. Large dead limbs in the canopy, a trunk with visible cracks or hollow sections, fungal conifers at the base, a lean that has visibly progressed, or a tree that has already dropped a major limb are all things a reasonable owner should act on. If those signs were there and nothing was done, the neighbor's liability exposure is real.

Documentation wins these disputes. If a neighbor's tree worries you, say so in an email and keep a dated photo with it. If a neighbor has raised concerns about your tree, get a written assessment from a certified arborist — they typically run $75 to $500 — because a report that says the tree is sound is your best defense, and a report that recommends removal that you then ignore becomes the strongest evidence against you.

### Boundary trees, overhanging limbs, and the treble-damages line

Two more rules resolve most of the arguing that happens over a fence line.

**If the trunk sits on the line, the tree is jointly owned.** The Michigan Supreme Court held in *Lennon v. Terrall*, 250 Mich. 100 (1932), that neither owner may cut down, injure, or destroy a boundary tree without the other's consent. Both share the maintenance obligation. Removing a boundary tree unilaterally is one of the most expensive mistakes available in this area of law.

**Branches over your side are yours to trim, within limits.** Michigan common law lets you cut limbs and roots that cross onto your property, without permission and without a lawsuit. You may only cut up to the line — reaching past it is trespass — and you may not cut so aggressively that you kill the tree. Under MCL 600.2919, anyone who cuts down, injures, or destroys trees on another person's land without permission is liable for **three times** the actual damages. Willful destruction also carries criminal exposure under MCL 750.382, scaling from a 93-day misdemeanor to a 10-year felony depending on the value destroyed.

This is why we tell homeowners to hire an arborist before they take a saw to a mature tree that leans over a fence. The trimming you save $400 on can cost you triple the tree's value.

### The partition-fence route most homeowners never hear about

Michigan has a statute for exactly this situation, and almost nobody knows it exists. The Line Fence Act, Act 34 of 1978 (MCL 43.51 through 43.60), sets up a local process for partition fences — the fence that separates two adjoining properties — including a fence-viewer mechanism for when neighbors cannot agree.

Under MCL 43.55, a fence viewer engaged on a partition-fence question can determine whether one owner is using a fence the other built and maintained, and if so, **what percentage of the cost of construction and maintenance that owner is responsible for.** The viewer issues a decision, and there is an appeal path.

Two honest limits. First, this is not a universal "your neighbor owes half" rule — it depends on the statutory conditions, the use of the land, and the process followed, and it fits agricultural and livestock situations more cleanly than it fits a decorative backyard privacy fence. Second, it does not establish where your boundary actually is; that takes a survey, which we covered in our guide to [property lines, surveys, and fence viewers](/blog/2026-10-03-fence-property-lines-surveys-michigan).

But if a neighbor is refusing to contribute to a shared fence that their side of the yard benefits from, the township clerk can tell you how to request a fence viewer, and that is a far better path than a shouting match over the property line.

If talking fails and the money at stake is under $7,000, Michigan's small claims court handles it — that ceiling went into effect January 1, 2024 under MCL 600.8401. Above that, or if you need an order, it goes to circuit court, and now you are paying a lawyer more than the fence is worth. For most residential fences, a written agreement before the work starts is the cheapest insurance there is.

## What Homeowners Insurance Actually Pays for a Fence

Your fence is not covered under your dwelling coverage. It is covered under **Coverage B — other structures**, the same section that covers a detached garage or a shed. On a standard HO-3 policy, that limit is commonly about **10% of your dwelling limit**, so a $300,000 house carries roughly $30,000 of other-structures coverage. Some policies are written at higher percentages; read your declarations page, because this is the ceiling on what your fence can ever collect.

Then comes the part that decides whether you get a check at all: what caused the damage.

| Cause of damage | Typically covered? | What to know |
|---|---|---|
| Windstorm, including a fence blown flat | Usually yes | The fence must have been in reasonable condition before the event; some Michigan policies carry a separate percentage wind/hail deductible |
| Hail, lightning, fire | Usually yes | Same condition test applies |
| A healthy tree falling on the fence | Usually yes | Act of God, no negligence — you claim on your own policy |
| A neglected or diseased tree the owner knew about | Depends | Your insurer may pursue the neighbor's liability coverage |
| Vehicle impact | Often yes | The driver's auto liability may also respond |
| Vandalism | Usually yes | File a police report; adjusters ask for it |
| Rot, termites, rust, wear and tear, neglect | No | Maintenance is yours, not an insured event |
| Flood, surface water, earth movement | No | Requires separate coverage; standard policies exclude it |

Two sublimits catch people. **Debris removal** is usually capped separately — a common structure is $500 per tree and $1,000 per incident — so a crew quoting $2,400 to cut up and haul a mature maple may leave you covering most of it. And a tree that falls in the yard without hitting a covered structure is often not covered at all, which means the limb that missed your fence by two feet is a straight out-of-pocket cleanup.

### Actual cash value: where the money disappears

This is the part that turns a covered claim into a disappointing check, and no insurance explainer will do the arithmetic for you, so let's do it.

Most wood fences are settled on **actual cash value (ACV)**, not replacement cost. ACV is replacement cost minus depreciation for age and condition. Cedar privacy fencing carries a useful life in the range of 20 to 30 years with maintenance; adjusters commonly work from the lower half of that range on a wood fence that has not been maintained.

Take a real example: a 176-foot cedar privacy fence built in 2011, damaged by a windstorm in November 2026. At today's Oakland County pricing it costs about $9,900 to rebuild — that is the number from our own [Troy replacement](/blog/2026-11-07-troy-pine-fence-rebuild-cedar-project-spotlight), where a 176-foot board-on-board cedar rebuild came in at $9,977 all-in, or $56.69 per linear foot.

Now run the claim:

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Replacement cost of the damaged run | $9,900 |
| Age at loss | 15 years |
| Useful life applied by the adjuster | 25 years |
| Depreciation | 60% |
| Actual cash value (ACV) | $3,960 |
| Policy deductible | $2,500 |
| **Check you can expect on an ACV policy** | **$1,460** |
| What you actually pay the contractor | $9,900 |
| **Your out-of-pocket gap** | **$8,440** |

That is the reality of an ACV settlement on a fifteen-year-old wood fence, and it is why so many homeowners conclude the claim was not worth filing. If your policy carries replacement cost on other structures, or if the fence is scheduled, the math improves dramatically — recoverable depreciation is released once the work is completed, and the payout moves toward replacement cost less your deductible. American Family, Progressive, and Travelers all note the same thing in their consumer guidance: wood fences are frequently eligible for ACV only, while scheduling a structure can unlock replacement cost. If your fence matters to you, that is a fifteen-minute conversation with your agent worth having before the storm, not after.

One more subtraction to watch: **betterment**. When new boards go onto an old fence, an adjuster may argue the repair leaves you better off than you were, and hold back part of the cost. On a partial repair of a weathered run, that argument has teeth. It is also negotiable — which is what the next section is about.

### Should you file at all?

Run this arithmetic before you call in a claim:

- **Repair cost well below your deductible:** do not file. You will pay for the estimate and get nothing.
- **Repair cost close to your deductible:** talk to your agent before filing. A claim that nets you a few hundred dollars still goes on your loss history, and insurers share that history. This is not a decision to make on the phone in the driveway.
- **Repair cost several times the deductible:** file, and file promptly, because prompt notice is a condition of coverage.

The estimate comes first in every one of those scenarios. You cannot make this decision without a number, and a contractor's written estimate is the number your adjuster will compare against anyway.

## The Michigan Claim Timeline, With the Statute Behind Each Deadline

Michigan puts hard clocks on the claims process, and they cut both ways — some bind the insurer, some bind you.

| Step | Who | Timing | Authority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Give prompt notice of loss | You | Immediately; no fixed day count, but delay that prejudices the insurer is a coverage problem | Policy language; MCL 500.2833(1)(l) requires the policy to state it |
| Insurer specifies in writing what proof of loss it needs | Insurer | Within 30 days of receiving your claim | MCL 500.2006(3) |
| Signed, sworn proof of loss | You | 60 days after the insurer requests it (ISO HO-3 form) | Policy conditions |
| Payment of the claim | Insurer | Within 60 days of satisfactory proof, after which 12% simple interest accrues on the overdue amount | MCL 500.2006(4) |
| Notice of intent to repair or replace | You | 180 days from the date of loss (ISO form) | Policy loss-settlement condition |
| Deadline to sue the insurer | You | Not less than 1 year from the loss, with the clock tolled from notice until a formal denial | MCL 500.2833(1)(q) |

If the insurer goes quiet, Michigan's Department of Insurance and Financial Services takes consumer complaints at 877-999-6442 and at michigan.gov/DIFScomplaints. DIFS also publishes a plain-language guide to homeowners claims that is worth ten minutes of your time — it is the same guidance we point customers to.

The practical takeaway: report the damage the week you find it, photograph before you clean up, respond to every request in writing, and put the first anniversary of the storm on your calendar.

## What Actually Breaks in a Storm: A Contractor's Walkthrough

Homeowners see the panel that is lying in the grass. That is rarely the whole scope, and the gap between what the insurance scope covers and what the fence actually needs is where homeowners lose money.

Here is what we check, and what each symptom means.

| What you see | What it usually means | Repair tier |
|---|---|---|
| A limb through one or two bays | Impact damage; boards and possibly one rail | Panel and rail replacement |
| Boards intact but the whole bay is skewed into a parallelogram | Racking — the bay moved on its fasteners and the posts are now out of plumb | Rail and fastener rebuild, possible post reset |
| Rails split at the pocket where they meet the post | Shear failure at the connection, common with aged pine rails | Rail replacement along the affected bays |
| Post cracked or soft at the grass line | Rot at grade, usually where the string trimmer has been hitting it for years, plus storm load | Post replacement |
| Concrete collar visible above grade, post lifted | Frost heave gripped the footing and pushed the assembly up | New footing set below frost depth |
| Gate will not latch, everything else straight | Gate post cantilever failure — a gate post carries a lever no line post does | Gate post reset or replacement, 6x6 upsizing |
| Fence looks fine but moves when you push it | Fastener withdrawal or a shallow footing that has not failed yet | Probe and reset before the next storm |

The ten-minute inspection any homeowner can do: walk the line and push every post hard at shoulder height. A post that moves is a post that will fail. Then probe each post with a screwdriver at the grass line — if the blade sinks in easily, the post is gone below grade no matter how good the panel looks. Then sight down the run from one end; the eye catches a lean that a photo hides.

That inspection is also what an honest contractor does before quoting, and it is why we do not give firm repair numbers over the phone for storm damage. The visible damage and the actual damage are different measurements, and the difference is usually the posts.

## Repair or Replace? The Threshold We Actually Use

After a storm, the question is never just "what is broken." It is whether the broken part is worth fixing on a fence that is nearing the end of its life.

We use four tests, and if a fence fails two of them, we tell the homeowner to put the money into a rebuild.

1. **The post test.** Probe every post. If more than roughly 30% of them are soft, loose, or leaning, the fence is structurally finished — the panels are just riding on failed foundations.
2. **The age test.** Pressure-treated pine in Michigan realistically gives 12 to 18 years. Cedar gives 20 to 30. A 20-year-old pine fence with storm damage is a fence you will be replacing in two years regardless.
3. **The racking test.** If multiple bays have gone out of square, the fasteners and rails have taken a set. Patching panels onto a racked frame buys you one more season.
4. **The matching test.** New cedar next to fifteen-year-old weathered cedar is a visible patch, and if you care how it looks from the kitchen window — which most people do, since the whole point is [privacy you can see](/blog/2026-11-07-troy-pine-fence-rebuild-cedar-project-spotlight) — a partial repair on an old run is often the worst of both worlds.

### When a partial repair is the right call

A four-year-old cedar fence with two bays down and sound posts is a repair, full stop. Rebuilding it would be a waste of money and we will say so.

The break-even is usually around the 40% mark: when the cost of repairing the damaged sections approaches 40% of what a new run would cost, and the fence is more than twelve years old, the arithmetic favors replacement. That is not a sales position. It is the same math the adjuster runs when they decide whether to total a fence.

## When We Tell You Not to Rebuild Yet

Not every damaged fence should be rebuilt in November, and pretending otherwise is how contractors lose the referrals that built their business.

**If the fence is 20-plus years old and only two bays are down**, patch it, get the yard secure for the winter, and plan the replacement for spring when the ground thaws and the crew is not fighting frost. You will spend $600 now and $9,000 later, and the $600 buys you a winter with a contained dog and a private yard.

**If insurance will pay for a full run and you have the coverage**, take the settlement and rebuild once. Patching a claim-covered fence and then replacing it in three years means paying two deductibles for one fence.

**If the fence is a shared boundary fence**, settle the cost-sharing question before the first board comes off. Once the work starts, the negotiation gets harder, and a written agreement — location, height, materials, who pays what, maintenance going forward — is worth more than any handshake. ## Protecting What Matters Before the Next Storm

Almost every failure we see after a storm was decided years earlier, at the footing.

Michigan's residential code calls for footings below the frost depth, and in this part of the state that means 42 inches. A post set 22 inches deep in Oakland County clay will heave, rotate, and eventually snap, and no amount of good cedar on top of it changes that. Our post-repair guide covers the [42-inch rule and why shallow posts always lose](/blog/2026-09-12-leaning-fence-posts-michigan-repair), and the storm-loading mechanics — 768 pounds of wind pressure on a single 8-foot bay at 80 mph — are in our breakdown of [why some fences fail while their neighbors stand](/blog/2026-10-24-storm-proof-wood-fence-michigan-wind-snow-load).

The short list for a fence that survives:

- **Footings below frost depth**, belled at the base, with gravel drainage and a crowned concrete collar so water runs away from the post.
- **Three rails, not two.** The middle rail is the difference between a fence that racks and a fence that holds.
- **Ring-shank nails or structural screws.** Smooth nails withdraw under reversing wind load — it is the single most common failure signature we find.
- **6x6 gate posts.** A gate post carries a lever arm no line post does.
- **Ground-line maintenance.** Keep soil and mulch off the post, keep the string trimmer away from it, and probe it once a year. Rot at grade is the number one post killer in this county.

A fence built to last is not a marketing phrase. It is a specific set of decisions, most of them underground, that determine whether you are picking up boards after the next November storm.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### If my neighbor's tree falls on my fence, who pays in Michigan?

Usually you do, through your own homeowners policy. Michigan treats a healthy tree that fails in a storm as an act of God, so the tree's owner is not automatically liable. If the tree was visibly hazardous and the owner knew — dead limbs, trunk cracks, a worsening lean — the owner may be liable, and written notice from you to them makes that case much stronger.

### Does homeowners insurance cover a fence blown down by wind?

Usually yes, under Coverage B, the other-structures section of your policy, typically limited to about 10% of your dwelling coverage. Wind, hail, fire, vandalism, vehicle impact, and a healthy tree falling on the fence are all commonly covered. Rot, termites, neglect, and flood are not.

### How much will insurance actually pay for a 15-year-old wood fence?

Less than you expect. Wood fences are usually settled at actual cash value, so the adjuster depreciates for age and condition before subtracting your deductible. On a $9,900 fence with 60% depreciation and a $2,500 deductible, the check is about $1,460 — which is why many homeowners with older fences choose to pay for the repair themselves.

### Should I file a claim for a fence repair?

Only if the repair costs meaningfully more than your deductible. If the estimate is below your deductible, filing pays you nothing and still puts a claim on your loss history. If it is several times the deductible, file promptly, because prompt notice is a condition of coverage.

### How long do I have to file a homeowners claim in Michigan?

There is no fixed day count for notice — the policy requires prompt notice, and delay that prejudices the insurer can jeopardize coverage. The harder deadlines are: the insurer must tell you in writing within 30 days what proof of loss it needs (MCL 500.2006), payment is due within 60 days of satisfactory proof or 12% interest accrues, and the deadline to sue is at least one year from the loss (MCL 500.2833).

### Can I trim branches from my neighbor's tree that hang over my fence?

Yes, up to the property line, under Michigan's self-help rule, and you pay for it yourself. You cannot reach past the line or step onto their property without permission, and you cannot cut so aggressively that you kill or seriously damage the tree. Cutting a neighbor's tree without permission exposes you to treble damages under MCL 600.2919.

### What if the tree trunk sits right on the property line?

Michigan treats it as jointly owned. Under *Lennon v. Terrall*, 250 Mich. 100 (1932), neither owner may cut, injure, or destroy a boundary tree without the other's consent, and maintenance is shared. Get any agreement in writing before a saw shows up.

### Do I need a permit to repair a storm-damaged fence in Oakland County?

Usually not for a like-for-like repair of a few damaged sections, but most cities do require a permit for a new or fully replaced run, and the rules differ by municipality. Check with your city's building department, and see our city-by-city fence rules guide for the height, setback, and permit requirements in your community.

### Is it cheaper to repair the damaged sections or replace the whole fence?

Repair wins on a fence under 12 years old with sound posts — replace the two damaged bays and move on. Replacement wins when more than about 30% of the posts are compromised, when the fence is beyond its service life, or when the repair cost approaches 40% of a new run. We measure posts before we quote either way.

## The Bottom Line

A fallen tree on a fence is a legal question, an insurance question, and a construction question, in that order. Michigan's default is that a healthy tree in a storm is nobody's fault, so the claim goes on your own policy, under Coverage B, settled at depreciated value. The exceptions all turn on what a neighbor knew and what you can prove about it.

The part you control is the estimate. Get one in writing, compare it to your deductible, and decide with numbers instead of adrenaline. And whether you repair or rebuild, insist on the parts that survive the next storm: footings 42 inches down, three rails, and fasteners that do not back out.

That is what protecting what matters actually looks like — and it is why our fences are built to last.
