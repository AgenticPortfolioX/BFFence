# Troy Fence Rebuild: Pulling a 22-Year-Old Treated Pine Fence and Rebuilding in Cedar Without Losing the Garden
*Published: November 7, 2026 | By BF Fence*

---

Every fence replacement starts with a homeowner standing in the backyard saying some version of the same sentence: *"I think it's got another year in it."*

Sometimes it does. On this job, in a Troy subdivision off Big Beaver, it did not — and the reason was not the pickets, which still looked fine from the kitchen window. It was what was happening fourteen inches below the grass, where twenty-two years of freeze-thaw had been quietly working on posts that were never set deep enough to begin with.

This is a walkthrough of that project: 176 linear feet of six-foot treated pine coming out, 23 concrete footings getting broken out of the ground, a mature perennial border that had to survive the whole thing, and 176 feet of board-on-board western redcedar going back in on the same line — corrected by fourteen inches. It is also the honest version of the money, because replacement work gets quoted badly in this market and homeowners deserve to see where the number comes from.

If your fence is leaning, if the gate drags, if the posts move when you push on them, this is the post you want. Repair is sometimes the right answer. We will tell you when it is. But there is a point where patching a fence is just paying twice, and this project was past it.

## The Five-Minute Test That Decides Repair or Replace

You can make most of this decision yourself in five minutes with a screwdriver and a pair of hands, and you should make it before anybody quotes you anything.

**Push on the posts.** Not the panels — the posts. A sound post set in concrete does not move. If the top of a post shifts more than an inch when you lean your weight into it, the post has lost its grip on the ground, and no amount of new pickets fixes that.

**Probe the bottom six inches.** Take a screwdriver and push it into the post right at the grass line, on the shadiest side. If the tip sinks in easily, you are into rot. The bottom of a wood post is where it always goes first, because that is where water sits, air does not move, and the ground stays wet longest. If it is soft at grade on more than a couple of posts, the fence is closer to the end than it looks.

**Look at the rails, not the boards.** Homeowners look at pickets because pickets are what they see. Rails are what hold the thing up. A rail that sags between posts, or that crumbles when you push a thumb into the end grain, is a rail that has taken water at the fastener.

**Open and close the gate.** A gate that drags is telling you the hinge post has moved. Gates are the honest diagnostic on any fence, because they are the only part that gets used every day.

Then do the arithmetic. **If 30 percent or more of the posts are compromised, replacement beats repair on cost.** On a 176-foot run at eight-foot bays, that is about seven posts out of 23. Below that threshold, section repair is genuinely cheaper and we will quote it that way. Above it, you are buying a new fence one panel at a time, and you will pay more for the privilege.

On this Troy fence, 14 of the 23 posts were soft at grade. The decision made itself.

## What Came Out of the Ground on This One

The fence went in during the summer of 2004: six-foot treated pine, 5/8-inch dog-eared pickets, two rails, 4x4 posts on eight-foot centers, set in poured concrete. Standard subdivision work for that era, and it had held up better than most — twenty-two Michigan winters is a reasonable run for treated pine.

What killed it was not the wood. It was the depth.

When we pulled the first post, the concrete ball came up at **22 inches**. Michigan's residential code puts frost depth at **42 inches** for post footings, and that is not a suggestion — it is the depth below which the ground does not freeze and heave. A footing at 22 inches sits inside the frost zone. Every winter, the soil around it freezes, expands upward, and lifts the post a fraction of an inch. Every spring it settles back, but rarely all the way, because the hole has enlarged and grit has worked in. Twenty-two cycles of that is a fence that has been very slowly extruding itself out of the ground.

That is also why the top rail on the north run was four inches out of level while the panels still looked straight. The posts had not all moved the same amount.

Two more findings worth mentioning, because they show up on most replacement jobs:

**The old footings were bell-shaped, not cylindrical.** Somebody poured wide. That matters because a bell footing cannot be pulled — it has to be broken. It also means you cannot set a new post in the same hole and get it deep, because the old concrete is sitting exactly where your new hole needs to go.

**There was no gravel, no drainage, and no post protector at the base of any post.** The posts sat directly in concrete, and concrete holds water against wood. That is the standard way fences were built for decades and it is the reason the rot starts at grade.

None of this is a criticism of the crew that built it in 2004. It was normal practice. It is just not what we do now, and this project is the argument for why.

## Why Michigan Fences Lean (And Why Yours Probably Will Too)

Almost every leaning fence in Oakland County leans for one of three reasons, and only one of them is the wood's fault.

**One: the footing is above frost depth.** Covered above. It is the most common cause by a wide margin, and it is invisible until you dig. If your fence is heaving in one or two spots and the posts still feel solid, this is almost certainly it.

**Two: the soil under the footing is clay that holds water.** Troy has plenty of it. Clay expands when it freezes and moves when it is saturated, and a footing poured into wet clay in August behaves differently from one poured into dry clay in October. A wider hole with gravel at the bottom and concrete that is crowned above grade sheds water away from the post instead of into it.

**Three: the post itself has rotted at grade.** This is the one homeowners blame, and it is usually the consequence of the first two rather than an independent problem. Wood does not rot because it is wood. It rots because it stays wet, and a post in a shallow footing in clay stays wet.

The fix order matters. If you replace pickets on a fence with shallow footings, you have bought two years at best. If you reset the posts but keep the same rotted rails, you have bought three. **The only permanent repair is footings below frost depth, set in a hole that drains.** Everything above grade is cosmetic by comparison.

Our standard now, on every job: hole 8 to 10 inches in diameter, 42 to 48 inches deep measured from final grade, 4 to 6 inches of gravel at the bottom, concrete crowned so water runs off the top of the collar, and post-grade lumber rated for ground contact. That is not gold-plating. It is the difference between a fence that gets you to 2050 and one that gets you to 2040.

## The Garden Problem: Replacing a Fence Without Wrecking Twenty Years of Planting

Here is the thing nobody puts in a fence quote: **replacement work happens on mature lots.** A new build goes into a bare subdivision yard. A replacement goes into a yard where somebody has spent two decades building a border, and the fence line runs right through it.

This yard had a 40-foot perennial border along the east run — hostas, daylilies, coneflower, a stand of black-eyed Susan that had clearly been dividing itself for years — and a mature Japanese maple about 18 feet off the line with a root plate that extended well past it.

The plan was four things, and it cost $270 in hand-digging on the invoice:

**Mark the beds before anything else.** Every plant within three feet of the line got flagged. We do not move a machine or drop a panel until the flags are in, because a pallet of cedar set down in the wrong place flattens a decade of hostas in about four seconds.

**Hand-dig the six holes inside the root zone.** An auger is faster and it is what you use on open ground. Next to a mature maple, an auger will cut a four-inch structural root and you will not know you did it until the tree starts dropping branches in August. Those six holes were dug with a spade and a root saw — slower, more expensive, and the reason the maple is still fine.

**Track mats, not plywood.** We lay 3/4-inch plywood on the travel path and move material in a wheelbarrow rather than a machine wherever the ground is soft. Plywood spreads the load. On a wet November day, a bobcat track will leave ruts in a lawn that take two seasons to disappear.

**Stage on the driveway and carry, do not stage on the lawn.** It adds an hour of labor. It saves the yard.

Two of the border plants had to be lifted and heeled in for the week — a hosta clump and one daylily — and went back into the same spot on day four. Everything else stayed put.

This is the part of a replacement job that a homeowner notices for years. A fence is a fence. A dead maple is a grudge.

## Keeping the Dog In While There Is No Fence

There is a nine-year-old chocolate Lab in this house, and for about six hours on day one there was a 40-foot gap where the east fence used to be. This is a real project blocker and it should be planned, not improvised.

The sequence we use on any job with a dog in the house:

1. **Build the temporary run first, before demolition starts.** Four-foot welded wire on temporary steel T-posts, set inside the new line by about a foot, covering the section that is about to come out. It takes 40 minutes and it is the first thing on site.
2. **Demolish in short sections, not all at once.** Take out 40 feet, build it back, move on. There is never a full-perimeter opening.
3. **Set the gates last, and keep the driveway gate closed.** A gate opening is a hole in containment too.
4. **Tell the homeowner the two times the yard is genuinely open.** Ours were 9 a.m. and 2 p.m. on day one. The dog went inside both times.

If your contractor starts by knocking the whole fence down and then leaves for lunch, that is the tell. Sequencing is not a detail on a replacement job — it is the difference between a project and an incident. Protecting what matters starts before the first post comes out.

## What Troy Actually Requires: Chapter 83 in Plain English

Troy regulates fences directly under Chapter 83 of the City Code, and it is stricter than most of its neighbors in one specific way that catches people: the front-yard allowance is very small.

Here is what applied to this interior-lot project, straight from the ordinance:

| Rule | What Troy requires | What it meant here |
|---|---|---|
| Maximum height, residential | 6 feet above existing grade (Ch. 83, § 2(A)) | Rear and side runs built at 6 feet, measured from soil grade at each post — not from the old footing |
| Front of the front building setback line | 30 inches maximum | The side return stopped at the setback line; nothing taller went forward of it |
| Corner lots, common street line | 48-inch non-obscuring fence allowed, only where a double front setback is required and the lot shares a rear yard with the block | Not applicable — interior lot |
| Intersection sight triangle | No fence over 30 inches within 25 feet of the right-of-way crossing lines | Kept the corner return clear of the triangle |
| Materials | Ornamental fence — metal, wood, or masonry — in residential districts | Western redcedar qualifies |
| Electric and barbed wire | Electric fences banned citywide; barbed wire banned in residential except to guard an attractive nuisance, and then only at 6 feet or higher | Not used |
| Permit | Building Department permit required before construction (Ch. 83, § 5); fee per Chapter 60, § 60.03 | Filed before demolition, not after |
| The timing catch | No permit issues until final lot grading is approved; a fence in an easement also needs Public Works sign-off | This lot's grading was signed off in 2004 — if it had not been, the permit would have sat |

Two practical notes that do not appear in the ordinance but matter more than the ordinance does.

**"Six feet above existing grade" is measured at the post, and grade changes.** On a lot with any slope, a fence that is six feet at the high corner is seven feet at the low corner if you run the top rail level. Troy measures at grade, so the top rail follows the ground and the panels step or rack. We stepped this one in three places over about 11 inches of total fall, which is what keeps it legal and keeps the bottom from floating.

**The grading sign-off is the delay nobody plans for.** If a prior owner added a shed, a pool, a patio, or regrading without final inspection, the fence permit can stall behind it. On a replacement job, check that before you schedule demolition. We ask for the permit number before we set a start date for exactly this reason.

If you are in a subdivision with deed restrictions, the association can be stricter than the city and both apply. Read the covenants before you assume six feet.

## Tear-Out Day: The Part Nobody Prices Correctly

Fence removal runs **$3 to $7 per linear foot** in this market, and that number is quoted so casually that homeowners are surprised every single time by what it does not include.

What it does not include, on this job: 23 concrete footings.

You cannot leave them. A new post needs a hole, and the old bell-shaped footing is sitting in the hole. You cannot pull them either — bells do not come out of clay, they break. So a crew spends a half day with a digging bar, a sledge, and a demo blade, working each ball loose and lifting 60 to 100 pounds of concrete out of a two-foot hole, 23 times, and then loading it.

On this job: **$506 for footing breakout, on top of $563 for removal and haul-away.** That is roughly $1,070 of the project that a homeowner looking at a "$3 per foot" removal number would not have budgeted.

**Disposal is its own rule set.** Treated lumber is construction debris, not yard waste. It does not go in the curbside yard waste bag, it does not go in the compost pile, and it absolutely does not go in a burn barrel — Michigan's open-burning rules prohibit burning treated wood because the preservative does not disappear, it goes into the air and concentrates in the ash. It goes to a licensed disposal site as C&D debris. On a DIY teardown, that means a container, and a 10-yard container runs a few hundred dollars before you have touched the fence.

**Salvage what is worth salvaging.** Sound cedar pickets get saved by homeowners for raised beds and kindling. Treated pine does not — do not build a vegetable bed out of it, and do not burn it. On this job, about 30 feet of pickets were in good enough shape that the homeowner kept them for a garden project, and we stacked them at the curb of the driveway rather than the dumpster.

One more thing about tear-out that surprises people: **it is loud, and it is dusty.** Twenty-two years of dirt and pollen come off a fence when you take it apart. If you work from home, plan to be out on demo day.

## The Rebuild Spec We Used

This is the exact specification, because "cedar fence" means almost nothing on its own and the differences are where the money and the lifespan live.

**Posts: 4x4 ground-contact pressure-treated, 8-foot on center.** Not cedar. Cedar is the better wood above grade and the worse wood in the dirt — it has no preservative treatment and ground contact is where rot starts. Treated posts in the ground, cedar everywhere you can see, is the correct answer and it is what we recommend on every job.

**Gate posts: 6x6 treated, set 4 to 6 inches deeper than the line posts.** A gate leaf puts a lever arm on its hinge post that a line post never sees. Undersized gate posts are why gates sag, and sagging gates are the number one callback on fence work.

**Pickets: 5/8-inch by 6-inch western redcedar, board-on-board.** Board-on-board means alternating pickets are fastened to opposite faces of the rails, so every seam is covered by the board next to it. That is what keeps the fence opaque as the lumber dries and shrinks — a side-by-side fence butted tight in June opens visible gaps by January, and "privacy you can see" stops being true. It uses more material per foot. It is the right choice when privacy is the actual goal.

**Rails: three 2x4 cedar, top, middle, and bottom,** with the bottom rail at 6 to 8 inches off grade so air moves under the fence and a string trimmer does not destroy it.

**Cap rail on top.** It sheds water off the end grain of every picket, which is where a picket fails first. It also makes the fence look finished from both sides, which matters when your neighbor is looking at it.

**Fasteners: stainless steel ring-shank nails.** Cedar is acidic enough to eat galvanized fasteners over time — the tannins react with the zinc. Stainless costs more and is the only fastener we will put in cedar.

**Finish: none yet.** New cedar needs to dry before it takes a stain, and November in Michigan is the wrong window anyway. Staining is scheduled for spring, when temperatures hold above 50 degrees long enough for the finish to cure properly. Staining too cold is worse than not staining.

## What Went Wrong Mid-Job

Three things, and we are putting them in because a project writeup with no problems in it is a brochure.

**One: we cut a buried irrigation line.** Nine inches down on the north run, a one-inch poly line that fed two hose bibs and had been installed after the fence, which is why it was not where the utility locate said it would be. Private irrigation is not marked by the locate service — that is the homeowner's responsibility to disclose and ours to ask about. We now ask on every estimate. Two couplers and a 20-minute repair, at our cost, and both bibs work.

**Two: the old fence was 14 inches onto the neighbor's side on the north run.** Twenty-two years of mowing around a fence has a way of making a wrong line feel permanent. We set the new fence on the survey stakes, which pulled roughly 40 feet of the run inward. That meant regrading 14 inches of lawn along that stretch and building one custom-width panel at the transition. It also meant a conversation with the neighbor, who had been treating that 14 inches as his for two decades. We had it before demolition, not after, and with the survey in hand. Nobody was happy and nobody was surprised, which is the best available outcome.

**Two and a half, related:** Michigan allows a prescriptive easement claim after 15 years of open, continuous use. Twenty-two years is past that. This is not a reason to panic — a claim still has to be proven and most neighbors never pursue one — but it is a very good reason to build the new fence on the line rather than on the old fence, and to do it while everyone is still speaking.

**Three: two footings sat under the maple's root plate.** A machine would have broken them in ten minutes and severed a structural root doing it. We broke those two by hand, which took an hour and a half between them. That is the $270 hand-dig line on the invoice, and it is the cheapest insurance on the job.

## Four Days, Start to Finish

**Day 1 — Demo and layout.** Temporary containment up first. East run and north run stripped, pickets stacked for salvage, panels carried out rather than dragged. 23 footings broken out by mid-afternoon. New line set from survey stakes. Utility locate on site; irrigation line found the hard way.

**Day 2 — Posts.** 23 holes, 42 to 48 inches deep, gravel in the bottom, posts set and braced, concrete crowned. Gate posts set 6 inches deeper. Nothing gets hung on a post for 24 hours — green concrete and a 10-foot gate leaf is how you get a leaning gate post by spring.

**Day 3 — Panels.** Rails, then pickets, board-on-board, working in 40-foot sections so the dog run stays intact as long as possible. The maple root section hand-nailed rather than gun-nailed to keep vibration off the root plate.

**Day 4 — Gates, cap, cleanup.** Gates hung and adjusted, cap rail run, site swept, salvage stacked, dumpster pulled. Walkthrough with the homeowner at 3 p.m., including the two spots we would watch over the winter.

Four working days for 176 feet with two gates and full demolition is a normal pace. If a bid promises two days for this scope, either the footings are not coming out or the panels are going up on the old posts.

## Should You Repair Instead? An Honest Answer

We get asked this constantly, and the answer is genuinely sometimes yes.

**Repair is the right call when:** the posts are solid and the damage is confined to a few panels; a gate has sagged but the hinge post is sound; storm damage took out one section; the fence is under 12 years old and the rot is in the pickets rather than the structure. In those cases a section repair runs a few hundred dollars and buys you real years.

**Replacement is the right call when:** more than 30 percent of the posts are soft at grade; the fence is over 15 years old and showing rot in the rails as well as the pickets; the posts move when you push them; or the fence was built with shallow footings and has been heaving for years. In those cases the repairs are a subscription, not a solution.

The test costs nothing and takes five minutes with a screwdriver. Do it before you call anybody, and if a contractor will not do it with you in the yard before quoting, that tells you something about how the job will go.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do you know when a wood fence needs to be replaced instead of repaired?

Push on the posts, not the panels. If more than 30 percent of them move or are soft at the grass line when you probe with a screwdriver, replacement is cheaper than repairing. On a 176-foot fence at eight-foot bays, that is about seven posts out of 23. Below that threshold, section repair is genuinely the better spend, and a contractor who tells you otherwise is selling, not advising.

### How much does it cost to remove an old fence in Michigan?

Removal runs $3 to $7 per linear foot in 2026, or roughly $500 to $1,200 for a typical backyard fence, and that price usually assumes the footings stay in the ground. Concrete footings are a separate line item at $2 to $3 each to break out, plus disposal. On our Troy project, removal was $563 and footing breakout was another $506 for 23 posts.

### Why do wood fences lean in Michigan?

Almost always because the footing is above frost depth. Michigan's residential code puts frost depth at 42 inches, and a footing at 22 inches sits inside the freeze zone, so it lifts a little every winter and settles back less each spring. Twenty-two cycles of that is a fence that has slowly pushed itself out of the ground, which is exactly what we found on this job.

### Do I need a permit to replace a fence in Troy, Michigan?

Yes. Troy Chapter 83, § 5 requires a Building Department permit before any fence is constructed, and that includes a replacement on an existing line. The fee follows Chapter 60, § 60.03. One catch: no permit issues until the lot's final grading is approved, and a fence in an easement also needs Public Works sign-off, so verify the grading history before you schedule demolition.

### How tall can my fence be in Troy?

Six feet above existing grade in residential districts under Chapter 83, § 2(A), dropping to 30 inches for any portion in front of the front building setback line. Qualifying corner lots can run a 48-inch non-obscuring fence along the common street line. No fence over 30 inches may sit within the 25-foot sight triangle at an intersection. The Building Board of Appeals can waive height limits.

### Can you install a fence in November in Michigan?

Usually yes, until the ground freezes hard — often late November, sometimes early December if snow insulates the soil. Posts still have to reach 42 to 48 inches, and digging gets slower and more expensive as the season closes. This project ran in early November with no frost in the ground. Staining has to wait for spring regardless, because the finish will not cure below 50 degrees.

### Can I reuse the old post holes and footings?

No, and it costs more to try. Old footings are usually wider at the base than the hole you need, and most were poured as bells that cannot be pulled — they have to be broken out. Setting a new post beside an old footing means an oversized hole in disturbed soil, which is a worse foundation than a fresh one. Break them out and dig clean.

### What do I do with the old pressure-treated boards?

Treated lumber is construction and demolition debris, not yard waste. It does not go in curbside yard waste, it does not go in compost, and it must not be burned — Michigan's open-burning rules prohibit it because the preservative concentrates in the ash and goes into the air. Haul it to a licensed C&D site. Sound cedar pickets can be salvaged for garden projects; treated pine should not be used for vegetable beds.
