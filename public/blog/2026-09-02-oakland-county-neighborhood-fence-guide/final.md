# What Fence Fits Your Oakland County Neighborhood? A Community-by-Community Guide to Styles, HOAs, and What Works Where
*Published: September 2, 2026 | By BFFence*

---

Here's what most of those pictures won't tell you: **the best fence for you isn't the one you like best on a screen — it's the one that fits your neighborhood.** A six-foot solid wall that looks perfect on a private wooded lot in Bloomfield Hills would look out of place — and might not even be allowed — on a tight, open lot in a Troy association. A white picket that suits a historic street near downtown Rochester is the wrong tool for a busy road in Auburn Hills where you actually need privacy. Fencing is one of the few things you build that the whole street sees, every day, for the next twenty-plus years. Getting it right means reading your neighborhood first.

## Start With Your Street, Not Your Pinterest Board

Before you pick a style, take a real walk around your block — not a scroll through photos, an actual look at what's standing. Fences are neighborhood furniture. A street reads as coherent when the fences on it speak the same visual language, and a house reads as well cared for when its fence fits its architecture and its lot. That's not about being boring; it's about the difference between a fence that adds to your property and one that fights everything around it.

Here are the five questions we ask every homeowner on the first site visit — and the five you should ask about your own neighborhood before you call anyone:

**1. How big is your lot, really?** Lot size drives fence style more than taste does. On a standard quarter-acre subdivision lot, a six-foot board-on-board privacy fence is the classic answer because your neighbors are close and you need the screen. On two acres in Independence Township, you don't need a privacy wall — you need a boundary marker and maybe a split-rail or a low-profile fence that defines the property without boxing in the view.

**2. What era is your house — and your neighbors' houses?** A mid-century ranch, a colonial, a contemporary, and a craftsman each have a "native" fence language. Match the era and the fence looks like it grew there. Fight it, and the fence reads as a mistake even when it's brand new. We'll give you the matching guide a few sections down.

**3. Are you in an HOA, a subdivision with restrictions, or an older area without one?** This is the single biggest swing factor in what you can build, and it has nothing to do with your taste. Troy alone has dozens of neighborhood associations with architectural guidelines that can dictate your fence material, height, color, and which side faces the street. We'll break down how to handle that below.

**4. What's the landscape doing?** Mature trees, rolling grades, a creek, a lake view — each one changes the right answer. A fence that follows a steep Rochester Hills grade needs stepped sections to look right. A Lake Orion lot with a water view may want a design that protects privacy without walling off the lake. A heavily treed yard means you should think about cedar's natural rot resistance and how the fence will sit among the shade.

**5. Front yard or back?** The rules — and the good manners — are completely different. In nearly every Michigan community, the front yard is where height limits are strictest (typically three to four feet) and where open, see-through designs are expected so drivers and neighbors can see. The backyard is where the six-foot privacy wall lives. A lot of the "fence fights" we get called into start when someone puts a backyard-style wall in a front yard.

Answer those five questions honestly and you've already eliminated half the options. Now let's look at what that means community by community.

## The Communities: What Fits Where in Oakland County

Every community in Oakland County has its own character — its own lot sizes, its own housing stock, its own ratio of old neighborhoods to new subdivisions, its own HOA culture. Here's the local knowledge we've built one job at a time.

### Auburn Hills: The Practical Privacy Market

Auburn Hills is home base for us — our shop is on Williamsburg Circle — and it's a great example of a city with two distinct personalities. Around the corporate campuses and the Great Lakes Crossing corridor, you've got busy roads, commercial adjacency, and newer subdivisions where a solid privacy fence is the difference between a peaceful yard and a parking-lot view. Farther into the established residential areas, you'll find solid mid-century and newer family neighborhoods where board-on-board and shadowbox cedar fences are the workhorses.

What we see in the field: Auburn Hills homeowners tend to want *privacy you can see* — they're not shy about a full six-foot fence, especially when the lot backs up to a busy street or a commercial strip. Board-on-board cedar is the most common choice, with pressure-treated pine the smart budget alternative. If you're on a corner or near a road, expect your city to care about sight lines at the intersection — keep that in mind when you plan the front portion.

### Rochester & Rochester Hills: The Tree-Covered Classic

Rochester and Rochester Hills are some of the most established, most scenic communities in the county — think mature tree canopy, curving subdivisions, cul-de-sacs, and rolling terrain shaped by the Clinton River and Paint Creek corridors. Rochester Hills is one of Oakland County's largest communities, with housing that runs from entry-level to executive estates, and the downtown village of Rochester brings its own historic character.

What we see in the field: this is shadowbox country. The semi-private "good neighbor" look of a shadowbox fence suits the mature, established feel of these streets, and cedar takes a natural stain beautifully against all that green. Because the terrain rolls, we build a lot of stepped fences here — each section following the grade instead of trying to fight it. If your lot backs to a greenbelt, a creek, or a park, you may not need a full six-foot wall at all; a four-foot fence that defines the line and keeps kids and dogs in can be the better, better-looking answer.

### Troy: The HOA Reality Check

Troy is the community where "what fits" is often decided before you ever call a contractor — because Troy is full of established neighborhood associations with decades-old architectural guidelines. These aren't loose suggestions. Many Troy associations have detailed rules governing fence styles, materials, heights, and colors, and they've been enforcing them consistently for a long time. If you live in a Troy subdivision or association, the first step isn't picking a fence — it's pulling your CC&Rs (the covenants, conditions, and restrictions from your deed or your association) and finding out what's approved.

What we see in the field: the good news is that most Troy associations approve wood shadowbox and board-on-board fences in natural or neutral tones — clean, uniform lines fit the community's look. The homeowners who get in trouble are the ones who order first and ask later. Get the approval in writing before you spend a dime, and know that the association can dictate details as small as which side of the fence faces the street (the "finished side out" rule is common). We handle the approval paperwork with homeowners all the time — it's routine, but it has to happen.

### Lake Orion & Orion Township: Lake Country, Handled Right

Lake Orion is a different world — lakefront homes, older cottages, newer estates, and a village core that hugs the water. The fence conversation here is as much about the *view* as it is about privacy. If you're on the water or across from it, a solid six-foot wall can cut off sight lines your neighbors (and possibly your township) care about, while a well-placed privacy section can still give you a secluded patio or yard.

What we see in the field: natural cedar, board-on-board or shadowbox, in tones that blend with the trees and the water. We also do a lot of containment fencing here — lake lots often have odd shapes and steep drop-offs, and keeping kids and dogs safe around the water is job one. If your goal is a safe, private yard without wrecking a good view, talk to us about a layout that does both — this is exactly the kind of project where a site visit beats a catalog.

### Clarkston & Independence Township: Small-Town and Rural Edge

Clarkston keeps its small-town character, and Independence Township around it runs from village lots to genuine acreage. On the rural edge, you don't need privacy from neighbors who are a football field away — you need a fence that marks the boundary, contains the dog, and looks good doing it.

What we see in the field: rustic cedar is the natural fit here. Split-rail or low-profile fencing on bigger lots, board-on-board around the house where you want a private yard, and gates that match. Because lots are bigger and the look is more relaxed, homeowners here have more freedom than almost anywhere else in the county — which means the fence you choose should be driven by *your* property, not by a subdivision standard.

### Novi: The Newer Planned Community Standard

Novi has grown into one of western Oakland County's biggest communities, and much of its housing sits in newer planned developments — which usually means HOAs, architectural committees, and CC&Rs that were written when the subdivision was built. The rules in a newer Novi community often spell out allowed materials and colors in detail, and the approval process is standard procedure.

What we see in the field: in Novi's newer developments, you'll see a mix of cedar and composite, with shadowbox and board-on-board leading the way. Newer CC&Rs sometimes restrict wood in favor of "maintenance-free" materials — check yours before you fall in love with cedar, because your association may have already made the call for you. When wood is allowed, a clean shadowbox fence in a natural tone fits the tidy, planned look of these neighborhoods perfectly.

### Bloomfield Hills & Birmingham: Estate Scale and Standards

Bloomfield Hills and Birmingham operate on a different scale. Bigger lots, deeper setbacks, winding roads, and a strong local culture of architectural standards — Birmingham especially has historic districts and review boards that care a great deal about appearance. Fences here are less about screening a close neighbor and more about defining a larger property, framing a front entry, or adding security at a gate.

What we see in the field: estate-scale runs of fence, longer and straighter, with premium materials — clear cedar, thoughtfully detailed gates, and designs that read as intentional landscape architecture rather than utility. If you're in an area with architectural review, plan for a design review as part of your timeline. The good news: quality wood that's *built to last* is almost always the welcome answer at this end of the market.

### Farmington Hills: The Practical Middle Ground

Farmington Hills is one of the county's largest cities, with curvilinear subdivisions and a wide mix of housing from the 1960s through today. Some neighborhoods have associations with rules; many older areas don't. It's a practical, family-oriented market where the classic privacy fence does exactly what it's supposed to do.

What we see in the field: a healthy mix of board-on-board and shadowbox, cedar and pressure-treated pine. If you're not in an association, your main constraints are the city's own rules (front-yard height, corner sight lines) and your neighbors' feelings — which brings us to the single most useful habit in all of fencing: a five-minute conversation with the person next door before you build.

### Waterford & White Lake: The Lakeside Mix

Waterford and White Lake townships are lake country too — Cass Lake, Lake Oakland, White Lake and more — with a mix of older lake neighborhoods and newer subdivisions. Waterfront here means many of the same view-and-privacy questions as Lake Orion, while the inland subdivisions behave like classic suburban fencing markets.

What we see in the field: rustic cedar by the water, standard privacy fencing in the subdivisions, and a fair amount of replacement work — lake lots have older fences that have taken decades of weather. If you're buying a lakefront home with an existing fence, have it checked before you assume it's protecting anything; lakefront winters are hard on wood.

### Pontiac and the Older Urban Neighborhoods

Pontiac and the county's older, tighter urban neighborhoods are a different fencing environment entirely: smaller lots, houses closer together and closer to the street, more corner lots, and a mix of owner-occupied and rental properties. The rules can be stricter about front-yard fences and sight lines, and the margin for error on property lines is thinner.

What we see in the field: this is where a survey matters most — a few feet in the wrong direction on a tight lot is a big deal. Shorter fences, well-built gates, and repairs to existing fencing are the bread and butter here. If you're replacing an old fence in an older neighborhood, matching the established line and keeping the finished side toward your neighbors will keep you out of nine out of ten disputes.

## The HOA Reality Check: Read the Rules Before You Fall in Love

We keep coming back to HOAs because they're the number one reason a fence plan falls apart — and it's always avoidable. Here's the honest version of how it works:

**Your CC&Rs beat your taste.** If you live in an association or a subdivision with restrictions, the covenants on your deed are the law of your lot. They can specify fence height, material, color, style, and even which side faces out. City code is the floor; your CC&Rs are often the ceiling.

**Approval is a process, not a formality.** Most associations require an architectural review request before you build — plans, materials, sometimes a sketch of your lot. Give yourself two to four weeks for the approval step, and get the answer **in writing**. A verbal "that's fine" from a board member is worth exactly nothing when a different board member calls you six months later.

**The finished side out rule is everywhere.** Whether it's an HOA rule, a city ordinance, or just good manners, the nice side of your fence should face your neighbors and the street. A shadowbox fence is naturally good-neighbor-friendly because both sides look finished — that's why it's so popular in association communities.

**Your neighbors matter even without an HOA.** The cheapest insurance on any fence project is a conversation before you build: *"We're planning a fence along our shared line — here's the plan, and the finished side will face your yard."* People are far less likely to object to something they saw coming. And if you're building right on the line, Michigan's Fence Viewers Act (Act 34 of 1978) even provides a low-cost way to settle cost-sharing and maintenance disputes through the township — though no fence viewer can settle where the boundary actually is. Only a survey can do that.

## Match the Fence to the House: A Quick Style Guide

Once you know your neighborhood's rules and character, match the fence to the architecture. Here's the shorthand we use on every estimate:

| House style | Fence that fits | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Mid-century ranch | Horizontal slat or low board-on-board | Clean, low lines echo the roofline; horizontal boards suit the era's design language |
| Colonial / traditional | Shadowbox or board-on-board, painted or stained neutral | Symmetry and classic proportions match the formal look |
| Contemporary / modern | Horizontal slat, minimal trim, natural cedar | Straight lines and simple planes complement modern geometry |
| Craftsman | Board-on-board cedar, natural stain, substantial posts | Warm wood and honest materials echo craftsman values |
| Lake cottage / rustic | Board-on-board or rustic cedar, natural tones | Blends with trees and water instead of fighting them |
| Split-level / raised ranch | Shadowbox, 5–6 ft, stepped with the grade | Handles the multi-level roofline and rolling yards gracefully |

Two rules apply everywhere: **cedar takes a beautiful natural finish and stands up to Michigan winters**, and **whatever you choose, wood that's installed right — posts set below the 42-inch frost line, in concrete, with proper drainage — is built to last**. The style is the part people see; the installation is the part that decides whether it's still standing straight in twenty years.

## Fitting In Without Giving Up Your Yard

There's a tension in every fence decision: you want privacy and definition, but you don't want to be *that house* — the one whose fence fights the whole street. Here's how to get both:

- **Use the right height for the right job.** Six feet along the back and sides where neighbors are close; three to four feet out front or where the view matters. Not every foot of fence needs to be a wall.
- **Let the fence do its job where it matters most.** If your problem is the neighbor's deck overlooking your patio, screen that sight line — you don't necessarily need to fence the entire property line to reclaim your yard.
- **Choose a style with a good side and a good story.** A shadowbox fence is the peacekeeper: both sides finished, privacy in the middle. If you prefer board-on-board, point the finished side out and tell your neighbor you did.
- **Match materials to the street.** If every fence on your block is natural cedar, a bright white vinyl fence will read as a mistake no matter how "low maintenance" it is. Wood fences, in natural tones, fit Oakland County's landscape — literally.

The goal isn't to disappear into the background. It's to build something that protects your privacy, contains your kids and pets, adds to your property — and makes the neighbors think, *that looks good, maybe we should finally do ours too.*

## Why September Is the Month to Decide

Here's the timing truth from the field: spring is when everyone panics and calls, summer is when the crews are booked solid, and **early fall is the best window of the whole year to install a fence in Michigan.** The ground is still workable, the weather is comfortable for concrete and staining, the busy season is over, and booking windows are short — often one to three weeks instead of a month or more. A fence installed now gets its footings set before the ground freezes, which is exactly what the 42-inch frost line demands, and it's standing and settled by the time the snow flies.

September is also when the mistakes get made — homeowners who wait until November, when the ground is freezing and the crews are wrapping up, then find themselves staring at a spring install and a whole winter without the fence they wanted. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**What kind of fence fits my neighborhood in Oakland County?**
Start with your community's character: established tree-covered areas like Rochester Hills favor cedar shadowbox, HOA communities like Troy and Novi follow their CC&Rs, lake communities like Lake Orion and Waterford favor natural rustic cedar, and larger estate areas like Bloomfield Hills call for premium cedar at a bigger scale. When in doubt, a natural-wood shadowbox or board-on-board fence fits more Oakland County streets than any other choice.

**Do I need HOA approval before building a fence in Troy or a planned community?**
Almost certainly yes. Many Troy and Novi associations have architectural guidelines covering fence materials, heights, and colors, and they require written approval before installation. Read your CC&Rs first, submit the required request with your plan, and keep the written approval — verbal permission isn't enough.

**Is a privacy fence OK in a front yard in Michigan?**
Usually not as a full six-foot wall. Most communities limit front-yard fences to three to four feet and expect open, see-through designs for visibility and sight lines at corners. Solid privacy fencing is typically a side- and rear-yard feature. Check your city's zoning rules, since they vary across Oakland County.

**What's the difference between board-on-board and shadowbox fences?**
Board-on-board mounts pickets flush on both sides for a completely solid wall — maximum privacy, with a clear "front" and "back." Shadowbox (also called good-neighbor) alternates boards on each side of the rails, so it's semi-private with a finished look from both sides. For HOA neighborhoods or shared lines, shadowbox is the peacekeeping choice.

**What fence style goes with a mid-century ranch or a contemporary home?**
Horizontal slat fencing in natural cedar is the go-to for both ranch and contemporary architecture — its clean, low, straight lines complement modern geometry. Traditional and colonial homes read best with classic shadowbox or board-on-board in a neutral finish, and craftsman homes suit warm natural cedar with substantial posts.

**Should I match my neighbors' fence?**
You don't need to copy it, but you should complement it. A street reads well when fences share a visual language — similar height, material family, and tone. If the block is natural cedar and you install bright vinyl, it will fight everything around it. Wood in natural tones fits Oakland County's landscape, and cedar takes a stain that can be tuned to blend or contrast thoughtfully.

**How close to the property line can I build a fence?**
Michigan doesn't mandate a standard setback for most residential fences, but your city may, and the safest answers come from a property survey. Fences can legally sit right on the line in many cases, but knowing exactly where the line is before you dig is the difference between a smooth project and an expensive, awkward move. When in doubt, a survey is cheap insurance.

**Is September a good time to install a fence in Michigan?**
It's the best time. The ground is workable, the weather is right for concrete and stain, the spring rush is over, and booking windows are one to three weeks instead of a month or more. An early-fall install sets posts below the frost line before the ground freezes, so the fence is standing, settled, and ready by the time winter arrives.

**How do I find out my community's fence rules?**
Three sources, in order: your HOA or subdivision CC&Rs (if you have them), your city or township's zoning ordinance (most are posted online), and a quick call to your local building department. Your contractor can help navigate all three — we do it on every project, and we know the Oakland County communities well.
