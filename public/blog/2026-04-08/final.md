# Spring Fence Repair: 7 Warning Signs Michigan Winters Left Behind on Your Wood Fence

*By BF Fence — Oakland County's Trusted Fence Contractor | April 8, 2026*

---

The snow is finally melting across Oakland County. Your yard is re-emerging from months of white, and so is your fence — maybe looking a little rougher than you remember.

That's not your imagination.

Michigan winters are tough on wood fences. Between the heavy snowfall, ice buildup, and that relentless freeze-thaw cycle we all know too well, your fence takes a beating from November through March. The damage often hides under snow for months, only becoming visible once spring arrives and the ground softens.

The good news? If you catch it early, most winter fence damage is fixable without breaking the bank. Wait too long, though, and a loose board becomes a leaning section, and a leaning section becomes a full replacement.

Here's what to look for this spring — and what to do about it.

---

## Why Michigan Winters Are So Hard on Wood Fences

Before we get into the warning signs, it helps to understand *why* your fence takes such a hit. It's not just the cold — it's what the cold does to the ground, the moisture, and the wood itself.

Michigan experiences **60 to 100 freeze-thaw cycles** every winter. That means the temperature swings above and below freezing dozens of times between November and April. Each cycle does a little damage:

- **Water seeps** into tiny cracks in the wood.
- **It freezes** and expands, widening those cracks.
- **It thaws**, allowing more water in.
- **Repeat** — dozens of times.

Meanwhile, the ground itself is doing the same thing. Water in the soil around your fence posts freezes, expands, and pushes the posts around. This is called **frost heave**, and it's the number one cause of leaning fence posts in Michigan.

Oakland County averages **35 to 45 inches of snow** per year. That snow sits on your fence rails, presses against your panels, and keeps moisture trapped against the wood for months. By the time April arrives, you're not just looking at cosmetic damage — you're looking at structural stress that's been building all winter long.

That's why the first warm week of spring is the perfect time for a fence inspection. You can see the damage clearly, the ground is soft enough to work with, and you've got the whole summer ahead for repairs.

## Warning Sign #1: Cracked, Split, or Splintered Boards

**What to look for:** Thin cracks running across the wood grain. Wider splits at the ends of boards. Rough, splintered edges that weren't there last fall.

**Why it happens:** This is the freeze-thaw cycle at work. Every time water gets into a small opening and freezes, it expands by about 9%. Over a Michigan winter with 60-100 freeze-thaw cycles, those tiny cracks become real damage.

**How to check:** Walk your fence line slowly. Look at each board from the side — cracks are easiest to spot at an angle. Pay special attention to the tops of boards, where rain and snowmelt collect. Run your hand along the surface. If you feel rough spots or edges that catch your palm, that's new damage.

**When it's serious:** A few hairline cracks in a 200-foot fence? Normal wear. But if you're seeing cracks in 20% or more of your boards, or if the cracks run deep enough to expose the wood's interior grain, you've got a moisture problem that's accelerating faster than normal.

**What to do:** For minor cracks, a quality wood sealant applied in spring can stop the moisture intrusion and prevent further splitting. For deeper splits, the board should be replaced. If cracks are widespread, it may be time to think about whether the fence has reached the end of its useful life — more on that later.

## Warning Sign #2: Leaning or Tilting Fence Posts

**What to look for:** Posts that tilt to one side. Posts that sit higher or lower than their neighbors. Panels that no longer run in a straight line.

**Why it happens:** Frost heave. When the soil around a fence post freezes, it expands and pushes the post upward or sideways. When it thaws, the soil settles — but not always back to exactly where it started. After multiple freeze-thaw cycles, the post ends up slightly out of position. One bad winter can shift a post by an inch or more. Several winters in a row? You've got a serious lean.

The weight of snow and ice pressing against the fence panels makes this worse. That lateral force, combined with the ground movement, is why you sometimes see fence posts leaning away from the prevailing wind direction.

**How to check:** Stand at one end of your fence line and look down the row of posts like you're sighting a rifle. Posts should line up in a straight, level line. Any post that's visibly out of alignment is a problem. You can also grab the post and try to wiggle it. A properly set post in spring soil should have minimal movement. If it rocks back and forth easily, the concrete footing around it may be cracked or the soil has eroded.

**When it's serious:** A slight lean of an inch or two can often be corrected by digging out and re-setting the post. But if the post has moved significantly, or if the concrete footing has cracked, you're looking at a full post replacement. And if multiple adjacent posts are leaning, the damage is likely structural and spreading.

**What to do:** A single leaning post is a repair job. Re-set the post in fresh concrete, making sure the footing extends below the frost line (42 inches in Oakland County). If multiple posts are leaning, call a professional — there may be a drainage or soil issue that needs to be addressed before new posts go in.

## Warning Sign #3: Loose, Wobbling, or Gap-Filled Panels

**What to look for:** Panels that shake or rattle when you push on them. Visible gaps between panels that used to sit flush. Fasteners (nails, screws, brackets) that are rusted, loose, or missing.

**Why it happens:** Wind, ice, and snow create lateral force on fence panels all winter long. That constant stress loosens the fasteners connecting panels to posts. Rust from winter moisture weakens the metal connections further. Once one fastener fails, the stress concentrates on the remaining ones — and the problem accelerates.

**How to check:** Walk along your fence and press gently on each panel. It should feel solid and immovable. If a panel wobbles, rattles, or shifts when you push it, the fasteners are compromised. Look at the connection points where panels meet posts. Check for rust stains around screws and nails — that orange discoloration is a sign of metal corrosion.

Also look for uneven gaps between panels. If there's a gap that wasn't there last fall, something has moved or the panel has warped.

**When it's serious:** One loose panel? Tighten or replace the fasteners. But if multiple panels are loose, or if the same panel keeps coming loose despite repairs, the issue may be with the posts themselves (see Warning Sign #2). And if the panels are warped or bowed, the wood itself may be compromised.

**What to do:** Replace rusted fasteners with galvanized or stainless steel options — they resist Michigan's moisture much better. If screw holes have been stripped out, move up one size. For panels that won't stay tight, consider adding additional fastener points or upgrading to a heavier-duty bracket system.

## Warning Sign #4: Warped, Bowed, or Twisted Rails

**What to look for:** Horizontal rails that curve, sag, or pull away from posts. Rails that are no longer level. Visible twisting where the wood grain has distorted.

**Why it happens:** Wood is a natural material, and it absorbs moisture. When one side of a rail gets wet (from snow, rain, or ground moisture) and the other side stays dry, the wood dries unevenly. This causes warping — the wet side expands, the dry side contracts, and the board bends.

In Michigan, this happens all winter long. Snow melts and soaks the top of a rail. Wind dries one side. Ice keeps moisture trapped on another. By spring, the rail has twisted out of shape.

**How to check:** Sight down each rail from one end. The rail should be straight and level. If it curves upward, downward, or to one side, it's warped. You can also use a level — place it on top of the rail and check. Even a half-inch of bow over a six-foot rail is significant.

**When it's serious:** Minor warping can sometimes be corrected with fasteners or shims. But if a rail has twisted significantly, it's putting stress on the posts and panels it connects. A warped rail weakens the entire fence section. If multiple rails are warped, the wood may have reached the end of its resistance to Michigan's climate.

**What to do:** For minor warps, you can sometimes straighten the rail by adding additional fastener points and pulling it back into alignment. For severe warping, replacement is the answer. And if warping is widespread, consider upgrading to a higher-quality wood species or applying a protective sealant to the replacement rails to prevent future moisture absorption.

## Warning Sign #5: Rot, Mold, and Soft Spots

**What to look for:** Dark stains on the wood, especially near the base. Green or black patches (mold or mildew). Wood that feels soft or spongy when you press it with your thumb.

**Why it happens:** Michigan's long winters create the perfect conditions for rot. Snow piles up against the base of fence posts and lower boards, keeping them wet for months. The soil stays saturated from snowmelt. And the lack of sunlight on the north-facing side of fences means those surfaces dry out slowly, giving mold and fungi time to establish.

Pressure-treated wood resists rot, but it's not immune. The treatment chemicals break down over time, especially in areas where moisture is constant. In Michigan, the bottom six inches of fence boards and the base of fence posts take the most abuse.

**How to check:** Get down to ground level and look at the base of every post and the bottom of every board. Press your thumb firmly into the wood. If it gives easily, or if your thumb leaves a dent, the wood is rotting from the inside. Check behind shrubs and near downspouts where moisture is trapped. Look at the north-facing side of your fence — that's where rot typically starts first.

**When it's serious:** A small soft spot on one board? Replace it. But if the base of a fence post is soft, that post is compromised structurally. A rotting post can't hold the fence upright, and it's only a matter of time before the section it supports starts to lean or collapse. If multiple posts show signs of rot, you may be looking at a replacement project.

**What to do:** Replace any board with rot. For posts, if the rot is limited to the surface, you can sometimes treat it with a wood hardener and seal it. But if the rot has penetrated deep into the post, replacement is the only reliable solution. To prevent future rot, make sure the soil around posts drains properly — standing water is the enemy. Trim vegetation back from the fence line to improve airflow and sunlight exposure.

## Warning Sign #6: Rusted, Corroded, or Failing Hardware

**What to look for:** Orange rust stains on hinges, brackets, screws, and nails. Hardware that's loose, flaking, or has visible corrosion. Gates that sag, drag, stick, or won't latch properly.

**Why it happens:** Michigan's winter moisture is relentless on metal. Snow, ice, and salt spray from nearby roads all contribute to corrosion. Even galvanized hardware — which is designed to resist rust — eventually breaks down under the constant assault of Michigan winters. Once the protective coating is compromised, rust sets in quickly.

Gate hardware takes the most abuse because it's under constant mechanical stress. A gate that was perfectly aligned last fall may sag or drag by spring because the hinges have corroded or the screws have loosened.

**How to check:** Inspect every piece of metal on your fence — hinges, brackets, screws, nails, latches, and locks. Look for orange discoloration, flaking metal, or hardware that's visibly loose. Test every gate: open it, close it, latch it. The gate should swing freely, close without forcing, and latch securely. If it drags on the ground, sags to one side, or won't latch, the hardware needs attention.

**When it's serious:** A rusty screw here and there? Normal maintenance. But if the brackets connecting panels to posts are corroded, the structural connections are weakened. And if a gate's hinges are so corroded that the gate is barely hanging on, that's a safety and security issue.

**What to do:** Replace all corroded hardware with galvanized or stainless steel replacements. For gates, check the hinge posts — if the post has shifted (see Warning Sign #2), new hardware won't solve the problem. The post needs to be re-set first. Lubricate all hinges and latches with a silicone-based lubricant after replacing hardware.

## Warning Sign #7: Gate Problems — Sagging, Dragging, and Latch Failure

**What to look for:** Gates that don't close flush. Gates that drag on the ground when opening or closing. Latches that don't align or don't engage properly. Gates that swing open on their own or won't stay closed.

**Why it happens:** Gates are the most mechanically complex part of your fence, and they take the most stress. The weight of the gate hangs on two (sometimes three) hinge points, and any movement in those hinge posts translates directly into gate problems.

In Michigan, gate problems in spring are almost always caused by post movement. Frost heave shifts the hinge posts, the latch post, or both. Even a half-inch of movement can cause a gate to drag, sag, or fail to latch. Winter ice can also freeze gates shut, and the force of breaking them free can damage the hinges or warp the gate frame.

**How to check:** Open and close each gate slowly. Watch the gap between the gate and the latch post — it should be even from top to bottom. If the gap is wider at the top or bottom, something has moved. Check if the gate drags on the ground at any point during its swing. Test the latch — it should engage cleanly without forcing. If you have to lift the gate to latch it, the hinge post has settled.

**When it's serious:** A minor drag or sag can be corrected with hinge adjustments or a turnbuckle brace. But if the gate frame itself is warped, or if the hinge post has shifted significantly, you're looking at a more involved repair.

**What to do:** For sagging gates, a turnbuckle brace (a diagonal cable from the bottom hinge corner to the top latch corner) can often correct the sag without replacing anything. For post movement, the post needs to be re-set. For gates that drag on the ground, you can sometimes trim the bottom of the gate — but that's a temporary fix. The real solution is to fix the post.

## Repair or Replace? How to Decide

Not every problem means you need a new fence. Here's a simple framework:

**Repair when:**
- Less than 20-30% of your fence is damaged
- The posts are solid and properly set
- The fence is less than 10 years old
- The damage is limited to boards, fasteners, or hardware
- You're catching problems early (like right now, in spring)

**Replace when:**
- More than 30% of your fence shows damage
- Multiple posts are leaning or rotted
- The fence is 15+ years old and showing widespread wear
- You're repairing the same sections repeatedly
- The wood is soft or crumbling across multiple boards

A well-maintained wood fence in Michigan can last **10 to 15 years**. With premium materials like cedar and consistent maintenance, you might get 20 years or more. But Michigan's climate is demanding, and neglect accelerates the timeline significantly.

## The Cost of Waiting

Here's something most homeowners don't think about: **spring is the cheapest time to repair your fence.**

Why? Three reasons:

1. **Contractors aren't as busy yet.** Peak fence season is May through September. If you call in April, you'll likely get better pricing and faster scheduling.

2. **The ground is soft.** Repairing or replacing posts is much easier when the ground has thawed but hasn't dried out and hardened. By mid-summer, digging post holes becomes a much bigger job.

3. **Small repairs stay small.** That loose panel you ignore in April? By July, after spring rains and summer storms, it's pulled three more panels loose and the post has started to lean. A $50 fastener replacement becomes a $500 post replacement.

We've seen it hundreds of times across Oakland County. Homeowners who call in April get a quick fix. Homeowners who wait until August are often looking at a much bigger project.

## Protecting Your Fence Going Forward

Once you've made your spring repairs, here's how to keep your fence in good shape for next winter:

### Apply a Quality Wood Sealant
A fresh coat of stain or sealant every 2-3 years creates a moisture barrier that slows the freeze-thaw damage. Apply it in late spring or early summer, after repairs are complete and the wood has had time to dry.

### Maintain Proper Drainage
Make sure water flows away from your fence posts. If you notice standing water near posts after rain, regrade the soil or add drainage. Redirect downspouts so they don't discharge near the fence line.

### Trim Vegetation
Keep bushes, shrubs, and tree branches trimmed back from your fence. Vegetation traps moisture and blocks sunlight, creating the perfect environment for rot.

### Inspect Annually
Make a spring fence walk part of your annual home maintenance routine. Catch problems early, and you'll save yourself money and headaches.

### Address Issues Promptly
That wobbly panel? Fix it now, before the next storm makes it worse. That rusty hinge? Replace it before it fails completely. Small fixes are cheap. Big fixes are not.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the first signs of winter damage on a wood fence?
The earliest signs are small cracks in boards, slight leaning of posts, loose panels, and rusted hardware. These problems start small but worsen quickly with spring rain and summer heat. If you notice any of these signs, schedule a professional inspection.

### When should I repair my fence in Michigan?
Spring is the best time — ideally April or early May. The ground is soft, contractors have more availability, and you catch problems before summer storms make them worse. The longer you wait, the more expensive the repair becomes.

### How much does fence repair cost in Oakland County?
Minor repairs (fasteners, individual boards) typically run $150-$500. Post replacement can range from $200-$400 per post. Major section repairs may cost $500-$1,500. Full fence replacement for a standard residential lot runs $2,500-$8,000 depending on materials and length.

### How long does a wood fence last in Michigan?
A well-maintained wood fence in Michigan typically lasts 10-15 years. Premium cedar fences with consistent maintenance can last 20+ years. Untreated or neglected fences may show significant deterioration in as little as 5-7 years.

### Does a fence increase property value?
Yes. Studies show homeowners typically recoup 30-70% of their fence installation cost at resale. Privacy fencing is the most valued type among homebuyers. A well-maintained fence signals a cared-for property.

### What causes fence posts to lean after winter?
Frost heave — the freezing and expansion of soil around fence posts — is the primary cause. Michigan's 60-100 annual freeze-thaw cycles push posts out of alignment. Snow and ice pressing against panels add lateral force that accelerates the leaning.

### Is it better to repair or replace a fence?
If less than 30% of your fence is damaged and the posts are solid, repair is usually more cost-effective. If more than 30% is damaged, posts are failing, or the fence is 15+ years old, replacement often makes more financial sense.
