## Why the Fence Is the Backbone of a Kid-Safe Yard

Here's a number that should stop every parent cold: drowning is the **leading cause of death among children ages 1 to 4** in the United States — more than any other cause, including car accidents. The CDC tracks roughly 4,000 unintentional drowning deaths every year nationwide, and for kids ages 5 to 14 it's the second leading cause of unintentional injury death, behind only motor vehicle crashes. Most of those deaths happen in residential settings, and most are preventable.

We're not telling you this to scare you — we're telling you because it explains why the fence is the most important structure most families will ever build in their backyard. A good backyard fence does four jobs at once:

- **It contains.** Little kids stay in; the street, the neighbor's pool, the pond, and the woods stay out.
- **It delays.** The fence is the layer that buys time. A properly latched gate doesn't replace supervision — it makes supervision possible, turning "watch them every second" into "watch them from the kitchen window."
- **It screens.** Privacy and safety are the same purchase. When neighbors can't see in, your kids aren't performing for an audience. That's the "privacy you can see" part of fencing.
- **It signals.** A well-built fence tells the whole street this is a cared-for home — its own quiet form of protecting what matters.

Here's what a fence cannot do: it can't replace supervision, swimming lessons, or common sense — no barrier is a babysitter. The right mental model is layers, and each one makes the others work better.

## What Michigan Code Actually Requires Around Pools

If you have a pool — or a neighbor with one, or you're house-hunting near one — this section matters most. Pool barriers are the most code-heavy fencing in residential construction, and the rules are specific, technical, and enforced at inspection.

Michigan doesn't have a standalone "pool fence law." Instead, the state adopts the International Residential Code, whose pool provisions (Appendix G) require a compliant barrier around **any residential pool deeper than 24 inches**, enforced by your local building department. The barrier is part of your pool permit and gets inspected before the pool can legally be used. Here's what inspectors actually check:

| Requirement | Michigan Residential Code Baseline |
|---|---|
| Barrier height | **Minimum 48 inches** from grade on the outside face (some communities require 60") |
| Openings | No opening may pass a **4-inch sphere** (roughly a tennis ball); vertical members ≤ 4" apart |
| Horizontal members | If horizontal rails are less than 45" apart, vertical spacing tightens to **1¾ inches** max |
| Bottom clearance | Max **2 inches** between the bottom of the barrier and grade |
| Gates | **Self-closing and self-latching**, swinging **outward, away from the pool** |
| Latch | At least **54 inches** above grade (or in a child-unreachable configuration on the pool side) |
| House doors/windows | If the house wall is part of the barrier, every door and operable window to the pool area needs an **audible alarm** |
| Alternative | An **ASTM F1346-rated safety cover** can substitute for some alarm requirements |

The 48-inch number is the floor, not the ceiling. Several Oakland County communities require more: **Bloomfield Hills, Birmingham, and Beverly Hills** commonly require a full **60-inch** barrier with tighter setbacks, and some townships run even taller — Springfield Charter Township's ordinance calls for a six-foot protective fence around pool areas. Novi builds its pool rules on both the Michigan Residential Code and the International Swimming Pool and Spa Code. The practical takeaway: **the community you're in changes the requirement**, so a fence that passes in one township can fail a few miles away. Confirm height and setback rules with your local building department before you build — a reputable contractor pulls the local code as part of the permit package.

Two realities rarely make it into the online summaries:

**Your house is probably part of the barrier.** In most Michigan backyards, the house wall serves as one side of the pool enclosure — allowed under the code. But it triggers the alarm rule: every door from the house to the pool area must sound an audible alarm when opened, with the temporary deactivation switch mounted at least 54 inches high, out of a small child's reach. Sliding doors count. Basement walkouts count. Pet doors count, and inspectors flag them. A battery-powered door alarm runs $15 to $40 per door and installs in ten minutes — the cheapest safety upgrade in this guide.

**Your insurance company has opinions.** Pools are what the law calls an "attractive nuisance," and most homeowners policies condition liability coverage on a compliant barrier — sometimes a compliant cover too. If a child is injured in your pool and your barrier doesn't meet policy specs, a claim can be denied. Call your carrier before you build or buy near a pool and ask what their barrier requirements are — often a notch above local code, and cheaper to build to now than to retrofit later.

## Above-Ground Pools: The Rules Are Different

Above-ground pools are everywhere in Oakland County — Waterford, White Lake, Clarkston, and the lake towns especially — and their rules surprise most owners.

For an above-ground pool, the pool's own wall usually *is* the barrier. If the wall stands at least 48 inches above grade all the way around and offers nothing to grab or step on from outside, the code accepts it in place of a separate fence. The catch is access: **any ladder or steps have to be removable, lockable, or otherwise secured whenever the pool is unattended.** A ladder that folds up and locks out of reach is the usual answer, and removing it can't leave an opening that passes a 4-inch sphere.

Two things quietly void the shortcut:

**A wall under 48 inches.** If your above-ground pool sits lower than 48 inches off grade — common with partially sunk pools — the wall doesn't qualify and you need a real barrier around the whole thing.

**An attached deck.** This is the big one. The moment a deck gives a child a flat path to the water without the ladder, inspectors treat the pool like an in-ground pool: full compliant barrier required. We've built many a short wood fence section for exactly this reason — a family with a great deck and a pool they suddenly realized was wide open.

And if your community requires 60 inches, the above-ground wall won't reach the local minimum anyway.

## Building a Fence Small Kids Can't Beat

Now the actual fence craft. Pool code gets the attention, but the fence most families need is simpler: a strong, climb-resistant wood perimeter around the play yard. The good news is that the same design choices that make a fence beautiful also make it safe — which is why well-built wood beats every other option for family yards.

### Height: Match the Fence to the Kid

| Situation | Recommended height |
|---|---|
| Toddlers and young kids (ages 1–6) | 4 feet minimum, solid construction |
| Mixed ages, active kids, or kids who climb | 5–6 feet |
| Athletic kids or known climbers | 6 feet, no exterior footholds |
| Pool barrier (check local code first) | 4 feet baseline / 6 feet in 60" communities |
| Privacy + containment in one build | 6 feet (the standard Oakland County privacy fence) |

Four feet stops most small children, but not a determined seven-year-old — which is why the design details below matter more than raw height. Most Oakland County families who want privacy and containment end up at six feet of well-built cedar, which is genuinely hard for a child to beat.

### The Anti-Climb Design Rules

**No footholds on the outside face.** This is rule one. Children climb fences like ladders: they need something to stand on. A board-on-board fence — pickets mounted flush on both sides of the rails, no gaps — presents a flat, unbroken face with nothing to grip. That's why board-on-board is our default for family yards. A shadowbox (good-neighbor) fence is beautiful and semi-private, but its alternating boards expose the horizontal rails as ladder rungs on both sides, and kids absolutely use them. For a pure family-safety build, board-on-board wins.

**Keep the spacing honest.** The 4-inch sphere rule isn't just for pools — it's a great standard for any fence around young children, because it's sized to a small child's head. Solid board-on-board and shadowbox designs have no through-gaps at all, another reason wood privacy fencing is the family-safe choice over wide-spaced styles.

**Watch the bottom.** A two-inch gap is fine for a pool barrier and most yards — a toddler isn't going through it. But when bottom boards rot or a section settles, gaps grow, and kids are experts at finding them. More on that in the audit checklist.

**Nothing climbable near the line.** The fence is only as good as what's parked next to it. We routinely see playsets, trampolines, sheds, AC units, firewood piles, and patio furniture sitting against the fence — each one a step stool. Keep climbable objects a few feet off the line, and swing sets well clear.

**Choose the right wood, finished right.** Cedar is our go-to for family fences: naturally rot-resistant, stable through Michigan freeze-thaw, and — important with barefoot kids — smooth and splinter-free for years when milled well. Pressure-treated pine is a fine budget choice and structurally tough, but it can check and splinter more as it dries. Whatever wood you choose, seal or stain it within the first year: a maintained fence is smoother, safer, and built to last.

## The Gate Is the Weak Link in Every Yard

If we had to name the most common safety failure across hundreds of family yards, it's not the fence — it's the gate. A fence can be perfect for twenty years and still fail the day a gate latch stops catching. Gate hardware takes more abuse than any other part of the fence: it swings thousands of times a year, bakes in summer sun, freezes in January, and gets hung on by small children who haven't learned patience.

Here's what a child-safe gate needs:

**Self-closing hinges.** The gate should close itself every time, without anyone remembering. Spring-loaded hinges cost a little more and are worth every dollar — "I thought I closed the gate" is a sentence no parent should have to say.

**A latch a child can't reach or work.** Mount the latch high — 54 inches is the pool-code standard and a sensible goal for any family gate: out of reach for small children, comfortable for adults. If it sits lower, it should be on the yard side, positioned so a child can't reach through and flip it. Don't trust a plain gravity latch with a curious three-year-old.

**A gate that doesn't sag.** A sagging gate is a gate whose latch slowly stops lining up — and a latch that doesn't catch is no latch at all. Proper bracing, heavy hinges, and posts set deep enough to stay put keep a gate true for decades. This is where cheap installation shows up two years later, and why we brace every gate and set every gate post below the frost line.

**The bottom gap check.** For a pool barrier, the gap under the gate counts toward the 2-inch clearance rule — and gates are where that rule gets violated most, because gates settle and drag. Keep gate bottoms tight to the ground and check them seasonally.

**Double gates need double hardware.** Wide double gates are wonderful for mowers, but they need cane bolts (drop rods) at the bottom of the inactive leaf — otherwise the unlatched leaf swings open at kid level. Every double gate we build gets cane bolts on both leaves.

One honest note: buy good hardware. The cheap self-latching hardware that passes inspection often fails within a few years under Michigan's sun and ice. Stainless or heavily coated hardware is the difference between a gate that self-latches for fifteen years and one needing a new latch every third spring.

## Two-Zone Yards: Play Area, Pool Area, and the Fence Between Them

The most common mistake we see in family yards with pools is making one fence do two jobs poorly. The cleaner solution is usually two zones.

**Zone one is the play yard** — grass, swing set, sandbox. This is where the family fence lives: six-foot board-on-board for privacy and containment, with a child-safe gate on the driveway or street side.

**Zone two is the pool** — and if code allows, the pool gets its own dedicated barrier, sized and gated to the pool rules (48 or 60 inches, self-closing gate swinging away from the water, tight bottom clearance). A separate pool barrier means the pool is protected even when the main gate is open for the mower, the delivery driver, or the neighbor kids — and you're not asking your privacy fence to double as a pool barrier, which it can do only if every detail lines up: height, spacing, bottom gap, gate hardware, latch height.

**When one fence does double duty:** if your lot is small or you prefer a single perimeter, a well-built six-foot board-on-board fence *can* serve as the pool barrier — provided it meets every pool requirement, including gate swing and latch placement. Confirm the plan with the building department before you commit, not after. The most expensive fence we've seen was built to pool spec in a community that required a taller barrier than the homeowner was told about.

**Play-set placement** deserves its own rule, because it's the one we repeat most in the field: keep climbing and swinging equipment well away from the fence line. A swing set six feet from the fence is a fall zone; a swing set two feet from the fence is a launch pad — kids climb to the top of the slide, step onto the fence rail, and over they go. CPSC playground guidance for supervised play spaces calls for fencing at least 48 inches with latches out of reach and vertical members 4 inches or less apart; for home yards the practical rule is simpler: equipment clear of the fence, gates latched, play zone visible from the house. The same logic applies to trampolines: an enclosure net is not a fence, and one parked against the perimeter hands your kids the neighborhood on a silver platter.

## Michigan Seasons Are Part of the Safety System

A fence that's safe in July can be unsafe in February, and Michigan is the reason.

**Frost heave moves gates.** Posts set above the frost line shift as the ground freezes and thaws — and a shifted post means a gate that no longer self-closes or a latch that no longer lines up. Every fence we build sets posts below the frost line with concrete footings, because in Michigan that's not a detail, it's the whole game. If your gate has started sticking or catching, that's the first red flag of frost movement — fix it before it becomes a "the gate didn't latch" story.

**Snow is a climbing aid.** A good snowbank against the fence line is a ramp: a four-foot fence with two feet of drifted snow next to it is a two-foot fence. Keep snow cleared from the fence line in winter, and remember it when you choose heights — the winter version of your yard is part of your yard.

**Ice finds the latch.** Latches and hinges that hold water freeze solid in January, and self-closing hinges that can't move in the cold are self-closing hinges that don't close. Good hardware sheds water and keeps working in the cold; cheap hardware seizes. Buying a house in winter? Test every gate latch while you're there — a frozen latch in January is the same broken latch in June, just with better excuses.

**Wood moves.** Cedar and pine expand and contract with the seasons; a gate built without proper clearance binds in summer and gaps in winter. A well-built wood fence is designed for Michigan's swing — boards gapped for expansion, gates braced against movement, hardware chosen for the weather. That's what "built to last" actually means where the temperature swings a hundred degrees between January and July.

## The 12-Point Family Safety Audit

Here's the checklist we walk with every family client — and the one you can walk this weekend without calling anyone. Give yourself an hour and a notepad.

1. **Walk the full perimeter.** Look for gaps between boards, rot at the base, and any opening a child's head could pass (4-inch rule — carry a tennis ball).
2. **Push on every section.** Firm posts don't move. Rocking sections mean failing posts — pushing on a loose section is how gaps open.
3. **Test every gate latch — twice.** Open each gate, let it close, and confirm it latches *by itself* every time. Repeat; the first pass lies.
4. **Check latch height and reach.** Can a small child reach through, under, or around to flip the latch? If yes, move it up or change the hardware.
5. **Look for footholds.** Horizontal rails, lattice, stored items, playsets, spools, AC units — anything climbable within a few feet of the line.
6. **Check the bottom gaps.** More than 2 inches under a pool barrier or gate is a violation and a squeeze-risk; more than about 4 inches under a yard fence invites a determined toddler.
7. **If you have a pool:** confirm the barrier meets your community's height (48 or 60 inches), the gate swings away from the pool, the latch is at 54 inches, and bottom clearance is under 2 inches.
8. **Test the door alarms.** Every door from the house to the pool or yard should alarm when opened. Replace batteries.
9. **Secure the pool ladder** (above-ground pools) — locked, removed, or folded out of reach when the pool is unattended.
10. **Move the playset.** Anything climbable sits well clear of the fence line, with a proper fall zone under swings and slides.
11. **Look at it from a child's height.** Get on your knees. The world looks different at 3 feet tall, and the gaps and toeholds you'll spot down there are exactly the ones your kids have already found.
12. **Book the repair.** Anything that fails gets fixed — not scheduled for someday. Rotted boards, a gate that won't latch, a rocking section: these are the difference between a fence and a false sense of security.

If you're fixing more than one or two items, it's worth having a professional look at the whole fence — sometimes the honest answer is that a tired fence has stopped being safe and replacement is the real fix. Our fence replacement guide covers how to make that call.

## FAQ: Child-Safe Fencing Questions We Hear Every Week

**What kind of fence is safest for a backyard with kids?**
A solid wood fence with no gaps, no exterior footholds, and a child-safe gate — our default is six-foot board-on-board cedar. The flush surface gives a child nothing to climb, the solid construction passes the 4-inch sphere test by design, and cedar stays smooth and splinter-free. Whatever the style, gate hardware matters most: self-closing hinges and a high, child-resistant latch are non-negotiable.

**Do I need a fence around my pool in Michigan?**
Yes. Michigan adopts the International Residential Code, which requires a compliant barrier around any residential pool deeper than 24 inches. The baseline is 48 inches with self-closing, self-latching gates and openings that can't pass a 4-inch sphere — and some Oakland County communities, including Bloomfield Hills, Birmingham, and Beverly Hills, require a taller 60-inch barrier. The barrier is inspected as part of your pool permit.

**How tall should a fence be to keep kids in the yard?**
Four feet stops most small children and is the pool-code floor, but for mixed ages, active kids, or known climbers, plan on five to six feet. Design matters as much as height: a six-foot fence with climbable rails or playsets parked against it is easier to beat than a four-foot fence with a flat face and clear ground. Check your community's height rules first — front-yard and HOA restrictions vary across Oakland County.

**Are self-closing gates required for kid-safe yards?**
For pool barriers, yes — code requires gates to be self-closing and self-latching, swinging outward from the pool. For any yard with small children, self-closing hardware should be standard anyway: "I thought I closed the gate" is how kids reach streets, pools, and ponds. It's a small upgrade that removes human error from the system.

**What is the 4-inch sphere rule?**
It's the code standard that no opening in a barrier may allow a sphere 4 inches in diameter to pass through — sized to approximate a small child's head, so kids can't squeeze through gaps or get stuck. It applies to picket spacing, gate gaps, and the space under a pool barrier (held to 2 inches at the bottom). A tennis ball is about 4 inches across, making it a perfect field-testing tool.

**Can my privacy fence double as my pool fence?**
Yes, if it meets every pool-barrier requirement: the right height for your community (48 or 60 inches), no openings that pass a 4-inch sphere, no climbable exterior, a gate that swings away from the pool and self-latches with the latch at 54 inches, and no more than 2 inches of bottom clearance. Many families prefer a dedicated pool barrier inside the yard so the main gate can stay open without compromising the pool — but a compliant single perimeter fence is a legitimate solution. Confirm your plan with the building department before you build.

**What are the rules for above-ground pools?**
If the pool wall stands at least 48 inches above grade all the way around, the wall itself can serve as the barrier — as long as the ladder is removable, lockable, or otherwise secured whenever the pool is unattended. The shortcut stops applying if the wall is under 48 inches or an attached deck gives a child a path to the water without the ladder; either situation triggers a full compliant barrier. Communities requiring 60-inch barriers need a taller solution than most above-ground walls provide.

**How do I know when my fence is no longer safe for kids?**
Run the 12-point audit: gaps that pass a tennis ball, boards you can push through, rot at the base, rocking sections, a gate that no longer self-latches, a latch a child can reach, or anything climbable against the fence line. If you're fixing more than a couple of items — or the fence is showing its age across the board — it's time for a professional assessment and an honest conversation about replacement.
