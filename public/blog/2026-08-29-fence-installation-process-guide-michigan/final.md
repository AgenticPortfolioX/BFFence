## Step 1: The Free Estimate — What the Contractor Is Actually Looking At

Every fence project starts the same way: with a visit to your property. Most reputable contractors, including us, offer a free estimate. This isn't a sales pitch disguised as a site visit — it's the most important part of the process, because it's where the contractor learns the things that will determine your timeline, your cost, and your fence's lifespan.

Here's what a good estimator does when they walk your yard:

- **Walks the full property line**, not just the side you want fenced. Slopes, trees, utility boxes, and low spots all live on the edges.
- **Measures the actual linear footage** — the number your quote is built on.
- **Checks the soil conditions.** Oakland County soil is a mix of heavy clay, sandy loam, and rocky patches — sometimes on the same lot. Clay holds water and makes holes collapse; rock makes digging slow.
- **Looks at grade changes.** A perfectly flat yard is the exception. Slopes mean stepped or racked fence sections, which take more layout time and material.
- **Checks for existing structures** — sprinkler heads, invisible dog fences, AC condensers, garden beds, and old fence remnants.
- **Asks about the neighbors.** A fence that hugs the property line is a shared visual experience, and a heads-up conversation before you build saves a lot of tension later.

Expect the estimate visit to take 20 to 40 minutes for a typical residential lot. Within a day or two, you'll get a written quote that spells out the material, linear footage, style, number of gates, and timeline. A good quote is transparent — it shows you what you're paying for, not just a bottom line.

One thing we tell every homeowner during the estimate: be honest about your budget. There's no shame in a number. A good contractor will work within it, recommend the material that fits, and tell you what you're giving up — and what you're not — at each price point. What you don't want is a contractor who lowballs the estimate and finds "surprises" later.

## Step 2: Property Lines — Know Your Boundary Before Anyone Digs

This is the step that separates a smooth fence project from a neighborhood dispute. Michigan has no statewide rule that says where a fence must sit on your property — it can go right on the line or a few inches inside it — but where you build it determines who owns it, who maintains it, and who can legally tear it down.

Here's the plain truth: **most homeowners do not actually know where their property line is.** The lot lines on your deed's plat map don't always match where the previous owner's shrub line or the neighbor's driveway edge suggests the boundary sits. And by the time a fence is up, a mistake is expensive — you can end up paying to move a fence that's two feet onto the neighbor's land, or worse, paying to take it down entirely.

What we recommend:

- **Pull your plat map** — the survey diagram from your deed, available at the Oakland County Register of Deeds or often through your township's online portal.
- **Look for survey markers** — the iron pins or stakes at the corners of your property, often buried just below grade. If you can't find them, that's a sign you should invest in a survey.
- **Get a boundary survey if there's any doubt.** A licensed surveyor will set new pins, and the cost — typically a few hundred dollars for a residential lot — is cheap compared to moving a fence.
- **Talk to your neighbor before the estimate, not after.** A quick conversation — "we're planning a fence along our shared line, want to see the plan?" — prevents a fence built on bad feelings. If you build on the line itself and the neighbor benefits, they may even be willing to split the cost. A shared boundary fence that both sides maintain lasts longer.

If a boundary dispute ever does come up, Michigan has a mechanism for it: the **Fence Viewers Act (Act 34 of 1978)**. Each township appoints at least one "fence viewer" who can be called in to settle disputes about fence cost, maintenance, and animal damage. You file a written request and pay a small fee — typically $25 — to the township treasurer, and the fence viewer issues a binding decision within seven days. It's a low-cost alternative to court — but fence viewers settle *cost and maintenance* disputes, not *boundary* disputes. Where the line actually is, only a survey can answer.

## Step 3: Permits — Does Oakland County Actually Require One?

This is the question we answer more than any other, and the honest answer is: **it depends on your city.** Michigan's statewide Residential Code generally exempts fences under seven feet tall from a *building* permit. But that's only the starting point. Every city and township sets its own zoning rules — permit requirements, height limits, setbacks, and materials — and those rules are often stricter than the state baseline.

Here's the general pattern across Michigan:

- **Front yards**: most cities cap fences at 3 to 4 feet, and many require open (not solid) designs so drivers and neighbors can see.
- **Side and rear yards**: 6 feet is the most common limit for a solid privacy fence.
- **Corner lots**: nearly every municipality adds "visibility triangle" restrictions so fences don't block sight lines at intersections.
- **Permit requirements**: many Metro Detroit communities require a fence permit for new installations — Troy, Royal Oak, Birmingham, and Ferndale among them.

In Oakland County specifically, the rules vary from one municipality to the next. Auburn Hills, Rochester Hills, Troy, Lake Orion, Clarkston, Novi, and Bloomfield Hills all have their own ordinances, and what's a simple application in one town can be a full site-plan review in another. A few things that are nearly universal:

- **Height limits** of 4 feet in the front and 6 feet in the side/rear, unless your property is unusual (large lots, agricultural zoning, and so on).
- **Fence placement rules** — often the fence must sit a few inches inside the property line, and never in the public right-of-way (the strip between the sidewalk and the street belongs to the city).
- **"Good neighbor" rules** — in many communities, the finished side of the fence must face your neighbor's property, not your own.

So who pulls the permit? Some contractors leave it to the homeowner; good ones handle it as part of the project. We handle permits on most jobs because we know the local offices, the forms, and the typical review times — generally a few days to a few weeks depending on the municipality. One more reason to start early rather than assuming you can build next weekend.

And yes, skipping the permit can cost you: fines, a stop-work order, or being forced to move or remove a fence that doesn't meet code. It's not worth the gamble, and any contractor who suggests you skip it isn't doing you any favors.

## Step 4: MISS DIG 811 — The Free Call That's Also the Law

Before a single hole gets dug, Michigan law requires something most homeowners have never heard of: **MISS DIG 811**.

MISS DIG is Michigan's utility notification center — a free, nonprofit service that coordinates the marking of underground utilities. When you — or your contractor — file a locate request, MISS DIG notifies every utility company with buried lines near your property, and they send locators to paint and flag exactly where their lines run. The colored flags each have a meaning: red for electric, yellow for gas, orange for communication lines, blue for water, green for sewer.

Why does this matter for a fence? Because fence posts go down three-plus feet, and underground utilities often run far closer to the surface than anyone expects. Hitting a gas line or a buried electric feed with a post-hole auger is not a minor inconvenience — it's a serious safety hazard and a very expensive repair. The locate is fast, free, and required by law, and a professional crew will never dig without one.

A few things to know about the locate process:

- **Lead time**: plan for 2 to 10 business days between the request and the marked lines. It's rarely instant, especially in spring.
- **The flags stay put**: once lines are marked, leave the flags and paint alone until the digging is done. The marks are your map of what's underground.
- **Locates are valid for a limited window** — typically a few weeks — so time the request so it's active on install day.
- **Private lines are on you**: MISS DIG marks utility company lines, but not private lines like your invisible dog fence, sprinkler system, or a neighbor's buried conduit. Tell your contractor about anything you've buried.

MISS DIG is one of those steps that feels like bureaucratic friction until you understand what it's protecting. It's 10 minutes online or one phone call, and it's the difference between a clean install and a disaster. We never skip it, and neither should you.

## Step 5: Material Ordering and Scheduling — Where the Real Lead Time Lives

Here's a number that surprises most homeowners: the actual installation is usually the *shortest* part of the project. For a typical residential wood privacy fence, a two-person crew can install 50 to 80 linear feet per day — so a 200-foot fence is roughly two and a half to four days of work. But the time between signing the contract and the crew showing up is where the calendar really stretches.

Here's what a realistic end-to-end timeline looks like for a typical Oakland County fence project:

| Phase | Typical Duration | Notes |

| Estimate visit | 1-3 days after first call | Depends on your schedule |
| Written quote | Same day to 2 days | — |
| Your decision | 4-7 days (avg) | Range is 1-14 days |
| Permit (if required) | 3-21 days | Varies wildly by city |
| Utility locate (MISS DIG) | 2-10 business days | Required before digging |
| Material ordering | 1-7 days stock / 5-14 special order | Cedar and treated pine usually stocked |

The takeaway: if you want a fence up before the leaves turn, the time to call is now, not in October. The bottleneck is almost never the crew — it's the combination of permits, utility locates, material availability, and everyone else in Michigan who wants a fence at the same time you do.

For material specifically, the two workhorses of the Michigan fence world are cedar and pressure-treated pine — both usually stocked items for a busy contractor. Specialty materials (composite, aluminum, vinyl, or custom gates) can add lead time, so if you're set on something specific, plan for it.

## Step 6: Your Prep Checklist — What to Do Before Install Day

Here's the part where you, the homeowner, can make the biggest difference in how smoothly your install goes. A little prep work on your end saves time on install day, keeps the crew on schedule, and protects your yard in the process:

- **Mow and trim the fence line.** The crew needs to see the ground and walk the line. Tall grass hides obstacles and slows everything down.
- **Mark your sprinkler heads.** If you have an irrigation system, flag every head along the fence line.
- **Dig up or flag your invisible dog fence wire.** This is the number one surprise of fence installs. The wire is buried an inch or two deep, it runs right where fence posts go, and once we hit it, the fence-building stops while the fence-fixing begins.
- **Clear the fence line of furniture, toys, planters, and garden decor.** Anything moveable should be moved before we arrive.
- **Plan for the dog.** If your dog is reactive or escape-prone, have a plan to keep them inside or kenneled on install days. An open fence line is an invitation. (We're dog people — we'd just rather meet yours at the end of the job, when the fence is already up.)
- **Think about access.** The crew needs to get materials to the yard — usually through a gate or the side of the house. Make sure the path is clear and wide enough for a wheelbarrow and several hundred pounds of lumber.
- **Tell us about buried anything.** Old wells, septic fields, French drains, landscape lighting wire — if it's in the ground and you know about it, we need to know about it.
- **Confirm the plan with the HOA if you have one.** Deed restrictions and HOA rules can be stricter than city code, and they override it in many cases. If your neighborhood requires architectural approval, get it before we dig, not after the fence is up.

These eight items take about an hour on a Saturday and save at least that much time on install day. They also protect you from the two most common mid-project surprises: a severed dog-fence wire and a sprinkler head that used to be exactly where a post is now.

## Step 7: Install Day — What Actually Happens

Let's walk through a typical install day for a residential wood privacy fence, because knowing what's happening — and why — makes the whole experience less mysterious.

**Early morning — layout and locate.** The crew arrives, walks the line, and confirms the marks: property corners, the MISS DIG flags, your sprinkler flags, the fence string line. Layout is where a good crew earns its keep. Post spacing is measured and marked — for most wood fences, posts go every 6 to 8 feet, with corner and gate posts placed first. Getting the layout right means the fence is straight, square, and exactly where you agreed it would be.

**Mid-morning — digging and post-setting.** This is the physical heart of the job. Post holes are dug with a power auger to a depth below Michigan's frost line — which is **42 inches** in most of the state. A post set at 42 inches or deeper, in a concrete footing with gravel at the bottom for drainage, is locked in place. A post set shallow is a fence that will heave and lean every winter as the ground freezes and thaws. This isn't a detail to skimp on — it's the difference between a fence that lasts twenty years and one that needs re-setting in five.

**Midday — concrete and framing.** Posts get set in concrete, braced plumb, and left to cure. While that happens, the crew starts on framing — the horizontal rails that carry the fence boards. For board-on-board (also called shadowbox) construction, boards are installed on alternating sides of the rails, which gives you privacy from both directions and lets air move through the fence. It's the style we recommend most often for Oakland County yards, because it handles wind and snow load beautifully.

**Afternoon — pickets, panels, and gates.** Boards go up, tops are trimmed level, and gates are hung. Gates are where precision really shows: a gate that sags six months after install is a gate that wasn't framed or braced right on day one. Every gate adds roughly 30 to 60 minutes of crew time — and it's time well spent.

**End of day — cleanup.** A professional crew leaves the site cleaner than they found it — scrap lumber stacked for pickup or hauled away, excess concrete removed, nails and debris swept up. You shouldn't have to spend your weekend cleaning up after a contractor.

**Final walkthrough.** Before the crew leaves, you walk the fence with the foreman. Check the post alignment, the board spacing, the gate swing, the latch. This is your chance to ask questions and flag anything that doesn't look right — and a contractor who stands by their work will walk it with you happily. That's the standard we hold ourselves to: when we hand over a fence, we want you to be able to see it's built to last.

## Step 8: How Long Does It Actually Take? (The Honest Numbers)

We gave you the full project timeline above, but let's break down the *installation* itself by fence type, because "how long does fence installation take" is one of the most-searched fence questions in the country — and the honest answer depends on what you're building. These numbers assume a two-person crew, reasonably flat terrain, no old fence to remove, and normal (non-rocky) soil:

| Fence Type | Linear Feet per Day | 200-Foot Project |
|---|---|---|
| Wood privacy (board-on-board) | 50-80 ft | 2.5-4 days |
| Wood picket | 60-90 ft | 2-3.5 days |
| Vinyl privacy panels | 80-120 ft | 1.5-2.5 days |
| Chain link (4 ft residential) | 100-150 ft | 1.5-2 days |
| Aluminum ornamental | 80-120 ft | 1.5-2.5 days |
| Composite | 60-100 ft | 2-3.5 days |
| Wrought iron | 40-60 ft | 3-5 days |

And here are the things that add time — worth knowing before you budget your expectations:

- **Old fence removal**: adds half a day to a full day for a 200-foot fence, and the old concrete footings are the real time-killer (15 to 30 minutes per post just to pull them).
- **Rocky or clay soil**: can add 40-50% to the digging time. Oakland County has plenty of both.
- **Slopes**: stepped fences take more layout and cutting time — roughly 30% more.
- **Tree roots**: a root system near the line means hand-digging or shifting posts.
- **Gates**: 30-60 minutes each, and worth every minute.
- **Weather**: crews don't install in active rain — concrete won't set right — and frozen ground in winter needs specialized equipment. That's why fall is such a sweet spot, more on that next.

## Step 9: The Fall Timing Advantage — Why Late Summer Is the Smart Time to Book

We're writing this in late August, and here's the honest truth about Michigan fence season: **fall is the best time to build a fence, and it's also when schedules fill up fast.**

- **Spring** (March-May) is peak demand — everyone waits out the winter and then calls at once. You'll be booking three to four weeks out, and the ground is often wet from snowmelt.
- **Summer** (June-August) is productive — long days, dry ground — but crews run at capacity and heat slows everyone down.
- **Fall** (September-November) is the sweet spot: the ground is still workable, the weather is comfortable, and the spring rush is over. Booking windows tighten to one to three weeks. A fence built in September has the whole winter to settle before heavy use in spring.
- **Winter** (December-February) is doable — and we do build in winter — but frozen ground requires heavier equipment, and the window is narrower.

## Step 10: After the Crew Leaves — First-Year Care

Your new fence is up. Congratulations. How you treat a new wood fence in year one largely determines how it looks in year ten:

- **Let the wood breathe.** Fresh cedar and pressure-treated pine both need time to dry before they take a finish well. Most manufacturers and contractors recommend waiting two to three months before staining or sealing new wood.
- **Watch for settling.** In the first year, posts and panels settle as the concrete cures and the ground compacts. A tiny bit of movement is normal. A gate that sticks slightly in humid weather is also normal — it usually corrects itself.
- **Don't pile anything against the fence.** Soil, mulch, or firewood stacked against the bottom of the boards traps moisture and starts rot at exactly the spot where rot does the most damage — ground level.
- **Know what "built to last" means for maintenance.** A wood fence in Michigan is a 15-to-30-year investment *with* care: a re-stain every few years, a seasonal check for loose boards and hardware, and prompt attention to any board that cracks or splits.

And one more thing: keep your paperwork. Store the contract, the permit — if your city issued one — the material specs, and any warranty information in a file. Ten years from now, when you want to add a gate or extend the fence, you'll know exactly what was installed and how.

## Step 11: Common Delays — and How to Avoid Every One of Them

The difference between a project that takes three weeks and one that takes three months is almost never the installation. It's almost always one of these:

1. **Skipping or delaying the property line check.** Building in the wrong place means stopping everything, moving the fence, or legal trouble. Fix it up front with a survey.
2. **Waiting on the permit.** If your city requires one, the clock doesn't start until the application is in. File early.
3. **Forgetting MISS DIG until the last minute.** A 2-10 business day locate lead time doesn't care about your schedule. Request it the day you sign the contract.
4. **Special-order materials.** If you want a specific composite color or a custom gate, order early. Stock cedar and pressure-treated pine move fast; everything else waits.
5. **The weather.** Michigan's summer storms and winter freezes are the two wildcards. Fall installation dodges both.
6. **The invisible dog fence.** The buried wire that nobody remembers is the single most common mid-project surprise we see. Flag it.

Every one of these is preventable with a little planning, which is exactly why the upfront steps in this guide matter.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does fence installation take in Michigan?
For a typical residential wood privacy fence, the installation itself takes 2.5 to 4 days for a 200-foot fence with a two-person crew. The full process — estimate, permits, utility locate, material ordering, and scheduling — typically runs 3 to 4 weeks from first call to finished fence, with a realistic range of 2 to 8 weeks depending on your city's permit process and the time of year.

### Do I need a permit to build a fence in Oakland County?
It depends on your city. Michigan's Residential Code exempts fences under seven feet from building permits, but individual municipalities set their own zoning rules — and most Metro Detroit communities, including several in Oakland County, require a fence permit for new installations. Check with your city or township building department, or ask your contractor to handle it as part of the project.

### What is MISS DIG 811 and do I need it for a fence?
MISS DIG 811 is Michigan's free utility notification center. When you request a locate, utility companies mark their buried gas, electric, water, sewer, and communication lines with flags and paint. It's required by Michigan law before any digging project — including fence posts — and it's the only way to safely know what's underground before you dig.

### How deep do fence posts need to be in Michigan?
At least 42 inches — Michigan's standard frost depth. Posts set at 42 inches or deeper in a concrete footing with gravel at the bottom are anchored below the freeze-thaw zone, so winter frost heave can't push them up. Posts set shallower will lean, heave, and fail within a few winters.

### What should I do to prepare my yard for fence installation?
Mow and trim the fence line, mark sprinkler heads, flag any invisible dog fence wire, clear the line of furniture and decor, plan for your dog during install days, ensure the crew has access to the yard, and tell your contractor about anything buried on the property.

### What happens on fence installation day?
The crew lays out the fence line, digs post holes below the frost line, sets posts in concrete, frames the rails, hangs the boards and gates, and cleans up. Most residential wood fences take two to four days, and a professional crew will walk the finished fence with you before they leave.

### Can a fence be built on the property line in Michigan?
Yes — Michigan doesn't mandate a setback for most residential fences, though many cities have their own placement rules and prohibit fences in the public right-of-way. The bigger issue is making sure you actually know where the line is: a survey is the only reliable answer, and a fence built on the wrong side of the line is an expensive mistake.

### Is fall a good time to install a fence in Michigan?
Fall is the best time. The ground is still workable, the weather is comfortable, the spring rush is over, and booking windows are shorter — one to three weeks. A fence built in the fall also gets a full winter to prove its frost-line footings before heavy use in spring.
