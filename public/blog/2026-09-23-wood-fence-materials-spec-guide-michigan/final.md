# Inside a Michigan Wood Fence: The Material Specs That Decide Whether It Lasts 15 Years or 30
*Published: September 23, 2026 | By BF Fence*

---
Most homeowners shop for a fence the way they shop for a car: by the name on the outside. Cedar. Six feet. Privacy. And that is exactly how two quotes for the "same" fence end up thousands of dollars apart — because the words on the outside of a fence are the only part of it that isn't engineered.

This is the conversation we have in driveways across Oakland County every fall. A quote is not a fence — it's a list of parts, and parts have specs. When one contractor writes "6-foot cedar privacy fence" at $32 a foot and another writes the same sentence at $44 a foot, one of them isn't overcharging. They're describing different objects. The difference lives in the ground, in the lumber grade, in the metal holding the boards on, and in how far apart the posts sit.

We've torn out enough failed fences to know which differences matter. Almost none die at year ten because of the wrong *style* — they die because of a post rating nobody read, a fastener nobody specified, or a spacing nobody questioned.

Here is the whole fence, taken apart. Use it to read your next quote line by line.

## The Spec Sheet, Part by Part

A wood privacy fence is not a single product. It's seven decisions stacked on each other, and each one has a right answer for Michigan — plus a cheaper answer that looks identical on the day it's installed.

| # | Component | The question that decides it | What "cheap" looks like | What "built to last" looks like |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Post rating** | What use category is the treated lumber rated for? | Any "treated" 4x4 from a stack | UC4A ground-contact rated, tag verified |
| 2 | **Post size & spacing** | How much wind will this fence catch? | 4x4 at 8 ft on every run | 4x4 at 8 ft on sheltered runs, 6x6 or 6 ft spacing on exposed sites and gates |
| 3 | **Picket grade** | How much heartwood, and how many knots? | Best-face graded, sapwood-heavy | No. 1 or better, heartwood-dominant |
| 4 | **Board size** | 4 in. or 6 in., and what thickness? | 5/8 in. thin pickets | Full-thickness boards, sized to the style |
| 5 | **Rails** | Two or three, and how heavy? | 2 rails, 2x3 | 3 rails on a 6-ft fence, 2x4 |
| 6 | **Fasteners** | What is the zinc doing, and how thick is it? | Electro-galvanized staples | Hot-dip galvanized ring-shank or stainless |
| 7 | **Footing & drainage** | Below the frost line, with water able to leave? | Bag of concrete, post on dirt | Belled footing 6 in. below the frost line, gravel base, crowned concrete |

If your quote answers all seven in writing, you're working with a professional.

## Start Underground: The Post Rating Decides Year Ten

The least visible and most consequential spec on any fence is the treatment rating of the posts — the one homeowners almost never check.

Pressure-treated lumber is not one product. It is graded into **use categories** by the American Wood Protection Association (AWPA), and those categories are printed on the end tag of every board:

| Use category | Where it's allowed | Can it go in the ground? |
|---|---|---|
| **UC3B** — above ground | Balusters, rails, handrails, lattice, some decking | **No.** Ground contact is specifically not allowed, and the material should stay at least six inches above grade |
| **UC4A** — ground contact | Fence posts, deck posts, landscape timbers | **Yes.** This is the correct rating for a fence post |
| **UC4B** — heavy-duty ground contact | Retaining walls, foundations, posts in constantly wet or acidic soil | Yes — heavier than a residential fence needs |

Read that first row again, because it is where a cheap fence is born: UC3B is treated lumber, sold in the same stack as posts, and it looks like a post — but it isn't rated to sit in Michigan soil, and every spring it spends in the ground is on borrowed time.

**How to verify it.** The end tag states the use category, the preservative system, and the retention in pounds per cubic foot (pcf). The preservative systems — ACQ, copper azole (CA-B, CA-C), and MCA — carry different retention numbers, which is why published tables disagree with each other. What matters to you: **"ground contact" on the tag, or UC4A, is the answer you want**, and UC4A posts typically carry retention in the range of 0.15 to 0.25 pcf depending on the system. If a contractor can't tell you the use category of the posts they're selling you, that's the most useful red flag you'll get all week.

Here's the honest cost of getting it wrong. A pressure-treated pine fence, well maintained, is typically good for **10 to 15 years** in a climate like ours; contractors regularly report replacing newer pressure-treated fences right in that window. Cedar, properly cared for, runs **15 to 20 years**. And the part that fails first, almost every time, is the bottom of the post — the twelve inches where soil contact, moisture, and oxygen all meet. A properly rated post in a properly drained hole isn't a finish detail. It is the entire lifespan of the project.

## The Post Itself: Size, Spacing, and the Wind You Don't See

Once the rating is right, the next two decisions are size and spacing — and in Michigan, both are really wind decisions in disguise.

**A solid privacy fence is a sail.** That isn't a figure of speech. Using ASCE 7 wind pressure relationships, a solid 6-foot privacy panel takes about **9.2 psf at 60 mph, 25.6 psf at 100 mph, and 36.9 psf at 120 mph**. Put that on a standard 8-foot section and the numbers get real fast:

| Wind speed | Pressure on a 6-ft solid fence | Lateral force per 8-ft section |
|---|---|---|
| 60 mph | 9.2 psf | 442 lb |
| 80 mph | 16.4 psf | 787 lb |
| **100 mph** | **25.6 psf** | **1,229 lb** |
| 120 mph | 36.9 psf | 1,771 lb |

An 8-foot section of 6-foot solid fence in a 100 mph gust carries **over half a ton** of sideways load, while the section itself weighs 96 to 120 pounds — a standard 6-foot dog-ear privacy fence with 5/8 in. pickets runs about **12 to 15 pounds per linear foot** of dead weight. The fence weighs a tenth of what the wind asks it to hold.

That load goes into the posts, which behave like cantilever beams: fixed at the bottom, free at the top, with **the post-to-ground connection as the weakest point in the whole system**. Which is why post spacing is the highest-leverage number on your quote.

| Post situation | Common spec | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Line posts, standard height | 4x4, 8 ft on center | Industry standard; 8 ft matches standard lumber lengths |
| Line posts, exposed or windy site | 4x6 or 6x6, 6–8 ft on center | Handles the load the standard spec doesn't |
| Board-on-board or heavier panels | 4x4, 6–8 ft on center | Overlapping boards add 30–40% more weight |
| Horizontal slat fences | 4x4 or 6x6, 6 ft on center | Shorter spans prevent board sag |
| Corner posts | Set 6 in. deeper, ~50% more concrete | Absorbs force from two directions at once |
| Gate posts | **6x6, set 4–6 in. deeper** | Carries the gate's constant lever arm |

Two field notes matter more than the table. First: **a walk gate hung on a 4x4 post will loosen that post inside two to three years** — it isn't luck; every swing loads the hinge post in a way a line post never experiences. Second: on an exposed site — a field edge, a lakefront, a hilltop, a corner lot with a wind funnel between houses — tightening spacing from 8 feet to 6 feet isn't an upsell. It's the difference between a fence that stands through a Michigan squall line and one that racks.

One more wind detail: a solid board fence blocks all the wind and takes all the load, while board-on-board and shadowbox fences let roughly **15 to 25% of the wind pass through** and cut the load by about the same percentage.

## Cedar Grades, Decoded

"we want cedar" is a material choice, not a grade choice — and grade is where the real money goes:

| Grade | What it means | Field reality |
|---|---|---|
| **Clear / Clear Vertical Grain** | No knots or imperfections; heartwood only. Vertical grain is sawn so the growth rings run parallel to the face and is less likely to twist | Premium. Beautiful, stable, expensive. Rarely necessary for a whole backyard |
| **No. 1 (also sold as "Select" or "#1 & Better")** | Fewer and smaller knots, cut from closer to the heart of the tree. Knots are tight "quality knots" less likely to fall out. Fewer color variations | The sweet spot for a Michigan privacy fence that's meant to last |
| **No. 2** | More and larger knots, more color variation. The market default when a quote just says "cedar" | Serviceable, but expect more pieces to loosen, split, and drop out over time |
| **No. 3 / Rustic** | Lowest fencing grade | Fence-grade in name only; expect to replace boards early |

Two grading details separate an honest quote from a clever one. **Best-face grading is the norm** — boards are graded on their best side, so a "No. 1" board can legitimately have a No. 2 back. The gap between best-face and *2-face graded* material is real money and invisible in a quote that just says "cedar." Ask which one you're getting; vague answer, assume the cheaper one.

The second is **heartwood versus sapwood**. Cedar's resistance to rot and insects lives in the heartwood. Higher grades are heartwood-dominant; lower grades carry more sapwood — which matters most at the bottom of the board, exactly where fences fail. A fence whose lower boards are sapwood-heavy has a scheduled failure date, even if the top looks fine.

## Board Size and Thickness: The Gap Story

Walk any ten-year-old wood fence in Oakland County and you'll hear the same complaint: the boards have shrunk and the fence is full of daylight. That isn't a cedar problem — it's a board-size and drying problem.

| Choice | Trade-off |
|---|---|
| **Wider boards (1x6)** | Cover more area per board, need fewer pieces, feel more solid. But wider boards move more as they dry, so gaps between them creep open more over the years |
| **Narrower boards (1x4)** | More dimensionally stable; gaps stay more consistent over the life of the fence. More boards needed, and they suit pattern and lattice-style designs |
| **Full thickness vs. thin pickets** | Thicker boards hold fasteners better, cup less, and feel like a fence when you lean on them. Thin 5/8 in. pickets are the standard low-cost option |
| **Kiln-dried vs. green** | Kiln-dried lumber has already given up most of the moisture it's going to lose, so it moves less after installation. Green lumber keeps shrinking on your fence |

The practical version: **expect some movement in any wood fence, and specify to keep it small.** Narrower boards move less, full-thickness boards resist cupping, and kiln-dried lumber has less moisture left to lose. Staining and sealing all six sides of every board sheds water far better than spraying the visible face — which is why the finish decision belongs in the material conversation, not two years later.

## Rails: The Most-Skipped Line on the Quote

Rails carry the pickets and stiffen the panel. They're also the line item most likely to quietly disappear from a quote, because a fence with two rails looks exactly like one with three rails until it's four years old.

| Fence height | Common rail count | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Up to about 4 ft | 2 rails | Top and bottom carry the pickets |
| Around 5 ft | 2–3 rails | The middle rail stiffens the panel and reduces board warping |
| **6 ft privacy** | **3 rails** | Keeps tall boards straight; roughly 2.5 ft between rails |
| Tall/8-ft panels | 3–4 rails | The extra rail supports the longer board span |

Placement matters as much as count. The **bottom rail sits about 6 to 10 inches above grade** — keeping wood out of the wet zone and leaving room to trim and maintain underneath. The **top rail sits within about 6 to 8 inches of the top of the boards**, where it supports the board ends without showing above them.

Why the middle rail isn't optional at six feet: it halves the unsupported span of every board. Tall, thin boards with only two support points cup and warp, and once a board cups it never comes back. On a Michigan fence that sees 40-degree temperature swings twice a year, the middle rail is the cheapest insurance on the project.

## Fasteners: Where Cheap Fences Rust First

Nobody ever asks about fasteners. Everybody eventually sees them fail.

| Fastener | Construction | Typical outdoor life | Where it belongs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Electro-galvanized** | Zinc electroplated, very thin — roughly 5–8 microns | **1–3 years before rust spots** | Indoor and dry applications only |
| **Hot-dip galvanized (HDG)** | Dipped in molten zinc; thick, metallurgically bonded coating, roughly 45–85 microns or more | **15–30+ years typical** | The outdoor standard: fences, decks, siding |
| **Stainless steel (304/316)** | No coating — the alloy itself resists corrosion | Decades, effectively indefinite | Coastal and marine conditions — and cedar |

The word "galvanized" on a box is not a specification. Electro-galvanizing deposits a zinc layer roughly **ten times thinner** than hot-dip, which is why so many "galvanized" fence staples are rusted within a few years. ASTM A153 governs hot-dip galvanized fasteners; ask which one your contractor uses.

There's a second reason this matters on cedar. **Cedar and redwood contain natural tannins — acids that react with galvanized hardware when wet.** The result is black staining around every fastener head, cosmetic at first and a corrosion site after that. Stainless avoids the reaction; hot-dip galvanized is the middle ground.

Then the nail-versus-screw question, worth about a nickel a fastener:

| | Ring-shank hot-dip galvanized nails | Exterior screws |
|---|---|---|
| Pull-out resistance | Ridged shank bites into the wood fibers; resists the seasonal expansion and contraction that pops smooth nails | Higher in static pull tests |
| Failure mode | Fewer head failures | Screw heads can snap where the head meets the shank, and screws are less forgiving when the fence moves in wind |
| Speed | Fast — installed with a nail gun | Slower |
| Fastener cost | Roughly **1 to 1.5 cents each** | Roughly **6 to 7 cents each** |
| Future repair | Harder to remove cleanly | Easy to back out and replace a picket |

Our spec: **hot-dip galvanized ring-shank nails for pickets and rails, and stainless on cedar where the budget allows.** We use screws where we want future serviceability — a gate brace, a panel we may need to open. What we never use is the thin electro-galvanized staple.

## Footings, Frost, and Water: The Michigan Details

Everything above ground is replaceable. The part in the ground is what the fence stands on, and Michigan is unforgiving about it.

- **Frost depth.** Michigan Residential Code R403.1.4 requires exterior footings to extend **42 inches below grade**, and Michigan frost depth runs **42 to 48 inches** depending on region. The right practice is to put the bottom of the footing at least **6 inches below** the published frost line.
- **Post depth in practice.** A 6-foot privacy fence commonly uses an **8-foot post with about 48 inches buried**. The old "bury one-third of the post" rule gives you about 36 inches on a six-footer — not deep enough in Michigan clay.
- **Belled footings.** A wider, flared base resists frost uplift and anchors against wind; high-wind specs commonly go to a 12-inch diameter with a bell at the bottom.
- **Crown the concrete.** Crowned and sloped away from the post so water sheds instead of pooling. Footings set slightly below grade with gravel at the base drain better than a flush collar that traps water.
- **Rot barrier at grade.** A sleeve over the top few inches above grade and the first few of burial is cheap insurance on the most vulnerable part of the fence.
- **Gate and corner posts go deeper.** Four to six inches deeper than line posts, every time. That's where the load concentrates.

The freeze-thaw cycle does the rest: water enters the soil, freezes, expands, and lifts, so a footing that's too shallow moves while one set deep enough doesn't care. If your posts are leaning in different directions, that isn't a fence problem — it's an underground problem, the one our [post repair guide for Michigan fences](/blog/2026-09-12-leaning-fence-posts-michigan-repair) walks through.

## Eight Questions to Ask Every Fence Contractor

Print this. Hand it to whoever quotes your fence.

1. **What use category are the posts rated for — UC3B or UC4A?** If they say "ground contact," ask to see the tag.
2. **Are the fence boards best-face graded or 2-face graded, and what grade?** "Cedar" is not an answer.
3. **How many rails, and what dimension?** On a six-foot privacy fence: three, and 2x4 beats 2x3.
4. **What fasteners — electro-galvanized, hot-dip galvanized, or stainless?** The first is an indoor fastener.
5. **How deep are the footings, and what's under them?** Below the frost line, gravel at the base, concrete crowned away from the post.
6. **How far apart are the posts, and does exposure change that?** A flat "eight feet everywhere" on an exposed site tells you wind load was never considered.
7. **What's the post spec at the gates and corners?** If gates get the same posts as the line, you're buying a gate that will sag.
8. **What does the estimate include for removal, disposal, and the permit?** Vague answers become change orders later.

Any contractor who answers all eight without flinching is worth their price. Any who gets defensive about two just told you something important.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does a wood fence last in Michigan?

A well-maintained pressure-treated pine fence typically lasts 10 to 15 years in Michigan. A cedar fence with proper care runs 15 to 20 years, and a properly specified cedar fence — ground-contact-rated posts, No. 1 heartwood-dominant boards, three rails, hot-dip galvanized or stainless fasteners, and footings below the frost line — can reach 25 years or more with restaining every three to four years. The post fails first almost every time, which is why the post rating matters more than the species.

### What grade of cedar should I buy for a fence?

For a Michigan privacy fence, cedar graded No. 1 — sometimes sold as Select or #1 & Better — is the right balance of longevity and cost. It comes from closer to the heart of the tree, has fewer and smaller knots that stay tight instead of falling out, and holds up better where moisture collects. No. 2 cedar is the market default when a quote simply says "cedar," and it shows more loose knots and more variation. Whatever grade you choose, ask whether the boards are best-face graded or 2-face graded — it's invisible in writing but real in money.

### What does "ground contact" mean on a treated fence post?

"Ground contact" is the plain-language version of AWPA use category UC4A, and it means the lumber is rated to sit in soil for its service life. Above-ground-rated material, UC3B, is treated but explicitly not allowed in ground contact and should stay at least six inches above grade. The end tag states the use category, the preservative system, and the retention in pounds per cubic foot. Retention requirements differ by preservative, so the use category is the reliable thing to check.

### Do I need stainless steel fasteners for a cedar fence?

Stainless is the safest choice, because cedar and redwood contain natural tannins that react with galvanized hardware when wet, causing black staining and corrosion around fastener heads. If stainless isn't in the budget, hot-dip galvanized fasteners are the widely used middle ground and last far longer than electro-galvanized. Remember that "galvanized" on a label doesn't tell you which process was used, and electro-galvanized hardware can rust within one to three years outdoors.

### How many rails should a 6-foot privacy fence have?

Three — a top, middle, and bottom rail, spaced roughly two and a half feet apart. Two rails are fine on fences up to about four or five feet, but on a six-foot privacy fence the middle rail halves the unsupported span of every board and keeps tall, thin boards from cupping and warping. The bottom rail sits about six to ten inches above grade, and the top rail within six to eight inches of the top of the boards.

### How far apart should fence posts be in Michigan?

Eight feet on center is the industry standard for most wood fence styles. In Michigan, though, spacing is really a wind decision. On exposed sites — open field edges, lakefronts, hilltops, and funneling corner lots — tighter spacing of six feet, or a larger post, or both, is the right call, because a solid six-foot panel in a 100 mph gust carries over 1,200 pounds of lateral force per eight-foot section. A contractor who quotes one spacing for every yard regardless of exposure hasn't considered wind load.

### Why are my fence boards shrinking and leaving gaps?

Wood moves as it dries — those gaps are boards that gave up moisture after installation. Wider boards such as 1x6 move more across their width than narrower 1x4 boards, which is why 1x4 pickets hold more consistent gaps over the life of a fence. Kiln-dried lumber has already released most of the moisture it's going to lose, so it moves less than green lumber. Staining and sealing all six sides of each board — not just the visible face — slows moisture cycling.

### Why does one fence quote cost less than another for the "same" fence?

Because the headline is the same and the spec isn't. One quote may be priced with above-ground-rated posts, best-face No. 2 cedar, two 2x3 rails, thin electro-galvanized staples, eight-foot post spacing, and shallow footings. The other may use ground-contact-rated posts, No. 1 heartwood-dominant boards, three 2x4 rails, hot-dip galvanized fasteners, tighter spacing on exposed runs, and footings set below the frost line. Both say "6-foot cedar privacy fence." The spec upgrades usually add 15 to 30% to the price and can double the lifespan, so the cheaper quote is often the more expensive fence over twenty years.
