## 1. Board-on-Board Privacy Fence

**The Standard. For Good Reason.**

This is the most popular wood fence style we install in Oakland County — and it's popular for every good reason you'd expect.

### What It Is
A board-on-board fence is built with vertical pickets installed on both sides of the horizontal rails, overlapping each other. From the outside (or the inside), you see a solid wall of wood with no gaps. The "board-on-board" name comes from the construction method: the face boards are installed first, then a second layer of boards is installed behind them, covering the gaps between the first layer.

### What It Looks Like
From either side, it's a clean, continuous wood surface. No gaps, no peek-through. It looks solid from the street and solid from your backyard — which matters because in most neighborhoods, your neighbors are looking at the "good" side of your fence. A board-on-board fence eliminates that problem entirely since both sides look finished.

### Best Wood Choice
**Pressure-treated pine** is the go-to for board-on-board privacy fences. It's cost-effective, accepts stain beautifully, and when properly rated for ground contact (UC4A minimum — and for posts, we actually prefer UC4B for the extra protection), it holds up remarkably well in Michigan's freeze-thaw cycles. **Cedar** is an excellent premium upgrade if you want the natural beauty and rot resistance that comes with it — though expect to pay 20–30% more.

### Best For
- **Privacy**: Complete and total. This is your best option if your backyards are close together or you have a busy street behind your property.
- **Wind blocking**: A solid board-on-board fence creates a very effective windbreak for your backyard.
- **Security**: No gaps means no visibility into your yard from the outside.
- **Noise reduction**: While no fence is a soundproof wall, a dense board-on-board fence does take the edge off road noise.

### Oakland County Considerations
This style works well on any lot size, but it's especially popular on quarter-acre and smaller lots in Rochester Hills, Auburn Hills, and Troy where homes sit closer together. You'll want to make sure your fence height complies with local zoning — 6 feet for rear and side yards is standard in most Oakland County municipalities.

**Estimated cost: $30–$45 per linear foot installed** (pressure-treated pine)

### What We See in the Field
After six, seven, eight years, board-on-board fences in pressure-treated pine that have been properly stained or sealed still look sharp. The boards age evenly, and any minor warping is hidden by the overlapping construction. This is the style that ages the most gracefully.

## 2. Shadowbox (Good Neighbor) Fence

**The Diplomat of Fences.**

If you share a property line with a neighbor and you both want something that looks great from both sides without the full material cost of board-on-board, the shadowbox is your fence.

### What It Is
Also called a "good neighbor" fence, the shadowbox uses vertically installed pickets alternated on opposite sides of the horizontal rails. From a distance, it looks nearly solid. Up close, you'll see small gaps between the overlapping boards — about a half-inch to an inch, depending on how it's built.

### What It Looks Like
This is where the shadowbox earns its reputation. From either side of the property line, you see a finished face. There's no "good side" and "bad side" the way there is with stockade or stockade-style fences. The gaps create a subtle shadow effect — hence the name — that gives the fence visual depth and character.

### Best Wood Choice
**Cedar** is our top recommendation for shadowbox fences. The natural color variation in cedar boards enhances the shadow effect beautifully, and cedar's resistance to rot and insects means the alternating pickets all age at a similar rate. Pressure-treated pine works fine too (and costs less), but cedar is the material where this style really shines.

### Best For
- **Shared property lines**: When you and your neighbor are both contributing to the fence cost or both need it to look good from their side.
- **Airflow**: The small gaps between boards allow wind to pass through slightly, reducing the pressure on the fence during strong storms — a real advantage during those Michigan spring squalls.
- **Curb appeal**: The shadow effect gives the fence a more designed, intentional look compared to a plain solid panel.

### Oakland County Considerations
Some Auburn Hills and Rochester HOAs actually prefer shadowbox fences because they present a uniform appearance on both sides — which maintains neighborhood consistency. If you're in an HOA-gated community or a planned development, check your association's architectural guidelines before committing to a specific style.

**Estimated cost: $35–$50 per linear foot installed** (cedar)

### What We See in the Field
Shadowbox fences in cedar age beautifully, but the gaps between boards mean you should plan to re-stain a bit more frequently — every 2–3 years instead of 3–4 — to keep the wood equally protected on both faces. The good news? This fence is easy to maintain because access to both sides of every board is straightforward.

## 3. Dog-Ear Picket Fence

**Classic American Curb Appeal.**

When someone says "white picket fence," they're usually picturing something close to a dog-ear fence. It's the style that says "welcome home" without saying a word.

### What It Is
The dog-ear picket fence gets its name from the shape of the picket tops — the corners are cut off at a 45-degree angle, creating a truncated point that resembles a folded-down dog ear. The pickets are typically spaced evenly with gaps between them (usually 1 to 3 inches), mounted on horizontal rails.

### What It Looks Like
Clean, traditional, and friendly. The dog-ear cut gives the top line of the fence a rhythmic visual pattern that's more interesting than a flat-topped fence but less ornate than a scalloped design. It's a style that works with practically any home architecture — ranch, colonial, Cape Cod, modern farmhouse.

### Best Wood Choice
**Cedar** is the traditional choice for picket fences, and for good reason — it's naturally resistant to rot and insects, takes stain exceptionally well, and has a warm, inviting color that fits the aesthetic. Pressure-treated pine works too and is more budget-friendly, but cedar is where this style looks and ages best.

### Best For
- **Front yards**: This is by far the most popular application. The dog-ear picket defines your property line without blocking sightlines, which is exactly what you want at the street.
- **Containing pets and small children**: The gaps keep the fence from acting as a wind sail (important in open areas), and the height is usually sufficient to keep a dog in or keep small kids from wandering.
- **Decorative boundary definition**: When you want to mark your property without creating a visual wall.

### Oakland County Considerations
Dog-ear pickets are the style most likely to be welcomed (or even required) by front-yard design guidelines. In Rochester and Troy especially, older neighborhoods have established architectural traditions, and the dog-ear picket fits right in. Many Oakland County municipalities have specific height restrictions for front-yard fences — 4 feet is the standard — so factor that into your planning.

**Estimated cost: $25–$35 per linear foot installed** (pressure-treated pine)

### What We See in the Field
Picket fences get more direct exposure to foot traffic, lawn maintenance equipment, and street-level elements than backyard privacy fences. The pickets nearest your driveway will show more wear from car doors, trash cans, and foot traffic. We recommend using galvanized or stainless steel fasteners on picket fences — they hold up much better in the salt and grit that Michigan winters bring.

## 4. Scalloped Fence

**Where Design Meets Function.**

The scalloped fence takes the basic picket or privacy design and adds a wave-like curve along the top line. It's not just decorative — the curve has a practical function in certain yard layouts.

### What It Is
A scalloped fence follows the contour of a slightly sloped yard by creating a gentle wave pattern along the top edge. The boards follow the grade, rising and falling with the land, creating a curved "scallop" effect between each post. This is different from a fence that steps down rigidly at each post — the scallop creates a flowing, organic appearance.

### What It Looks Like
Elegant and custom-built. The gentle curves along the top of the fence give it a handcrafted look that instantly elevates your property's aesthetic. It reads as intentional and well-designed, not off-the-rack.

### Best Wood Choice
**Cedar** is the preferred material for scalloped fences because it's lighter and easier to shape into the curves. The natural beauty of cedar also complements the more decorative nature of this style. Premium western red cedar gives the highest-end result.

### Best For
- **Gently sloped lots**: Many Oakland County properties have subtle grade changes. The scalloped design accommodates these naturally without awkward stepping or large gaps at the bottom.
- **Front yards with architectural character**: If your home has curved rooflines, arched entries, or other flowing design elements, a scalloped fence echoes that shape beautifully.
- **Garden areas and flower beds**: The varying height of a scalloped fence creates visual niches that work wonderfully as planting borders.

### Oakland County Considerations
Properties around Lake St. Clair and in the hilly areas of northern Oakland County (Clarkston, Lake Orion) often have enough natural grade variation to make scalloped fences look right at home. On flat lots, the scallop is purely aesthetic — and a lovely one, but worth understanding the trade-off.

**Estimated cost: $40–$55 per linear foot installed** (cedar)

### What We See in the Field
Scalloped fences require more precision in layout and cutting. Each board needs to be measured and cut to follow the curve, and the result is only as good as the measurement. This is one of those styles where hiring a professional isn't just a recommendation — it's pretty much required. A DIY scalloped fence almost never looks as clean as a professionally built one.

## 5. Horizontal Slat Fence

**Modern, Minimal, Bold.**

If you're looking for something that breaks away from traditional vertical fencing, the horizontal slat fence delivers a contemporary look that stands out in any neighborhood.

### What It Is
Instead of vertical pickets, the horizontal slat fence uses boards running left to right, mounted against vertical posts. The slats can be installed with gaps between them (for a semi-private look), or tightly packed for near-complete privacy. Many designs incorporate metal post accents or mixed materials for an even more modern aesthetic.

### What It Looks Like
Sleek, clean lines. A horizontal slat fence is the architectural equivalent of a well-tailored suit — it says contemporary without being trendy in a way that dates quickly. The horizontal lines draw the eye across your property, making narrow lots feel wider.

### Best Wood Choice
**Tropical hardwoods** like ipe are the premium choice for horizontal slat fences — they're incredibly dense, naturally rot-resistant, and have a rich, dark color that looks stunning. But in Oakland County (and given the cost of ipe), **cedar** is the practical recommendation. Cedar's natural straightness and dimensional stability make it well-suited for horizontal installation, and it's significantly more affordable.

We do not recommend pressure-treated pine for horizontal slat fences. The chemical treatment can cause uneven coloration across the wide face of horizontal boards, and PT pine is more prone to cupping — which is more visible on horizontal installations.

### Best For
- **Modern and mid-century home architecture**: If your home has clean lines, large windows, or contemporary landscaping, a horizontal slat fence is the natural complement.
- **Small properties**: The horizontal lines visually expand a small yard.
- **Commercial properties**: Offices, restaurants, and retail spaces in Oakland County are increasingly choosing horizontal slat fences for their modern, approachable look.

### Oakland County Considerations
This is a growing trend in Oakland County, especially in newer developments and renovated properties near downtown Birmingham, Rochester, and Troy. Some older neighborhoods and HOAs may have restrictions that favor traditional vertical styles, so always check before committing.

**Estimated cost: $45–$65 per linear foot installed** (cedar)

### What We See in the Field
Horizontal slat fences with proper post spacing (6 feet on center maximum) hold up well in Michigan's climate. The main thing to watch is water pooling — horizontal boards can trap water between the slats if not installed with adequate drainage gaps. We always recommend a small gap between slats to let water drain and air circulate.

## 6. Stockade Fence

**Budget Privacy Without Sacrificing Function.**

The stockade fence is one of the oldest wood fence styles in America, and it remains one of the most practical choices for homeowners who need privacy on a budget.

### What It Is
A stockade fence is built with closely spaced vertical boards (called "pickets") installed flush against each other on one side of the horizontal rails. The boards typically have rounded or flat tops. Unlike board-on-board, there's only one layer of pickets — no overlapping second layer.

### What It Looks Like
From the "good" side (the side without visible rails), a stockade fence presents a continuous, solid appearance very similar to board-on-board. The difference is on the other side — you'll see the horizontal rails and the back of the pickets. This is why stockade fences are sometimes described as having a "good side" and a "bad side."

### Best Wood Choice
**Pressure-treated pine** dominates in stockade fence construction. It's affordable, strong, and when the pickets are installed tightly together with proper fastening, a PT pine stockade fence will deliver solid privacy performance for 10–15 years. Cedar stockade fences exist but are relatively rare because the style doesn't showcase cedar's premium qualities the way shadowbox or board-on-board does.

### Best For
- **Budget-conscious homeowners**: A stockade fence delivers privacy at one of the lowest per-foot costs available in wood fencing.
- **Side yard applications**: Where only one side of the fence is visible (yours), stockade is perfectly adequate.
- **Rental properties and large lots**: Where you need a lot of linear feet of privacy fencing without premium material costs.

### Oakland County Considerations
Stockade fences are common throughout Oakland County, particularly in neighborhoods where fence height requirements for backyard privacy are the primary consideration. The "good side faces outward" convention applies here — when you're sharing a fence line, both parties should agree on orientation beforehand.

**Estimated cost: $25–$40 per linear foot installed** (pressure-treated pine)

### What We See in the Field
Stockade fences are often the first to show gaps as the wood shrinks and warps with Michigan's seasonal changes. The pickets pull away from each other over time, creating small gaps that weren't there on day one. This is normal, but it's worth knowing. A well-built stockade fence with properly dried lumber will minimize this effect. Regular staining helps keep the wood stable and reduces shrinkage.

## 7. Split-Rail Fence

**Open Property, Clear Boundaries.**

If your Oakland County property is on the larger side — say, half an acre or more — and you want to define your boundary or contain livestock without blocking views, split-rail is the style you're looking for.

### What It Is
A split-rail fence is exactly what it sounds like: rough-sawn rails (usually split from logs into rails) that are inserted into holes in upright posts, typically in a zigzag interlocking pattern that requires no nails, screws, or hardware. It's one of the oldest fence designs in American history.

### What It Looks Like
Rustic, open, and pastoral. A split-rail fence defines a boundary without visually enclosing the space. It complements large lots, wooded properties, and rural areas where privacy isn't the goal but demarcation is.

### Best Wood Choice
**Cedar** is the traditional material for split-rail fence. Its natural rot resistance and dimensional stability make it ideal for a fence that relies on friction and gravity to hold itself together — no hardware means the wood itself needs to resist decay. Cedar split-rail is available in both rough-sawn and sanded grades.

### Best For
- **Large properties**: When your lot size is measured in acres, not feet.
- **Rural or semi-rural Oakland County properties**: Think northern Oakland County townships, near Lake Orion or Clarkston.
- **Equestrian properties**: If you're keeping horses or other livestock, split-rail is the standard.
- **Ornamental boundary definition**: Where you want the visual line without any obstruction of sight or wind.

### Oakland County Considerations
Split-rail is less common in Auburn Hills and Rochester proper due to smaller lot sizes, but it's popular in the northern parts of Oakland County. If you're in a more developed suburban area, this style may feel out of place with your neighbors' stockade and board-on-board privacy fences.

**Estimated cost: $12–$20 per linear foot installed** (cedar, 3-rail)

### What We See in the Field
Split-rail fences are essentially maintenance-free. There are no nails to rust, no boards to picket-space, and no privacy panels to rot at ground level. After 15–20 years, the rails will naturally gray with weathering and some may need replacing, but this style has the lowest lifetime maintenance cost of any wood fence we install.

## How to Choose the Right Fence Style for Your Oakland County Home

### Start With What You Actually Need

Before you get caught up in aesthetics, answer these questions:

1. **Is privacy your priority?** If yes, board-on-board or shadowbox is the answer.
2. **Are you trying to contain a pet or child?** A solid privacy fence or a closely spaced picket fence works.
3. **Is curb appeal at the front of the house the main goal?** Dog-ear picket or horizontal slat (if your architecture supports it).
4. **Do you need to define a large property boundary?** Split-rail.
5. **Are you on a budget but still need privacy?** Stockade.
6. **Do you share the fence cost with a neighbor?** Shadowbox or board-on-board (both sides look good).

### Factor in Michigan's Climate

This is the part that national fence companies and out-of-state blogs don't address well. Michigan is unforgiving. We get 60–100 freeze-thaw cycles per winter. Our frost line is 42 inches deep. That means every fence style needs to account for posts being set deep, lumber being properly dried and rated, and the wood itself being species that survive seasonal moisture swings.

**Pressure-treated pine rated for ground contact (UC4A or UC4B)** is the most climate-appropriate budget option. **Cedar** is the most climate-appropriate premium option. Avoid untreated spruce, fir, or whitewood — they simply do not survive Michigan winters.

### Check Your Local Requirements

Oakland County municipalities each have their own fence ordinances:

- **Auburn Hills**: Requires permits for fence installation; 6-foot maximum for rear/side yards, 4-foot for front yards.
- **Rochester Hills**: Similar requirements; additional HOA guidelines may apply in planned communities.
- **Troy**: Permits required; specific setback requirements from property lines.
- **Birmingham**: More restrictive, especially in historic districts — always check before selecting a style.

Call the city or township building department before you finalize your plans. Or call us at (248) 604-6168 and we'll handle the permitting for you as part of the project.

## Common Mistakes Oakland County Homeowners Make When Choosing a Fence Style

### Mistake 1: Picking a Style That Doesn't Match the House

A horizontal slat fence on a Victorian in Rochester looks as out of place as a split-rail fence on a contemporary in Troy. Your fence should complement your home's architectural style. If you're not sure, send us a photo of your house — Justin and Chris will tell you honestly which styles work and which don't.

### Mistake 2: Ignoring the Neighbor Factor

Fences go on property lines. Even if you're paying for the entire fence, your neighbor lives with it. A shadowbox or board-on-board fence eliminates neighbor conflicts because both sides look good. If you're sharing the expense, make the decision together.

### Mistake 3: Choosing the Cheapest Material for the Style

A shadowbox fence built with untreated spruce will look great on day one and look terrible within two years. Match the material to the style. Cedar for premium styles. Properly rated PT pine for budget-conscious installations. Never use materials not rated for ground-contact applications in posts.

### Mistake 4: Not Account for Grade Changes

If your yard slopes — and many Oakland County yards do — a flat-topped fence will leave visible gaps at the high end and bury the low end in dirt. Scalloped fencing or stepping the fence is the solution. Don't try to force a flat top on a sloped lot.

### Mistake 5: Skipping the Permit

Yes, there's a permit process. Yes, it takes a little extra time. If you build without a permit, you risk being required to remove and rebuild the fence at your own cost. We handle permits on every project — there's no reason to skip it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the #1 best wood fence style for Oakland County homes?

For the majority of homeowners in Oakland County, **board-on-board in pressure-treated pine** delivers the best overall value when you balance privacy, durability, aesthetics, and cost. If you want the highest-end look and your budget allows, board-on-board in cedar is our top recommendation.

### Cedar vs. pressure-treated pine — which is better for Michigan?

Cedar is the premium choice. It's naturally resistant to rot and insects, has a beautiful appearance, and ages gracefully. Pressure-treated pine is the practical budget choice. It's chemically protected against rot and insects, costs significantly less, and takes stain well. Both are good options for Michigan. The wrong choice is untreated spruce, fir, or white pine — these simply won't last.

### Do I need a permit for a wood fence in Oakland County?

In most Oakland County cities and townships — including Auburn Hills, Rochester Hills, Troy, and Birmingham — **yes, you need a permit** for fence installation. The permit typically includes an inspection to verify proper post depth, setback compliance, and height limits. BF Fence handles the permit process for you on every project.

### What fence styles are HOA-friendly in Oakland County?

Most Oakland County HOAs allow board-on-board, shadowbox, and dog-ear picket styles. Horizontal slat and split-rail are more likely to require pre-approval, and some HOAs restrict fence height, color, and even material type. Always check your HOA's architectural guidelines before finalizing your plans.

### How often should I stain or seal my wood fence in Michigan?

Pressure-treated pine: every 3–4 years. Cedar: every 2–3 years. This is one of the most important maintenance steps you can take — it protects the wood from moisture, UV damage, and the freeze-thaw cycles that cause the most damage in Michigan. A well-maintained fence lasts nearly twice as long as an unmaintained one.

### Can I mix fence styles on the same property?

Absolutely. A very common approach in Oakland County is a dog-ear picket fence in the front yard (for curb appeal) and a board-on-board privacy fence in the backyard (for privacy). The transition between styles at the corner of your house just needs a clean post and consistent material.
