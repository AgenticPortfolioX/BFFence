# What's Eating Your Michigan Wood Fence? Termites, Carpenter Ants, and Rot — and How to Stop Them

*Published: 2026-10-07 | By BFFence*

---

A wood fence is one of the few things you buy for a Michigan home that's supposed to be ignored. You want to look up in ten years and see a straight, solid line — not lean on a post and feel it give. Yet the failures we get called out to fix almost never happen all at once. They start in one wet spot at the bottom of one post, and they work upward from there.

So here's the honest version of what actually attacks a wood fence in Michigan, what it looks like long before the fence is obviously failing, and — most importantly — the building and maintenance choices that make a fence a poor meal for the things that want to eat it.

As Oakland County's Trusted Choice for custom wood fencing, we've dug up our share of soft posts and pried off our share of boards with carpenter ants behind them. This is the field guide we wish every homeowner had before their fence went in.

## The One Rule That Explains Every Failing Fence

If you remember nothing else from this page, remember this: **rot and wood-destroying pests both need moisture.** Take away the water and you starve the entire problem.

Wood-decay fungi — the organisms that turn a solid cedar post into something you can push a screwdriver through — need four things to operate: oxygen, a food source (the wood itself), temperatures above freezing, and **moisture content above roughly 20 percent.** Below that threshold, fungal activity "stops almost entirely." That number isn't a guess; it's the operating line that forest-products researchers and preservation engineers have used for decades.

Termites are no different. Michigan State University's diagnostics team puts it plainly: subterranean termites "prefer wood of high moisture content," and severe damage is "often concentrated in areas where excessive moisture conditions persist." The very building habits that feed rot — boards in direct contact with soil, poor drainage, grade that slopes toward the structure — are also the habits that invite termites.

This is why the rest of this article keeps circling back to the same principle. A dry fence is a durable fence. Everything else — species, treatment, stain, inspection — is just how you keep it dry.

## Michigan's Real Threat List: Three Pests (and One Fungus)

National pest-control articles will hand you a list of a dozen creatures that theoretically like wood. Michigan's actual threat list for a fence is shorter and more specific. Here's what you're really dealing with.

### Eastern Subterranean Termites: The State's Most Serious Wood-Destroyer

Michigan has exactly one economically important termite, and it's a serious one. The **eastern subterranean termite** (*Reticulitermes flavipes*) is, in MSU Extension's words, "the most serious wood destroying pest in Michigan and… much more common than most people realize."

Two details matter enormously for a homeowner:

**First, where they are.** MSU's extension literature documents Michigan termite pressure as "limited to the two lower tiers of counties along the southern line of the state." Oakland County sits squarely inside that band. If your fence is in Auburn Hills, Rochester Hills, Troy, Clarkston, Lake Orion, or anywhere else in our corner of Metro Detroit, termites are a real, local possibility — not a Florida problem.

**Second, how they operate.** Subterranean termites must return to a soil nest to replenish moisture, so they build **shelter tubes** — those raised, earthy, mud-colored lines — to travel between soil and wood without drying out. They eat wood from the inside out, leaving a thin skin of sound-looking surface behind. A 60,000-termite colony consumes only about the equivalent of a two-foot length of 2x4 in a year, which is why termite damage is almost always discovered late.

Now the honest part: a fence is a **lower-priority target than your house.** Termites go for the structure first. But a fence post sitting in wet soil, with wood-to-ground contact and no drainage, is exactly the kind of easy, permanently-wet food source that supports a colony and gives it a staging area near your home. Which is why the same dry-build principles that protect your house protect your fence.

### Carpenter Ants: They Don't Eat Wood — They Tunnel Through It

Carpenter ants get blamed for "eating" fences, and that's the wrong picture. They don't eat wood at all. They **excavate** it — chewing out galleries and tunnels to build nests, then leaving behind sawdust-like debris called frass.

What draws them in is moisture. Michigan's damp climate and our large stock of older housing create ideal nesting conditions, and wet, softening wood is a standing invitation. A fence post with a cracked top, a joint holding water, or a base that never dries is precisely where a carpenter ant colony will set up. Left alone long enough, the tunnels hollow the wood from the inside, and the post stops doing its job.

It's also worth knowing the tell that separates them from termites: carpenter ants are large, black or red-and-black, and you'll often find coarse frass and clean, smooth-sided galleries. Termites are small, pale, soft-bodied, and leave mud in their tunnels instead of clean wood.

### Powderpost Beetles: The Fine-Dust Tell

Powderpost beetles are the quiet third pest. They bore into seasoned wood — often the wood that's already been milled and installed — and their signature is unmistakable once you know it: **a fine, talc-like powder** sifting out of small, round exit holes and piling up beneath.

Their damage is slower and more localized than a termite colony's, but a heavy infestation can hollow out boards enough to require replacement. Dry, unfinished wood that's held moisture at some point in its life is the usual victim — which again points back to water management.

### Telling Them Apart at a Glance

The three pests leave different evidence, and the difference decides who you call. Here's the short version:

| Clue | Subterranean termites | Carpenter ants | Powderpost beetles |
|---|---|---|---|
| What they do to wood | Eat it, from the inside out | Tunnel to nest; don't eat it | Bore in as larvae |
| Evidence left behind | Mud / shelter tubes; paper-thin surface skin | Coarse sawdust (frass); clean hollow galleries | Fine, talc-like powder from small round holes |
| Wood they prefer | Wet, connected to soil | Wet, softening | Seasoned, previously damp |
| Who handles it | Licensed pest professional | Licensed pest professional | Often just replace the board; treat if widespread |

One honest caution: the surface of a termite-damaged board can look perfectly sound right up until it crumbles under your hand. Evidence — not appearance — is what you act on.

### Rot: The Slow, Silent Home-Wrecker

Rot doesn't sting or bite, so it never makes the scary list. It should. In a Michigan fence, decay fungi do more total damage than every insect combined, and they do it invisibly.

The decay organisms need that 20-percent moisture threshold, and a fence gives them moisture in predictable places: the **ground line**, where soil holds water against the post; the **cut end grain**, which drinks water like a straw; and any **joint or fastener hole** where water sits after rain. Sealer alone will not save an untreated post in those spots — because sealer wears, and water is patient.

## "But It's Cedar — Cedar Doesn't Rot": The Heartwood Myth

This is the single most expensive misconception in wood fencing, and it costs homeowners thousands every year.

Cedar has a genuine natural advantage. Its **heartwood** — the darker, dense inner core of the tree — contains compounds called **thujaplicins** that make it far more rot- and insect-resistant than pine or fir. That's real, and it's why cedar is a premium fence wood.

But cedar is **not termite-proof, and it is not rot-proof.** Two things routinely defeat its natural chemistry:

**1. Sapwood has almost no resistance.** The pale outer layer of the tree carries little to none of the protective chemistry. A "cedar" board or post is often a mix of heartwood and sapwood, and the sapwood portion can begin to decay within just a few years in ground contact. Species name on the tag tells you very little about heart content — grade and cut do.

**2. Cut ends and weathered surfaces bypass the protection.** Every time a post is cut, notched, or drilled, you expose raw, unprotected wood. Those cut ends soak up water faster than any other surface. Left untreated, they're where decay starts.

So the accurate statement is this: **cedar heartwood is naturally durable; cedar sapwood is not, and cedar is not a substitute for either ground-contact-rated lumber or dry construction.** A cedar post set in wet clay with an untreated cut end will rot. We've pulled them out.

## Where Fences Actually Die: The Ground Line

If you want to know why a fence failed, you don't need to inspect the whole fence. Inspect the bottom six inches of every post.

Fence posts rot at the soil line because that's where all four of decay's requirements — oxygen, wood, warmth, and moisture — happen at once, and stay that way. That's the same reason the wood industry treats **ground contact** as its own special category of use.

The American Wood Protection Association (AWPA) rates treated lumber by **Use Category**, and the rating is printed on the end tag. For a fence, two categories matter:

| AWPA Use Category | Where it applies | ACQ retention | Copper azole (CA-C) retention |
|---|---|---|---|
| UC3B — above ground | Fence pickets and rails, deck boards, siding | 0.15 pcf | 0.06 pcf |
| UC4A — ground contact | **Fence posts**, deck posts, garden boxes | 0.40 pcf | 0.15 pcf |
| UC4B — heavy duty | Permanent wood foundations, severe or critical use | 0.60 pcf | 0.31 pcf |

The number that matters most for posts is **UC4A or higher** — roughly two to three times the preservative retention of above-ground lumber. Posts that touch soil, concrete, debris, vegetation, or anything that stays damp need UC4A **at minimum**.

Here's the trap: **UC3B above-ground lumber is frequently used for fence posts because it's cheaper, and it is not rated for soil.** You can't tell by the color or the weight. Only the end tag tells you. "Ground contact" rated stock is not an upsell — it's the difference between a post that lasts and a post that feeds fungus.

## The Build Choices That Starve Rot and Insects Before They Start

You can't spray, stain, or seal your way out of bad construction. You design the problem out. These are the choices that matter, and they're the ones a real contractor makes without being asked.

### 1. Use the right post — ground-contact-rated, or a naturally durable species

For posts that live in the ground, specify **UC4A ground-contact-rated** lumber and check the tag, or use a genuinely durable species. Naturally rot-resistant options include **cedar heartwood, redwood heartwood, and cypress**, and at the top of the heap for untreated in-ground use are **black locust** and **Osage orange**, which can outlast treated pine in field trials. Be aware that cedar posts — sapwood especially — can rot faster in the ground than properly treated pine. For most Michigan yards, a **ground-contact-rated treated post paired with cedar or treated boards** is the smart, honest build: durability underground, beauty above it.

### 2. Set the post in a footing that drains

Water must have somewhere to go. A post hole that collects water is a rot incubator, and even a treated post fails when water pools at the bottom. Post-setting details — the depth, the material, and especially the drainage at the base — matter as much as the post itself. Done right, the footing keeps the post stable through Michigan's freeze-thaw cycles *and* keeps water moving away from the wood.

### 3. Grade and drain away from the fence

One of the most common causes of premature fence failure isn't the fence at all — it's the yard. Soil that slopes toward the fence, a downspout that dumps at the base of a post, or an irrigation head that sprays the same boards every morning all create a permanently wet zone. Redirect water. Fix the grade. Move the sprinkler.

### 4. Leave an air gap — no wood in contact with soil

Wood that touches soil, mulch, leaves, or vegetation stays wet. A fence built with the bottom board **clear of the ground** dries out between rains, and that airflow is one of the most effective rot preventives there is. Never pile soil or mulch against the bottom of a fence, and keep vegetation from growing up through it.

### 5. Treat every cut end

This is the smallest step with the biggest return. Every time lumber is cut, drilled, or notched on site, the exposed wood loses its factory protection. **Brushing every cut end with copper naphthenate** — a proven preservative for posts of all species — restores that protection at the exact spot where water enters fastest. It takes seconds per board and can add years to the fence. (Borate products, which are popular for interior framing, are the wrong tool here: they leach out of exterior and ground-contact wood.)

### 6. Use the right fasteners

Modern treated lumber uses copper-based preservatives, which corrode bare or electro-plated steel. Use **hot-dip galvanized or stainless steel** fasteners and hardware. A fence held together by nails that rust away in a decade will start to sag and gap long before the wood itself fails — and open joints hold water, which starts the whole decay cycle again.

## The October Walkthrough: How to Inspect Your Own Fence

Early fall is the best time of year to inspect a Michigan wood fence, and it's not close. Summer's growth is done, the leaves are dropping and revealing the line, the soil is wet from fall rain, temperatures are still warm enough to expose problems, and insects are actively searching for overwintering sites. Walk your fence with a screwdriver and a flashlight, and go post by post. Here's what to look for.

| What you see | What it usually means | Urgency |
|---|---|---|
| Post gives when you push sideways | Soft wood below grade or a failing footing | High — fix before freeze |
| Raised, earthy mud lines on or near a post | Subterranean termite shelter tubes | High — call a pest pro |
| Fine powder under small round holes | Powderpost beetles | Medium — monitor/treat |
| Coarse sawdust, clean hollow galleries | Carpenter ants | Medium–high |
| Soft, spongy, discolored wood at the base | Active rot at the ground line | High |
| Mushrooms, mildew, or dark staining | Long-term moisture; decay in progress | High |
| Bubbling or peeling finish over soft wood | Paint/film trapping water over rot | High |
| Loose, gapped, or wavy boards | Fastener failure or board decay | Medium |

**The screwdriver test is the whole game.** Push a screwdriver into the bottom six inches of a post and into any cut end or joint. If it sinks in with little resistance, the wood is already compromised — the surface can look fine while the inside is gone. Do this in a few spots per post, especially anywhere soil, mulch, or a downspout touches the fence.

## Real Fixes for a Fence That's Already in Trouble

If your inspection turns up problems, the fix depends on how far the damage has gone — and it's worth being honest about the difference between a repair and a rebuild.

**Small, isolated rot or a surface issue:** remove anything holding moisture against the wood, correct the drainage, and treat exposed or cut wood with copper naphthenate. This stops the process if the structural wood is still sound.

**A soft cut end or a board edge:** cut back to sound wood, treat the new cut, and replace the damaged board. Localized board replacement is common and inexpensive.

**A rotted post with sound rails and boards:** a post can often be replaced or sistered without rebuilding the entire run — but only if the footing is redone so the new post doesn't inherit the old one's water problem. Replacing the post while leaving the wet footing intact is how a "fix" becomes a repeat call.

**Widespread rot, multiple soft posts, or visible insect infestation:** at this point you're choosing between a large repair and a replacement, and the math usually favors replacement. It's also the moment to call a licensed pest professional — termites and carpenter ants need treatment, not just new lumber, or they'll simply move into the new fence.

**A note on "just stain it":** staining and sealing are good preventive tools for sound wood, and they help the wood shed water and resist UV graying. But **sealer is not a repair.** Coating soft, wet, infected wood traps the moisture in and hides the problem while it gets worse. Fix the cause first, then protect what's sound.

## Your Rot-and-Insect Action Plan: Six Moves Before the Ground Freezes

Fall is the last comfortable window before Michigan's ground hardens, and it's the smartest time to get ahead of decay and insects. Work through these six in order, and you'll have done more for your fence's lifespan than any single product on a shelf can.

1. **Walk the line post by post.** Screwdriver in hand, check the bottom six inches of every post and every cut end. Note anything soft, spongy, dark, or giving.
2. **Break the wood-to-soil contact.** Pull soil, mulch, and vegetation back from the base of the fence. Wood that can dry out is wood that resists decay — this one habit prevents more rot than any coating.
3. **Fix the water.** Redirect downspouts, correct any grade sloping toward the fence, and re-aim sprinkler heads so boards aren't soaked every morning.
4. **Treat exposed and cut wood.** Brush copper naphthenate onto any raw cut end, notch, or drilled hole. Minutes of work for years of added service.
5. **Repair before winter.** Replace isolated rotted boards or a single failed post now, while the ground is still workable. A fix done in October is a fix you're not paying for in April — and frozen-thawed footings only get worse.
6. **Call a pro for anything that looks like insects.** Mud tubes, coarse sawdust, or fine powder mean the moisture problem has already attracted company, and that's a treatment issue, not just a carpentry one.

Do these six and you've turned a fence that was quietly feeding fungus and insects into a fence that dries out, holds its line, and has nothing left on the menu.

## Local Notes for Oakland County Homeowners

A few things make our corner of Michigan a little different from the generic advice you'll read online:

- **Clay soils hold water.** Much of Oakland County sits on heavy clay that drains poorly. That means post holes can hold standing water longer than you'd expect, and drainage details matter more here than in sandy regions.
- **Freeze-thaw pumps moisture.** Every spring thaw drives water into the ground line, then the next freeze cracks and lifts. It's a yearly stress test for every footing, and it's why the soil-line inspection is the one that counts.
- **Older housing stock.** Communities like Royal Oak, Ferndale, Berkley, Pontiac, and Waterford have homes — and fences — that predate 1980. If your fence has been there for a couple of decades and you've never looked at the posts, this fall is the time.
- **Termite pressure is regional and real.** Southern Michigan is documented termite country. If your neighbors have had termite treatment, pay attention to any wood touching soil on your own property.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do termites eat wood fences in Michigan?

Yes. Michigan's eastern subterranean termite is the state's most serious wood-destroying pest, and Oakland County falls within the southern-tier counties where it's established. Termites typically target a house before a fence, but a wet post in direct soil contact is a real attractant and should be inspected.

### Does cedar resist termites and rot?

Cedar **heartwood** resists both, thanks to natural compounds called thujaplicins. But cedar **sapwood** has almost no resistance, and cut ends and weathered surfaces lose their protection. Cedar is more durable than pine, but it is not termite-proof or rot-proof — and in ground contact, properly rated treated wood is generally the safer choice for posts.

### Why is my wood fence rotting at the bottom?

Because the ground line is where moisture, oxygen, warmth, and wood all meet and stay. Posts rot from the soil line up, and the fix is to keep that zone dry — ground-contact-rated posts, a footing that drains, no wood-to-soil contact, and treated cut ends.

### How do I stop my fence posts from rotting in the ground?

Use UC4A ground-contact-rated posts (or a naturally durable species), set them in a footing that drains, keep soil and mulch off the post, treat every cut end with copper naphthenate, and correct any grade, gutter, or sprinkler that keeps the base wet. Prevention beats repair every time.

### Are carpenter ants eating my fence?

No — carpenter ants don't eat wood. They tunnel through it to nest, and they're drawn to wood that's already wet or softening. If you find coarse sawdust and clean, hollow galleries in a fence post, that's a carpenter ant sign, and the moisture problem causing it needs fixing at the same time.

### What wood is best for fence posts in Michigan's climate?

For in-ground posts, UC4A ground-contact-rated treated lumber paired with cedar or treated boards above ground is the practical, cost-effective choice. Naturally durable species like cedar heartwood, redwood heartwood, cypress, black locust, and Osage orange can also work, but note that cedar sapwood posts can rot faster in ground contact than properly treated posts.

### Does staining or sealing prevent fence rot?

Staining and sealing help **sound** wood shed water and resist UV graying, and they're worth doing on a healthy fence. But they are not a repair and not a substitute for proper construction — sealer over untreated wood in ground contact, or over existing rot, hides the problem and can make it worse.

### How do I tell if a fence post is rotted below the ground?

Use the screwdriver test. Push a screwdriver into the bottom six inches of the post, and into any cut end or joint. If it sinks in easily, the wood is already failing, even if the surface looks fine. Also watch for a post that gives when you push it sideways — that usually means the below-grade wood or footing is gone.
