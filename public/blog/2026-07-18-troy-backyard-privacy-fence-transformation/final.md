## The Challenge: A Backyard Without Boundaries

The Johnson property sits on a standard Troy subdivision lot — roughly a quarter acre with side lot lines that feel closer than they look on paper. When we first walked the property, we saw exactly what they were dealing with:

**The privacy problem.** The lot backs up to a through street with a slight elevation change, meaning neighbors across the street and on both sides had direct sight lines into the yard. There wasn't a single spot where the family could sit outside without being visible from at least two directions.

**The containment issue.** With two young kids and a golden retriever named Copper, the Johnsons needed a fence that would keep everyone safe and contained. The existing property line was marked only by a few overgrown shrubs that had seen better days.

**The wind factor.** Troy's open suburban landscape means wind whips through unfenced backyards, especially in spring and fall. The Johnsons wanted to create a sheltered outdoor room — not just a boundary.

"We bought this house for the yard," Mrs. Johnson told us during the consultation. "But we barely use it because we feel like we're on display every time we step outside."

## Getting to Know the Property

Before we designed anything, we spent time understanding the Johnsons' specific property. Every Troy lot has its own personality, and the best fence installations start with a thorough site analysis.

**Lot dimensions and grade.** The lot measured approximately 80 feet wide by 135 feet deep, with a gentle slope from the house toward the back property line — typical for this part of Troy. That grade matters for fence installation because it affects post depth, drainage, and how the fence panels align visually.

**Soil conditions.** We dug test holes at three points along the proposed fence line. Troy sits on a mix of clay and sandy loam — decent drainage in the topsoil but heavy clay starting around 18 inches down. This is actually good news for fence posts (clay holds concrete well), but it means proper base preparation is critical to prevent frost heave.

**Sun and wind exposure.** The yard gets full western sun in the afternoon — a factor that influenced our material recommendations. Cedar handles UV exposure better than pressure-treated pine, which tends to check and crack more quickly in direct sun. The prevailing wind comes from the northwest, so we positioned the fence to create a windbreak across the patio area.

**Existing landscaping.** A mature maple tree near the southeast corner of the lot needed to be worked around — we didn't want to damage the root system, and we wanted to incorporate the tree into the design rather than fence it out. We also noted the location of underground sprinkler lines, which we'd need to flag during digging.

**Utility locates.** Before any digging, the Johnsons called Miss Dig (811) to have all underground utilities marked. This is mandatory in Troy and every municipality we work in — and we won't start a job until it's done. Gas lines, fiber optic cable, and sprinkler control wiring were all marked and avoided during post-hole digging.

## Designing the Solution — Board-on-Board Cedar Privacy Fence

After the site analysis, we sat down with the Johnsons to design the fence that would solve their privacy problems while complementing their Colonial-style home.

### Why Board-on-Board?

We recommended a **board-on-board design** — and here's why it was the right choice for this property:

Traditional shadowbox fences (where boards alternate on each side of the stringers) give you partial visibility through the fence from certain angles. For a family that wanted complete privacy, that wasn't enough. A standard dog-ear privacy fence (all boards on one side) provides privacy but looks the same from every angle — and the stringers are visible on one side.

Board-on-board is the sweet spot. Each picket overlaps the next by about an inch, creating a solid visual barrier with no gaps. From either side — the Johnsons' yard or their neighbors' — the fence looks finished and intentional. There's no "good side/bad side" compromise.

### Material Selection: Cedar, Straight-Grade

For this project, we specified **Western Red Cedar, straight-grade, #2 and better**. Here's why:

| Factor | Cedar | Pressure-Treated Pine | PVC/Vinyl |
|--------|-------|---------------------|-----------|
| Lifespan (Michigan) | 20-25 years with maintenance | 15-20 years | 20-30 years |
| Rot resistance | Naturally rot-resistant | Chemically treated | Full rot resistance |
| Aesthetic warmth | Rich, natural grain | Green tint fades to gray | Plastic look |
| Weight | Lightweight, easy to work | Heavy | Lightweight |
| Cost (per linear ft installed) | $25-35 | $18-25 | $30-45 |
| Repairability | Easy — replace individual boards | Easy | Difficult — color matching |
| Michigan winter performance | Excellent — resists splitting | Good — but checks over time | Can become brittle in extreme cold |

For the Johnsons, cedar was the clear winner. It had the natural warmth they wanted, it would hold up through Michigan's freeze-thaw cycles, and it was repairable if a board ever got damaged. The price premium over pressure-treated was worth it for the longevity and the look.

### Fence Specifications

- **Height**: 6 feet (maximum allowed for residential fences in Troy)
- **Design**: Board-on-board, with alternating 6-inch pickets overlapping by 1 inch
- **Posts**: 4x4 pressure-treated posts set 42 inches deep (below Michigan's frost line)
- **Post spacing**: 8 feet on center (standard, with gate posts at 4 feet)
- **Stringers**: Two horizontal 2x4 pressure-treated stringers per section
- **Top treatment**: Cedar 2x6 cap rail for a finished look and added weather protection
- **Pickets**: 6-inch straight-grade Western Red Cedar, dog-ear top
- **Gates**: Two — one 4-foot pedestrian gate (side yard access), one 10-foot double gate (backyard equipment access)
- **Finish**: Clear semi-transparent sealant, applied after installation

### Gate Design

The pedestrian gate was placed on the side of the house closest to the driveway — the natural entry point for the family. We installed it with heavy-duty self-closing hinges and a magnetic latch that the kids could operate but that would stay secure in the wind.

The double gate at the rear was designed for equipment access — lawn mower, snow blower, and the occasional lumber delivery for future projects. Each leaf was framed with extra bracing to prevent sagging over the 10-foot span.

## The Installation Process (Step by Step)

The installation took **six working days** — slightly longer than our average 4-5 day timeline for a lot this size, because of the difficult clay soil and the extra care needed around the mature maple tree.

### Day 1: Site Preparation and Layout

We started by marking the entire fence line with batter boards and string. This gave the Johnsons a chance to walk the perimeter and visualize the final layout before any digging began. We confirmed setbacks from the property line — Troy requires a minimum setback, which varies by zoning district, so we verified against the property survey.

The crew cleared vegetation along the fence line, removed the overgrown shrubs that were marking the old property boundary, and flagged the sprinkler heads that would need to be relocated. We also marked the root zone of the maple tree and adjusted the fence line by about 18 inches to stay outside the critical root zone.

### Day 2: Post-Hole Digging

This was the hardest day of the job. The clay soil at 18-24 inches was dense and wouldn't yield easily to the two-man auger. For the 18 post holes, we ended up using a combination of power auger for the first 18 inches and manual digging for the final 24 inches to the 42-inch target depth.

Why 42 inches? The frost line in Oakland County typically reaches 36-42 inches. Setting posts below the frost line prevents frost heave — the phenomenon where freezing ground pushes posts upward, causing gates to bind and fence sections to lean. We've seen too many fences in Troy and across Oakland County fail because posts were set only 24-30 inches deep. A fence is only as strong as its posts.

Each hole was filled with 6 inches of gravel at the bottom for drainage, then the post was positioned, leveled in both directions, and secured with a quick-set concrete mix. We waited 24 hours for the concrete to cure before hanging any weight on the posts.

### Day 3: Stringer Installation

With posts set and cured, we installed the two horizontal stringers — one at approximately 24 inches from the top, one at 24 inches from the bottom. Pressure-treated 2x4s were cut to length and notched into the posts using a router jig for clean, consistent joinery. Each stringer was secured with two hot-dipped galvanized deck screws per post-end.

**Important note on fastener choice:** We use only hot-dipped galvanized or stainless steel fasteners on cedar installations. Standard electro-galvanized fasteners react with the tannins in cedar and cause black staining over time — an issue we've seen on countless DIY fences in Oakland County. It's a small detail that makes a huge difference in the long-term appearance.

### Day 4: Picket Installation

The pickets were the most time-consuming part of the installation — and the most rewarding. Each 6-inch cedar picket was cut to length, gapped at exactly 1 inch overlap with the adjacent picket, and secured with two screws per stringer contact point.

We worked in sections, using a custom spacing jig to maintain consistent overlap across the entire fence. The board-on-board pattern means you have to work from one end to the other — you can't skip around — so we started at the house corner and worked around the perimeter.

The crew also cut and installed the cap rail — 2x6 cedar strips running along the top of the fence, mitered at the corners. The cap rail does three things: it protects the end grain of the pickets from moisture, it gives the fence a finished, furniture-grade look, and it eliminates the sharp picket tops that can be a safety concern for kids.

### Day 5: Gate Construction and Installation

Gates are the most technically demanding part of any fence installation, and the Johnsons' double gate was no exception.

The 10-foot double gate required a reinforced frame — 2x6 pressure-treated lumber sandwiched with 1/2-inch plywood gussets at the corners — to prevent sagging. We used heavy-duty strap hinges rated for 200 pounds per leaf, and installed a cane bolt at the bottom of the inactive leaf to lock it in place when not in use.

The 4-foot pedestrian gate was simpler but required careful alignment to ensure the self-closing mechanism worked properly. We set the gate with a slight uphill bias so it would close positively without slamming.

### Day 6: Finishing and Cleanup

The final day was all about the details. We:
- Installed post caps on all 18 posts (cedar pyramid caps, glued and pin-nailed)
- Applied a clear semi-transparent cedar-toned sealant to all fence surfaces
- Adjusted the sprinkler system heads that had been relocated
- Backfilled and graded around the post bases
- Hauled away all construction debris
- Walked the entire fence line with the Johnsons, showing them how the self-closing gates worked, explaining the maintenance schedule, and answering every question

## Navigating Troy's Permit Requirements

One of the most common questions we get from Troy homeowners is: **"Do I need a permit for a fence?"**

The answer depends on the fence height. Here's the specific breakdown based on Troy's ordinance:

| Fence Height | Permit Required? | Notes |
|-------------|-----------------|-------|
| Less than 4 feet | No building permit | Planning Department review still recommended |
| 4 to 6 feet | Planning review required | Submit site plan showing property lines and fence location |
| Over 6 feet | Prohibited in residential zones | Maximum height is 6 feet for residential |
| 7 feet or more (commercial) | Full building permit required | Falls under Michigan Building Code |

For the Johnsons' 6-foot fence, the permit process involved:
1. **Submitting a site plan** showing the fence location relative to property lines, the house, and any easements
2. **Planning Department review** to ensure compliance with Troy's zoning ordinance (Chapter 39)
3. **Obtaining the permit** from the Troy Building Department at 500 W. Big Beaver Road

The permit cost for a fence of this scope was approximately $75-125, depending on the project cost estimate. We handle the permit application as part of our service, so the Johnsons didn't have to navigate the city bureaucracy themselves.

**Important note for Troy homeowners**: Before you dig, you must call MISS DIG at 811 or visit missdig.org at least three business days before your project start date. This is a free service that marks all underground utilities so you don't hit gas lines, fiber optic cables, or electric service lines during digging.

## Why Cedar Was the Right Choice for This Project

We already walked through the material comparison table, but let's talk specifically about why cedar was the right call for this Troy property.

**Michigan's climate is hard on fences.** We get freeze-thaw cycles that can destroy a poorly built fence in three years. We get humidity that rots untreated wood. We get summer sun that fades and checks cheap lumber. And we get winter winds that test every joint and fastener.

Cedar handles all of this naturally. The tannins in Western Red Cedar act as a natural preservative — no chemical treatment needed. It's dimensionally stable, meaning it doesn't expand and contract as much as pine with humidity changes. And it's lighter than pressure-treated pine, which means less stress on the posts and stringers over time.

For the Johnsons — who plan to be in their Troy home for another 15-20 years — cedar was the investment that would pay off every single day in the form of a beautiful, low-maintenance fence that would last as long as they owned the house.

**But cedar does need maintenance.** We recommend:
- **Annual cleaning**: A gentle pressure wash (under 1,500 PSI) to remove dirt and mildew
- **Resealing every 3-4 years**: A clear or semi-transparent sealant to protect against UV damage
- **Immediate repair**: Replace any damaged boards promptly to prevent moisture from wicking into adjacent pickets
- **Winter prep**: Clear snow away from the base of the fence to prevent ground-level moisture damage

Some homeowners prefer the gray, weathered look that cedar develops over time. If that's you, you can skip the resealing — just know that the fence will weather faster and may have a shorter overall lifespan.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the maximum fence height allowed in Troy, Michigan?

Residential fences in Troy cannot exceed **6 feet in height**. This includes the posts and any decorative elements. Fences shorter than 4 feet typically don't require a building permit, but any fence 4 feet or taller requires review by the Planning Department. Commercial properties may have different height allowances.

### Do I need a permit to build a fence in Troy?

If your fence is under 4 feet tall, a building permit is generally not required. However, if your fence is between 4 and 6 feet tall — which most privacy fences are — you need a Planning Department review. Submit a site plan showing the fence location relative to your property lines. We handle all permit applications for our customers.

### What's the best wood for a privacy fence in Michigan?

Western Red Cedar is the best choice for most Michigan homeowners. It's naturally rot-resistant, stable in Michigan's humidity, and develops a beautiful patina over time. Pressure-treated pine is a good budget alternative, but it's heavier, more prone to checking and twisting, and requires more maintenance. Cedar will typically outlast pressure-treated pine by 5-10 years in Michigan conditions.

### How much does a cedar privacy fence cost in Troy, Michigan?

A custom cedar privacy fence installed by a professional contractor typically runs **$25-35 per linear foot** in the Troy area. For an average Troy lot (around 200 linear feet of fencing), expect a total installed cost of **$5,000 to $10,000** depending on design complexity, number of gates, and site conditions. The Johnsons' board-on-board project with two gates came in at approximately $7,200.

### How long does it take to install a custom wood fence?

Most standard residential installations take **4 to 6 working days**. The timeline depends on lot size, soil conditions, weather, and design complexity. Board-on-board designs take slightly longer than standard shadowbox or dog-ear fences because of the overlapping picket pattern. Complex gates (double gates, oversized gates) can add 1-2 days.

### Does a wood privacy fence increase home value?

Yes — multiple studies show that a quality wood privacy fence adds value to a home, with an estimated **50-70% return on investment** at resale. In Troy's competitive real estate market, a well-maintained privacy fence is a significant selling point. It signals to buyers that the property is move-in ready and that the outdoor space is usable from day one. More importantly, it adds **usable square footage** to your home — your backyard becomes an outdoor room rather than an unused space.

### What is board-on-board fence construction?

Board-on-board is a fence design where pickets are installed on both sides of the stringers, with each picket overlapping the next by about an inch. This creates a solid visual barrier with no gaps — unlike shadowbox fencing, which has alternating gaps. Board-on-board looks the same from both sides (no "good side"), provides better privacy, and offers a more finished appearance. It's our most recommended design for privacy-focused projects.

### How deep do fence posts need to be in Michigan?

Fence posts in Michigan need to be set **at least 36 inches deep**, and we recommend **42 inches** for gate posts and corner posts. Michigan's frost line reaches 36-42 inches in most of Oakland County. Setting posts shallower than the frost line means they'll heave upward during spring freeze-thaw cycles, causing gates to bind, fence sections to lean, and the entire structure to fail prematurely. Burying to 42 inches with a gravel base and concrete collar is the standard for a fence that lasts.
