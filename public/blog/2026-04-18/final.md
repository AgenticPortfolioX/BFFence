## Why Wood Is Still the Best Choice for Michigan Fences

Let's get this out of the way first. Yes, vinyl is low-maintenance. Yes, aluminum looks sleek. And yes, chain link is cheap. But here's why wood continues to be the top choice for residential fencing in Michigan:

**It actually looks good in your yard.** A well-built wood fence blends into a Michigan landscape. It doesn't look like a commercial installation. It looks like it belongs there. That matters when you're in a neighborhood where homes have character — which describes most of Oakland County.

**It handles Michigan weather when done right.** Wood has been used in Michigan construction for over a century. The material is proven. The key is choosing the right species and building it correctly. More on that below.

**It's repairable.** If a vinyl panel cracks, you're replacing the whole panel. If an aluminum post bends, you're replacing the post. With wood, you can replace individual boards, re-set posts, and make targeted repairs. That's a big deal when you're talking about a fence that needs to last 20-25 years.

**It's customizable.** You want a 6-foot privacy fence? Done. A 4-foot picket fence? Done. A horizontal modern look? Done. A classic shadow box? Done. Wood gives you options that other materials simply don't offer at the same price point.

**It adds real property value.** In Oakland County, a well-maintained wood privacy fence can add 5-10% to your home's perceived value. That's not just a number we're throwing around — that's based on what buyers actually look for when they're shopping in this market.

## The Three Main Wood Species for Michigan Fences

When it comes to wood fencing in Michigan, you're really choosing between three options. Each has its strengths, and the right choice depends on your budget, your priorities, and how long you plan to stay in your home.

### 1. Pressure-Treated Pine (Southern Yellow Pine)

**This is the most common fence material in Michigan, and for good reason.** Pressure-treated pine is affordable, widely available, and — when properly treated — holds up well in our climate.

**How it works:** Southern yellow pine boards and posts are treated with chemical preservatives (typically ACQ — alkaline copper quaternary) under pressure. The treatment penetrates deep into the wood fibers, making the wood resistant to rot, fungal decay, and insect damage. For fence posts, you want lumber rated UC4A or higher, which means it's rated for ground contact.

**Pros:**
- Most affordable option — typically 30-50% less expensive than cedar
- Readily available at any lumber yard in Michigan
- Strong and structurally sound (pine is a dense softwood)
- Chemical treatment provides excellent rot and insect resistance
- Accepts stain well once fully dried (wait 3-6 months after installation before staining)

**Cons:**
- Requires staining or sealing to look its best — greenish tint fades to gray if untreated
- Can warp, crack, and shrink as it dries (kiln-dried after treatment, or KDAT, is better but costs more)
- Not as naturally beautiful as cedar
- Heavier than cedar, which means harder to work with

**Best for:** Budget-conscious homeowners who want a solid, long-lasting fence and don't mind doing the maintenance work (staining every 2-3 years). This is the workhorse of the Michigan fence market.

**Expected lifespan in Michigan:** 15-20 years with proper maintenance. Posts can last 20-25 years if set below the frost line.

**Cost range in Michigan (2026):** $18-$30 per linear foot installed for a 6-foot privacy fence.

### 2. Western Red Cedar

**If you want the best wood fence money can buy, cedar is the answer.** Western red cedar is the gold standard for residential fencing, and there's a reason it commands a premium price.

**How it works:** Cedar contains natural oils and tannins called thujaplicins that act as preservatives. These chemicals make the wood naturally resistant to rot, decay, and insects — without any chemical treatment. The wood is also dimensionally stable, meaning it doesn't warp, shrink, or twist nearly as much as pine.

**Pros:**
- Naturally rot and insect resistant — no chemical treatment needed
- Extremely stable — minimal warping, twisting, or shrinking
- Beautiful natural color (warm reddish-brown) that ages to an attractive silver-gray if left untreated
- Lightweight and easy to work with
- Pleasant smell (seriously, it smells great)
- Lasts longer than pine with less maintenance

**Cons:**
- Significantly more expensive — typically 50-100% more than pressure-treated pine
- Availability can be inconsistent (supplies from the Pacific Northwest fluctuate)
- Softer than pine — more susceptible to physical damage (dings, dents)
- Not as strong structurally — may not be the best choice for very tall fences or heavy gates

**Best for:** Homeowners who want a premium look, plan to stay in their home long-term, and are willing to invest more upfront for lower lifetime maintenance costs. Also great if you want a fence that looks beautiful from day one without waiting for stain to cure.

**Expected lifespan in Michigan:** 20-25 years with minimal maintenance (occasional cleaning). With staining every 3-4 years, can last 25-30 years.

**Cost range in Michigan (2026):** $28-$45 per linear foot installed for a 6-foot privacy fence.

### 3. Redwood

**Redwood is the luxury option — beautiful, durable, and expensive.** It's less common in Michigan than cedar or pine, but it's worth knowing about if you're looking for something special.

**How it works:** Like cedar, redwood contains natural preservatives (tannins) that resist rot and insects. The heartwood (the inner, darker portion of the tree) is particularly durable. Sapwood (the outer, lighter portion) is less durable and shouldn't be used for fence posts.

**Pros:**
- Stunning natural appearance — rich red tones with tight grain
- Naturally resistant to rot and insects
- Very stable — minimal warping
- Lightweight yet strong

**Cons:**
- Most expensive option — 75-150% more than pressure-treated pine
- Limited availability in Michigan — you'll need to special order from most suppliers
- Sourced primarily from California, so shipping costs add up
- Sustainability concerns — old-growth redwood is protected, and second-growth wood is less durable

**Best for:** High-end projects where aesthetics are the top priority and budget isn't a major constraint. Also good for fence accents (post caps, top rails) combined with cedar or pine panels.

**Expected lifespan in Michigan:** 20-30 years for heartwood with minimal maintenance.

**Cost range in Michigan (2026):** $35-$55+ per linear foot installed for a 6-foot privacy fence.

## Fence Styles: Which One Fits Your Property?

The species of wood is only half the equation. The style of fence determines how it looks, how much privacy it provides, and how it performs in wind and snow. Here are the most common styles we install in Oakland County:

### Full Privacy (Stockade or Dog-Ear)

**What it is:** Solid fence with boards butted tightly together on one side. No gaps. Complete visual barrier.

**Privacy level:** 100% — you can't see through it at all.

**Best for:** Backyard privacy, pool enclosures, blocking street views, pet containment.

**Michigan considerations:** The solid surface acts as a wind barrier, which is good for blocking winter wind but means the fence takes more wind load. Posts need to be set deeper (42 inches recommended in Oakland County) and rails need to be beefier. In areas with heavy snow drift, make sure the fence line accounts for where snow piles up.

**Typical height:** 6 feet (most common), 4 feet (front yards where zoning allows).

### Shadow Box (Board-on-Board)

**What it is:** Boards are attached on alternating sides of the rails, creating a fence that looks solid from most angles but has small gaps that allow air and light through.

**Privacy level:** 85-90% — very good privacy with a slightly more open feel.

**Best for:** Homeowners who want privacy without a fortress look. Great for property lines where you want to be a good neighbor (both sides look finished).

**Michigan considerations:** The gaps allow wind to pass through, which significantly reduces wind load on the fence. This is a real advantage in Michigan — you'll see fewer shadow box fences leaning after winter storms compared to full privacy fences. The trade-off is slightly less privacy.

**Typical height:** 6 feet.

### Horizontal Slat

**What it is:** Boards run horizontally instead of vertically. Modern, clean-lined look that's become very popular in the last five years.

**Privacy level:** 85-95% depending on gap size (tighter gaps = more privacy).

**Best for:** Modern homes, contemporary landscapes, homeowners who want a distinctive look that stands out from the standard vertical fence.

**Michigan considerations:** Horizontal boards can be more prone to cupping (bowing inward or outward) because they trap water on the upper surface. This is especially true in Michigan where snow sits on the boards for months. Use kiln-dried wood, make sure there's adequate spacing for drainage, and consider a slight tilt on the top rail to shed water. Cedar is the best choice for horizontal fences because of its dimensional stability.

**Typical height:** 6 feet.

### Split Rail

**What it is:** Rustic fence with posts and horizontal rails, no solid panels. Open, pastoral look.

**Privacy level:** 0-20% — this is a boundary fence, not a privacy fence.

**Best for:** Large properties, front yards, decorative boundaries, rural or suburban-rural settings. Often used with wire mesh attached to keep pets in.

**Michigan considerations:** Split rail is the most wind-resistant style because it's almost entirely open. Posts are still set deep, but the fence itself takes minimal wind load. The main concern is that the open design doesn't block snow drifts at all — if you're using split rail along a road, snow will blow right through.

**Typical height:** 4 feet (2-rail) or 4.5-5 feet (3-rail).

### Lattice Top

**What it is:** Solid privacy fence on the bottom half or two-thirds, with a lattice panel on top for a decorative, semi-open look.

**Privacy level:** 70-80% depending on lattice density.

**Best for:** Homeowners who want privacy at eye level but don't want the fence to feel like a wall. Great for gardens where you want climbing plants.

**Michigan considerations:** The lattice section is more vulnerable to wind and snow damage than solid panels. Use thicker lattice strips (1x2 minimum, not the flimsy 1/4-inch stuff). Snow can accumulate in the lattice openings and add weight, so make sure the connection between the solid section and lattice is robust.

**Typical height:** 6 feet total (4 feet solid + 2 feet lattice).

## The Fence Post Question: Why It Matters More Than Anything Else

Here's the part most homeowners don't think about until it's too late: the posts are the most important part of your fence. Not the boards. Not the style. The posts.

If the posts fail, the fence fails. Period. And in Michigan, posts fail more often than any other component — because of frost heave.

### What Is Frost Heave?

When the ground freezes in winter, it expands. In Michigan, the frost line — the depth at which the ground freezes — is typically 36 to 42 inches, depending on your location. Oakland County is generally in the 36-42 inch range.

If your fence posts aren't set below the frost line, the expanding frozen ground pushes them upward. A little bit each winter. After a few years, your posts are sticking up above the fence line, the concrete footings are cracked, and the whole fence is wobbling.

**The fix is simple: set your posts deep enough in the first place.** In Michigan, that means:

- **Minimum 36 inches deep** for standard residential fences
- **42 inches deep** for areas with heavy clay soil (common in parts of Auburn Hills, Rochester, and Troy)
- **42 inches deep** for 6-foot privacy fences that take significant wind load

### Concrete Footings

Posts should be set in concrete. Not just tamped gravel, not just dirt — concrete. Here's what we do at BF Fence:

- Hole diameter: 10-12 inches for a 4x4 post, 12-16 inches for a 6x6 post
- Concrete: Premixed, high-strength (4000 PSI minimum)
- Post position: Centered in the hole with concrete all around
- Drainage: We add a few inches of gravel at the bottom of the hole before pouring concrete, which allows water to drain away from the post base

This matters because the number one killer of fence posts in Michigan isn't frost heave — it's rot at the base where the post meets the ground. Water sits in the concrete footing, the post stays wet, and the wood rots from the inside out. Proper drainage and the right post material (UC4B pressure-treated or cedar) prevent this.

Let's bring it all together. Here's a side-by-side comparison to help you choose:

### Budget-Friendly Option
- **Material:** Pressure-treated pine

- **Height:** 6 feet
- **Cost:** $18-$30/linear foot

- **Lifespan:** 15-20 years

- **Material:** Western red cedar

- **Best for:** Homeowners who want cedar's good looks without the full cedar price tag. The structural components (posts, rails) are the affordable pine, and the visible boards are cedar.

## Michigan-Specific Tips for Choosing Your Fence

Before you make your final decision, keep these Michigan-specific factors in mind:

**Check your local zoning.** Most Oakland County cities have setback requirements and height limits for fences. Auburn Hills, for example, typically allows 6-foot fences in backyards and 4-foot fences in front yards, with specific setback rules from property lines and rights-of-way. Check with your city's building department before you start.

**Call 811 before you dig.** This is non-negotiable. Michigan's MISS DIG system will mark underground utilities for free. It takes a few days, and it could save your life (or at least save you from hitting a gas line with a post hole digger).

**Consider your neighbors.** In Michigan, most cities have rules about which side of the fence faces out. A good fence contractor will make sure both sides look finished (shadow box is great for this). It's also common courtesy to let your neighbors know you're installing a fence, especially if it's on or near the property line.

**Think about snow.** Where does snow pile up in your yard after a storm? If it piles up against your fence, make sure the fence is built to handle the extra weight and moisture. Pressure-treated posts and rails, set deep, are your best defense.

**Plan for gates.** Gates are the most complex part of any fence. They need to be sturdy enough to not sag, and the posts they hang from need to be extra strong (we use 6x6 posts for gate locations). Think about where you need access — for mowing, for bringing in equipment, for everyday use — and plan your gate locations before installation starts.

## Hiring the Right Contractor in Oakland County

If you've decided on your material and style, the next step is finding a contractor you trust. Here's what we'd tell you if you were asking us at a backyard barbecue:

**Get at least three quotes.** But don't just compare the bottom line. Compare what's included: post depth, post material, rail material, board quality, concrete footings, and warranty. The cheapest quote often cuts corners on the things you can't see — like post depth.

**Ask to see their work.** Any good contractor will have photos or addresses of completed projects in your area. Drive by a few. Look at the fence lines. Are the posts straight? Are the boards evenly spaced? Is the fence line level, or does it follow the terrain gracefully?

**Verify they're local.** A contractor who knows Oakland County knows the soil, the zoning, the frost depth, and the neighborhoods. We've seen out-of-area contractors set posts at 24 inches in areas that freeze to 40 inches. That fence won't last five years.

**Ask about the warranty.** A reputable contractor offers a warranty on both materials and labor. At BF Fence, we stand behind every project because Justin and I are on every job site. We're not sending a crew and hoping for the best — we're doing the work ourselves.

**Don't pay everything upfront.** A deposit is normal. Paying in full before work starts is not. A good contractor will have terms that protect both of you.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is the best type of wood for a fence in Michigan?**
A: Western red cedar is the premium choice — it's naturally rot-resistant, stable, and beautiful. Pressure-treated pine is the best value — affordable, durable, and widely available. For most Michigan homeowners, pressure-treated pine with proper staining offers the best balance of cost and longevity.

**Q: How long does a wood fence last in Michigan?**
A: A well-built and maintained wood fence lasts 15-25 years in Michigan. Pressure-treated pine typically lasts 15-20 years. Cedar can last 20-25 years or more. The biggest factors are post depth (must be below the 36-42 inch frost line), drainage, and regular staining or sealing.

**Q: How deep should fence posts be in Michigan?**
A: In Michigan, fence posts should be set at least 36 inches deep — ideally 42 inches in areas with heavy clay soil, which is common across Oakland County. This places the bottom of the post below the frost line and prevents frost heave, which is the number one cause of fence failure in Michigan.

**Q: Should I stain my fence or let it weather naturally?**
A: Staining every 2-3 years significantly extends the life of your fence. Pressure-treated pine should always be stained — it looks better and the stain adds a moisture barrier. Cedar can be left to weather naturally to a silver-gray, but staining every 3-4 years will extend its life and preserve the warm color. In Michigan's climate, staining is worth the effort.

**Q: How much does a wood fence cost in Michigan?**
A: In 2026, a 6-foot wood privacy fence in Michigan typically costs $18-$30 per linear foot for pressure-treated pine and $28-$45 per linear foot for cedar, installed. A typical residential lot (150-200 linear feet) runs $3,000-$9,000 depending on material, style, and terrain.

**Q: Is cedar worth the extra cost over pressure-treated pine?**
A: Cedar is worth it if you prioritize appearance, plan to stay in your home long-term, and want lower lifetime maintenance costs. The upfront cost is 50-100% more, but cedar lasts longer, requires less frequent staining, and looks better from day one. For budget-conscious homeowners, pressure-treated pine with regular maintenance is a perfectly solid choice.

**Q: Can I install a fence in winter in Michigan?**
A: Technically yes, but it's not ideal. Frozen ground makes digging post holes extremely difficult. Concrete doesn't cure well below 40°F. And working in Michigan winter conditions is miserable. Most fence contractors in Oakland County book installations from May through October. If you want a spring fence, get your quote in March or April.
