# Backyard Security Fencing in Oakland County: What Actually Deters a Break-In — and What's Just Marketing
*Published: October 21, 2026 | By BFFence*

---

On November 1, the sun sets at 5:27 PM.

Not 6:29 PM, which is what the clock says on October 31. Five twenty-seven. Sixty-two minutes of evening gone in a single weekend, because daylight saving time ends on Sunday and the dark arrives all at once.

That is the week most Oakland County homeowners start thinking about who can see into their backyard. It is also the week our phone starts ringing with some version of the same question: *will a fence actually keep someone out?*

The honest answer is more interesting than yes.

We're BF Fence — Justin and Chris, two best friends building wooden fences in Auburn Hills, Rochester Hills, Troy, Clarkston, Lake Orion, Novi, and everywhere between. **Protecting what matters** is easy to say on a website. It gets harder when you sit down and look at what a fence genuinely does and does not do about a person who intends to enter your home.

So this is the honest version of backyard security fencing — the version we'd tell a neighbour over the fence line. Where a wood fence changes the math, where it does nothing at all, where a solid privacy fence can quietly work against you, and what to do about the one part of the system that fails nine times out of ten.

## Before You Buy Anything: What a Fence Can and Cannot Do

A fence does exactly two things in a security plan. It denies access, and it denies reconnaissance. That's it. Every real benefit traces back to one of those, and every marketing claim that doesn't is decoration.

Security professionals describe those two jobs as part of **CPTED** — Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design — a framework built on four principles published by the US CPTED Association: natural surveillance, natural access control, territorial reinforcement, and maintenance. A fence is the textbook tool for the middle two. A boundary that must be climbed is natural access control. A boundary that reads as owned and cared for is territorial reinforcement, and it does real work, because a yard that looks watched is a yard that gets skipped.

The problem is the first principle. **Natural surveillance means you can see your own space, and your neighbours can see it too.** A six-foot solid privacy fence is the best product on the market for blocking that. Which is the entire point of a privacy fence — and the entire reason it is a mixed security product.

Hold that tension. It runs through everything below.

## How Homes Actually Get Broken Into

Before deciding what a fence should do, look at where entry actually happens. The Bureau of Justice Statistics entry-point data, updated through the 2018–2022 victimization surveys, is remarkably consistent:

| Entry point | Share of residential burglaries |
|---|---|
| Front door | 34% |
| First-floor windows | 23% |
| Back door | 22% |
| Garage door | 9% |
| Basement windows | 6% |
| Second floor / other | 6% |

Add the doors together — front, back, garage — and you get **65%**. Add the windows and you get **29%**.

Now notice what is not in that table: "climbed the back fence." It isn't a category because it isn't how the overwhelming majority of residential burglaries resolve. The fence is not the crime scene. The fence is what stands between a stranger and the *22% back door*, and between a stranger and the yard they'd need to cross to get there.

That reframing matters, because it changes what you should buy. A homeowner who believes the fence is the security system spends money on height and ornament. A homeowner who understands the fence is the access-control layer spends money on the gate, the sight lines, and the lighting — and gets a materially safer yard for less.

## The Part Nobody Says Out Loud: Daylight Burglaries Outnumber Night Ones

Every security pitch you've ever seen assumes the intruder comes at 2 AM. The data says otherwise.

In 2024 the FBI recorded 216,601 daytime residential burglaries against 174,053 at night. Daylight wins. Average loss ran **$4,986 during the day** and $4,368 at night. Nationally, burglary fell to 779,542 incidents that year, an 8.6% drop from 2023 and roughly 64% below 2005 — and Michigan's rate of 183.9 per 100,000 sits below the national 229.2.

What that means for a fence is subtle, and it's the most useful thing in this article. Burglars overwhelmingly work when a house looks empty, not when it looks dark. Darkness matters for a different reason: it hides the *approach*. A person standing at your back gate at 5:45 PM in November is invisible from the street, invisible from your kitchen window if the fence is solid, and completely unremarkable to a neighbour driving past.

So the fence's real job after November 1 is not to stop a night raid. It is to make the approach visible to somebody other than the person approaching. Which raises the question of what your fence is doing to your sight lines.

## Where a Fence Genuinely Changes the Math

Four situations where a wood fence earns its cost as a security layer, not just a privacy product.

**It denies casing.** This is the big one, and it's why offender interviews consistently rank physical barriers above electronics. In the most-cited study of burglar decision-making — University of North Carolina Charlotte, interviews with 422 convicted burglars — 83% said they tried to determine whether a home had a security system before entering, and **60% said they would choose another target if security measures were present.** More than half said they abandoned an attempt when they discovered security mid-way. Physical hardening was rated the strongest deterrent of everything asked about — stronger than cameras, stronger than alarms. A fence removes the ability to walk the perimeter, count windows, check for dogs, and see what's sitting in the backyard. You cannot case a yard you can't see into.

**It closes the side and rear approach.** The front door is 34% of entries and a fence won't help there. But the back door at 22% is reachable through a side gate, a shared driveway, an alley, or an unfenced corner. Closing that route doesn't make the back door invulnerable; it makes the route longer and more exposed, which is the whole game.

**It buys time.** Time-to-entry is the metric that actually predicts whether an opportunistic burglary proceeds. Every minute spent climbing, forcing a gate, or working a latch is a minute in view, a minute closer to a neighbour noticing, a minute closer to a dog barking. A fence converts a fifteen-second approach into a ninety-second one.

**It marks territory.** CPTED's territorial reinforcement principle is unglamorous and effective. A maintained fence, a closed gate, a mowed strip, a lit porch — these signal occupancy and care. The opposite signals are a standing invitation: an open gate, a sagging panel, mail piling up, a dark house with a broken latch.

## Where a Fence Does Nothing — and Where It Backfires

Here is the section every other page about security fencing skips.

**A fence does not protect your front door, and your front door is the single most common entry point.** It does not protect first-floor windows. It does not protect the garage door. Together those are 66% of entries and none of them are on the fence line. If you install a beautiful cedar privacy fence and leave a hollow-core front door with a 30-year-old deadbolt, you have spent money on the wrong end of the problem.

**A solid fence can be cover.** A six-foot solid panel hides your yard from the street and from your neighbours' second-floor windows. That's the product working as designed. It is also a screen behind which someone can work a gate latch for two minutes without being seen by anyone but you. If your back line borders a greenbelt, a drainage easement, a commercial lot, or a rental with high turnover, a fully solid run on that side is privacy you paid for and cover you didn't think about.

**A fence does not stop a determined or professional burglar.** Everything in this article is about opportunity crime — the large majority of residential burglaries, where target selection is a quick judgement about risk and reward. A fence changes that judgement. It does not defeat someone who has decided specifically on your house.

**And some "security fencing" products make you less safe.** Fence spikes, broken glass set into a top rail, barbed wire, and anti-climb paint all appear on page one for this topic. In a residential subdivision they are a liability problem first and a security measure a distant second: injury exposure if a child climbs your fence, code and HOA conflicts, and — practically — a jacket laid over the top defeats all of them. We don't install them and we don't recommend them.

## The Sight-Line Problem: Solid vs. Open, and How to Get Both

This is the design decision that separates a fence that helps from a fence that mostly hides things.

| Fence style | Privacy | Visibility into your own yard | Security read |
|---|---|---|---|
| Board-on-board solid privacy | Highest — no straight-through sightline | None from outside; you also lose outside-in awareness | Best privacy, worst surveillance. Needs lighting and camera coverage to compensate |
| Standard dog-ear privacy (small gaps) | High | Very limited | Similar tradeoff, slightly better airflow and light |
| Shadowbox | Moderate — offset boards, open gaps both sides | Partial | Reasonable compromise; blocks casual view, keeps some visibility |
| Picket or spaced panel | Low | High | Strong natural surveillance, weak access control at ground level |
| Ornamental aluminum / open rail | None | Highest | Excellent visibility, easy to climb; best on the front and side-street lines |

The resolution is not to pick a winner. It is to build different fence lines for different jobs. Most of the yards we build in Oakland County want three different answers on three different sides.

**Solid where you need privacy** — the back line facing neighbours, a pool, a hot tub, a deck. **Open or picket where you need visibility** — the side that faces the street, a shared driveway, a side yard where you want to see a gate. And where a solid run has to face a vulnerable approach, add what the solid panel took away: a light aimed *into* the yard rather than at the fence, and a camera covering the gate and the back corner.

One detail worth knowing: a solid fence does not stop sound and does not stop a person who has already decided. What it reliably does is remove a stranger's ability to look before they commit. That is worth paying for. Just don't also remove your own ability to look.

## Height, Michigan's Six-Foot Ceiling, and Why It Changes the Plan

Every homeowner's first instinct is a taller fence. In Oakland County that instinct usually runs into a zoning code.

Most residential districts here cap side and rear fencing at **six feet**. Rochester Hills is a useful example: six feet along side and rear lot lines, with front and side-street setbacks held to three feet, and some districts reading closer to four feet in front. Variances exist, but they are a process, not a purchase.

That constraint has a real consequence: **you cannot fix a weak gate with more height.** If six feet is the ceiling, then the six feet you build has to be correct — posts set below Michigan's 42-inch frost line under the Residential Code, a panel with no toe holds, a gate with hardware that resists lifting, and a latch nobody can reach over.

There's a second Michigan-specific point. A fence that measures six feet in July does not measure six feet in February. Posts set 42 inches down keep the footing from heaving, but the bottom board lives in the top six inches of soil, and that layer moves every freeze-thaw cycle. A bottom board that sits flush in October can hang two inches off grade by March — which is a dog problem, a drainage problem, and a gap under a barrier you believed was continuous.

## The Gate Is the Weak Point

If we could only fix one thing on an existing fence, it would be the gate. Not the fence line. The gate.

A gate is the only part of a fence designed to move. It carries more load per post than any other section, it puts a hinge, a latch, and a swing arc into the barrier, and it's the part humans touch every day. It is also where a determined person starts, because a standard residential gate can be defeated in three ways that have nothing to do with climbing six feet.

| Failure | How it happens | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| **The lift** | Standard hinges let the leaf be lifted straight up and off, or the hinge pins tapped out | Anti-lift or security hinges with a fixed or non-removable pin, or a hinge-side security plate |
| **The reach-over** | Latch mounted at 48–60 inches, operable by hand from the outside | Latch or cane bolt mounted high on the inside, out of reach over the top |
| **The sag** | Gate drops a half inch, latch stops engaging cleanly, corner drags, gap opens on the latch side | Braced frame, three hinges on anything over 36 inches tall, and a gate stop so it can't swing past square |
| **The second leaf** | On a double drive gate, one leaf is locked and the other is not restrained | Padlockable drop rod or cane bolt into a ground receiver — required with most latching systems |

Those are cheap corrections, and on an existing gate most of them are a two-hour retrofit. We specify anti-lift hinges on residential gates as a matter of course, because a hinge that costs a few dollars more is the difference between a barrier and a suggestion.

A few specifics worth knowing. Cane bolts and drop rods are the right hardware for double gates and wide openings — a rod that drops into a receiver holds a leaf against forced opening in a way a latch alone cannot. Hinge count matters: two hinges up to 36 inches of gate height, three up to 60 inches, and gates heavier than that need heavier hardware than a residential kit. Latch height matters most: anything a person can reach over the top of the gate from the outside is a latch that isn't doing its job. And keyed-alike locks are worth the small extra cost, because a gate you have to hunt for a key to open is a gate that gets left open — which is the failure mode that costs you everything the fence was for.

## What to Add Around a Fence to Turn It Into a Layer

A fence is one layer. The published estimates on relative deterrence are worth seeing together, with the caveat that these are estimates compiled from offender research and field studies, not controlled trials:

| Measure | Published estimated risk reduction |
|---|---|
| Deadbolt plus reinforced door frame | 40–60% |
| Dog (audible alert) | 25–35% |
| Visible security cameras | 15–25% |
| Security lighting | 15–20% |
| Window alarms | 10–20% |

Read that table honestly and it tells you something uncomfortable: the two highest-impact items have nothing to do with your fence line. A reinforced front door is the single best dollar you can spend on home security, and a dog is second. The fence's contribution is structural rather than statistical — it's what makes the other layers work in the yard.

What genuinely pairs with a wood fence:

**Lighting aimed into the yard, not at the fence.** A floodlight pointed at the fence panel lights the fence. A light pointed across the yard lights the *person*. Motion-activated, mounted high, covering the gate and the back corner. This is the 15–20% line item and it's the cheapest thing on the list.

**Camera coverage on the gate and the rear approach.** Visible cameras carry 15–25%. Placement matters more than specification — one camera on the gate beats four on the front of the house. If your fence is solid, the camera is doing the surveillance job the panel gave up.

**Gravel or a hard surface along the back of the house.** Not a deterrent, a detection aid. Crunch and a hard surface where soft ground used to be means an approach makes noise, and noise is what gets a dog involved.

**Landscaping that doesn't hand someone a ladder.** A woodpile, a shed, a compost bin, or a mature shrub pressed against the fence line is a step. Keep the line clear, and keep plantings off the outside of the fence where they'd give footing.

**A gate that closes itself.** A self-closing hinge on the pedestrian gate removes the most common human failure in perimeter security — the household member who leaves it open. The fence's security value is only as good as its last user.

## Winter: The Six Weeks This Matters Most

The clock change compresses everything. Compute it for Detroit and the shape of the problem is obvious:

| Date | Sunrise | Sunset | Daylight |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oct 21 (EDT) | 7:52 AM | 6:43 PM | 10h 51m |
| Oct 31 (EDT) | 8:04 AM | 6:29 PM | 10h 24m |
| Nov 1 (EST) | 7:05 AM | 5:27 PM | 10h 22m |
| Nov 21 (EST) | 7:30 AM | 5:07 PM | 9h 36m |
| Dec 21 (EST) | 7:58 AM | 5:03 PM | 9h 04m |

Between publication and the solstice, Oakland County loses **1 hour 47 minutes** of daylight. Most of it comes off the evening. By mid-November the yard is fully dark before most people get home from work, which means the perimeter is unobserved for the hours when a house is most obviously empty.

Three things to do before the ground freezes:

**Walk the perimeter with a flashlight after dark.** Not at 3 PM. After dark. Stand where a stranger would stand and look at your house. You will see things in the dark that the daylight version of you never notices — the gate you can't see from any window, the gap between the shed and the fence, the motion light that doesn't reach the back corner.

**Test the gate with gloves on.** Latches that work in bare hands fail in cold hands, and a latch that ices up gets propped open instead. Check it in November, not January.

**Clear the snow-ramp zone now.** A plow piles snow against the fence, the pile freezes into a ramp, and a barrier that was six feet in November is four feet in February. If a particular run catches the plow, decide now whether it needs a solid panel with no grip, a shovel plan, or a different barrier — and if the gate ices into the ground every year, that's a hardware and clearance problem you can fix in October rather than fight all winter.

**Built to last** applies to the security hardware too. Stainless fasteners and hot-dip galvanized hinges cost marginally more and don't rust out in three Michigan winters. A gate that seizes in February is a gate that stays open in March.

## Corner Lots, Alleys, Greenbelts, and Vacancy

Some lots need more thought than others, and it's worth being specific because "Oakland County" describes a lot of different properties.

**Corner lots** have two street-facing sides and typically the most generous setback requirements, which means less fence where you want it and more open sight line to work with. The security answer on a corner lot is usually the opposite of the privacy answer: keep the street sides lower and more open, and put the solid run on the interior.

**Lots backing onto a greenbelt, drainage easement, or utility corridor** have a rear approach that is invisible from the street and often unmowed. This is the highest-risk configuration in the county and the one most worth a solid back line plus lighting and a camera. Check whether the easement restricts what you can build before you buy anything — the property-line mechanics are covered in our guide to [surveys and Michigan's Fence Viewers Act](/blog/2026-10-03-fence-property-lines-surveys-michigan).

**Shared driveways and alleys** mean your fence line is also somebody else's access route. A gate across a shared drive needs to be a real gate, with a drop rod and hardware that stands up to vehicle-adjacent use, not a decorative panel.

**Vacant and rental properties** are a different risk profile entirely — prolonged absence and high turnover both reduce the informal watching that does most of the work in a residential neighbourhood. If you own a rental or a property between tenants, the fence and lighting matter more, not less, and a maintained line is the cheapest signal of occupancy you have.

## The Insurance Question, Answered Honestly

You'll find a dozen pages claiming a fence lowers your homeowners insurance. Most of them hedge with "potentially" and "some insurers," because there is no published line-item discount for a fence at any major carrier we can find.

What carriers do commonly credit is monitored alarm systems — published ranges run roughly 2% to 20% depending on carrier and state — along with deadbolts, smart locks, and other listed safety devices. A fence is not on those lists.

That doesn't mean the fence is irrelevant to insurance. It means it matters as *documentation*, not as a discount code. Photograph the finished fence and the gate hardware, keep the receipt, and have the information available. If a claim or an underwriting review ever happens, a maintained fence with modern gate hardware is a straightforward story to tell, and a sagging fence with a liftable gate is the opposite. We tell homeowners the same thing we'd tell a neighbour: don't buy a fence for a discount that doesn't exist, and don't skip the paperwork if you do buy one.

## A Perimeter Walk You Can Do This Afternoon

Ten minutes, no tools, before the clocks change.

- Stand at the street and look at your house. How much of the yard can you see? Now imagine the reverse: what can someone in the yard see of the street?
- Open and close the gate. Lift it straight up. If the leaf comes off the hinges, that's the fix.
- Stand outside the gate and try to reach the latch over the top. If you can, so can anyone else.
- Check the gap between the gate and the post on the hinge side. A finger-width gap is a pry point.
- Look for steps next to the fence — a woodpile, a bin, a shed, a shrub, a downspout. Remove them.
- Find the dark corner. Every yard has one. That's where the light goes.
- Check the back door itself. Deadbolt, strike plate, frame. This is the 22%.
- Then check the front door. This is the 34%, and it's the one most homeowners never look at.

## Local Rules and the Neighbour Question

Two things to settle before you build a fence that's meant to be a barrier.

**Check the code for your city, not the county.** Height limits, setbacks, corner-lot visibility triangles, and permit requirements are municipal. The matrix lives in our [Oakland County permits and regulations guide](/blog/2026-05-20-oakland-county-fence-permits-regulations-guide). Getting this wrong is the most common way a good fence project stalls.

**Talk to the neighbour before the panels arrive.** A security fence is a boundary change, and boundaries generate friction. If your solid run faces their kitchen window, or your new gate opens onto a shared driveway, a two-minute conversation before the excavator shows up saves a season of grievance. Michigan's boundary-fence law gives adjoining owners specific rights and a formal dispute route, but almost nothing that reaches that stage needed to.

One more: if you hire anyone for this work, **MCL 445.1108** makes a home-improvement contract over $600 unenforceable by the contractor if it isn't in writing, and **MCL 445.1116** gives you three business days to cancel a contract solicited at your home. Both are worth knowing when someone knocks on the door in November offering a deal.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a wood fence actually deter burglars?

Partly, and it's worth being precise. In offender interviews, physical barriers rated as the strongest deterrent — stronger than cameras or alarms — and 60% of convicted burglars said they'd pick another target if security measures were present. But 65% of entries go through doors a fence doesn't protect. A fence buys time and denies reconnaissance. It is not a burglary solution by itself.

### What percentage of burglars enter through the back door?

22% enter through a back door, against 34% at the front door, 23% through first-floor windows, and 9% through the garage door. A backyard fence protects the yard and the route to the back door — the second most common entry point — while leaving the front door untouched.

### Is a six-foot fence enough to stop someone climbing in?

It stops casual climbing, but six feet isn't a wall. Most Oakland County residential districts cap side and rear fencing at six feet anyway, so the practical answer is different: remove toe holds, keep nothing climbable beside the line, and make the gate the difficult part instead of the panel.

### What is the weakest point of a backyard fence?

The gate, nine times out of ten. It's the only moving part, it carries the most load per post, and standard hinges let the leaf be lifted straight off. Anti-lift hinges, a padlockable latch mounted above reach, and a drop rod on double gates close the biggest gap in a residential fence.

### Do fence spikes stop burglars, and are they legal?

They're a liability problem more than a security measure. Spikes, broken glass on a top rail, and anti-climb paint create injury exposure, can conflict with local code and HOA rules, and are defeated by a jacket laid over the top. We don't install them and don't recommend them on residential fencing in a subdivision.

### Does a solid privacy fence make a house easier to break into?

It can. A solid six-foot panel blocks sight lines in both directions, which means it also hides someone working in your yard from neighbours and from the street. The fix is deliberate design: solid where you need privacy, open or camera-covered where you need visibility, and lighting aimed into the yard rather than at the fence.

### Does a fence lower homeowners insurance in Michigan?

Usually not as a line item. Carriers commonly credit monitored alarms and deadbolts, not fences. What a fence does reliably is support an underwriting or claims conversation — photograph the finished fence and gate hardware and keep the receipt so the improvement is on record.

### What is the best gate lock for a wooden fence?

A padlockable latch or cane bolt mounted high on the inside, paired with anti-lift hinges and a gate stop. Keyed-alike locks mean one key serves house and gate. But the lock matters less than what it's bolted to: a latch on a sagging gate is decoration.

### How do I stop my gate being lifted off its hinges?

Replace standard hinges with anti-lift or security hinges that have a fixed or non-removable pin, or add a hinge-side security plate. On a double gate, a drop rod into a ground receiver closes the other half of the opening. On most residential gates it's a two-hour retrofit.

### When should I check my fence for security gaps in Michigan?

Before daylight saving time ends on November 1. Walk the perimeter with a flashlight after dark, test the gate with gloves on, clear any snow-ramp launch point before the first plow, and confirm exterior lights and timers actually fire. The dark arrives all at once that weekend.

## The Bottom Line

A wood fence is the best access-control product you can put around a Michigan backyard, and it is not a security system.

It cannot protect the 34% of entries that come through your front door. It can hide someone from your neighbours if you build it solid without thinking about sight lines. It will not stop anyone who has decided specifically on your house.

What it does do is real: it denies the reconnaissance that target selection depends on, it closes the side and rear approach to the 22% back door, it buys the ninety seconds that decide whether an opportunistic burglary proceeds, and it marks your property as owned and watched. In a county where most residential districts cap you at six feet, that's a meaningful set of wins — as long as the gate holds, the latch is out of reach, and something lights up the corner of the yard when it shouldn't.
