## Why Western Red Cedar? The Best Wood for Michigan Privacy Fences

When a Rochester Hills homeowner asks us what wood to use for a privacy fence, we don't hedge. If the budget allows, the answer is almost always Western red cedar.

### Natural Durability in Michigan's Climate

Here's something that surprises a lot of homeowners: Michigan's climate is tougher on outdoor structures than most of the country. We get 30 to 45 inches of snow annually. Summer humidity sits in the 70 to 80 percent range. And every winter brings 40 to 60 freeze-thaw cycles that can warp, crack, and split lesser woods.

Cedar handles all of that because of what's inside it. Western red cedar contains natural oils and resins that act as built-in preservatives. These compounds protect the wood from rot, decay, and insect damage without any chemical treatment. Termites and carpenter ants—both of which are active in southeastern Michigan—tend to avoid cedar entirely.

That matters because a fence is a significant investment. Pressure-treated pine, while cheaper upfront, is still prone to warping, twisting, and splitting over time. We've replaced plenty of pine fences that looked rough after just 8 to 10 years in Michigan weather. A well-built cedar fence, on the other hand, will easily last 20 years or more with basic maintenance.

### The Natural Beauty Factor

Beyond durability, cedar is simply a beautiful wood. It has a rich reddish-brown color with distinct, tight grain patterns that you just don't get with treated pine. When the sun catches a new cedar fence in the late afternoon, it has a warmth and depth that transforms a yard.

One of the things the Johnsons loved most about their new cedar fence was how it aged. Unlike pressure-treated pine, which tends to fade to a dull gray pretty quickly, cedar weathers gracefully. If you leave it untreated, it develops an elegant silvery-gray patina over time that many homeowners prefer. The wood's integrity isn't compromised—it's purely an aesthetic change. And if you want to keep that warm reddish tone, a clear sealant every three to five years will do the trick.

For the Rochester Hills project, the Johnsons opted to let the cedar silver naturally. They liked the idea of a low-maintenance fence that would look established and timeless as the years went on.

## Board-on-Board: The Ultimate Privacy Fence Style

Once the Johnsons decided on cedar, the next question was style. And for a corner lot with total privacy as the goal, board-on-board was the obvious choice.

### What Is a Board-on-Board Fence?

A board-on-board fence uses overlapping pickets on alternating sides of the rails. Instead of the standard "good neighbor" style where pickets alternate sides panel by panel, board-on-board gives you full coverage from both sides. There are no gaps at any angle. You can stand at the fence line and see nothing but solid wood.

This is different from a shadowbox fence, which has pickets spaced slightly apart on each side so light filters through. Shadowbox looks nice, but it doesn't deliver total privacy. Board-on-board does.

### Why Board-on-Board Was Right for Rochester Hills

For the Johnsons' corner lot, board-on-board solved multiple problems at once:

**Total privacy from every angle.** With three exposed sides, a standard "good side/bad side" fence would have left the neighbors looking at pickets on one side and rails on the other. Board-on-board looks beautiful from both sides—no bad angles. The neighbors get a clean, finished look, and the Johnsons get complete seclusion.

**Visual screening without a fortress feel.** Even though the fence is solid, the overlapping boards create a subtle visual texture that feels warm and natural rather than like a wall. The cedar grain adds depth. It's private without feeling oppressive.

**Wind resistance.** Here's an underrated benefit of board-on-board construction: the overlapping boards allow a small amount of air to pass through the gaps between the overlapping layers. Solid panel fences catch the wind like a sail, which puts stress on the posts. Board-on-board lets some wind through, which means less strain on the structure during Michigan storm season.

**Sound dampening.** The overlapping boards create an extra layer of mass that helps muffle neighborhood noise. The Johnsons' property backed up to a moderately traveled road, and they noticed the difference immediately after installation. The fence didn't eliminate road noise entirely, but it softened it noticeably.

## The Installation: Behind the Scenes of a Rochester Hills Fence Project

Here's how the project actually went down. We think homeowners appreciate knowing what happens on a real job site, so we're sharing the step-by-step.

### Step 1: Permits and Property Markings

Before we put a shovel in the ground, there were administrative steps to handle. In Rochester Hills, any fence over 3 feet tall requires a zoning permit. The Johnsons' fence was going to be 6 feet, so that meant a permit application through the City of Rochester Hills Building Department.

We also submitted a site plan showing the fence layout, property lines, and setback compliance. Rochester Hills requires fences to be set back from property lines in accordance with Zoning Ordinance Section 138-10.107, and since the Johnsons were in an HOA community, we needed to confirm their covenants allowed the board-on-board style (they did—with written approval from the architectural committee).

We called MISS DIG (811) to mark underground utilities. This is non-negotiable on every job. The utility companies came out within two days and flagged gas, electric, and cable lines across the property. Smart homeowners ask their contractor about this before hiring them. If a fence company doesn't mention utility marking, that's a red flag.

### Step 2: Layout and Staking

With permits approved and utilities marked, we laid out the fence line with string lines and stakes. This is where the planning meets reality. The Johnsons walked the staked perimeter with us, and we fine-tuned the gate locations based on how they actually used their yard.

They wanted the double-drive gate wide enough to get a lawn tractor through (12 feet), and the two walk gates positioned so they didn't have to walk around the entire fence to get from the driveway to the backyard. We placed one walk gate near the patio and one near the side of the house.

### Step 3: Post Installation

For a 6-foot privacy fence, post depth is critical. Michigan's frost line runs about 42 inches deep in Oakland County, and fence posts need to be set below that to avoid frost heave. We dig each post hole to 48 inches—a full 4 feet down. That's overkill for some contractors, but we've seen too many fences shifted by frost heave to cut corners.

Each hole gets 6 to 8 inches of gravel at the bottom for drainage, then the post is set and plumbed with concrete. We use a fast-setting concrete mix that cures in about 24 hours. For this project, with 240 linear feet of fencing and a gate setup, that meant 32 posts total, placed 8 feet apart on center.

The Rochester Hills clay soil was a challenge. The ground here is heavy and dense, which makes digging harder but actually helps with post stability once the concrete sets. We used our hydraulic auger for the straight runs and hand-dug the corners and gate posts to ensure precise placement.

### Step 4: Rail and Picket Installation

Once the concrete cured, we installed the horizontal rails—two per section for a 6-foot fence (one at roughly 2 feet, one at 4 feet, with the posts extending above the top rail for that clean finished look). The rails are pressure-treated for ground contact resistance since they're the structural backbone of the fence.

The pickets are where the craftsmanship really shows. For a board-on-board fence, we install the first layer of pickets on one side of the rails, leaving about an inch of space between each board. Then we install the second layer on the opposite side, centered over the gaps. The result is total coverage from both sides with no visible gaps.

We spaced the pickets about 3/8 inch apart within each layer to allow for wood expansion and contraction with Michigan's humidity swings. Cedar moves with the seasons—it swells in summer humidity and contracts in winter dry air. Leaving that tiny gap prevents the boards from buckling when they expand.

Each picket is fastened with two galvanized deck screws at each rail intersection. We don't use nails for picket attachment—screws hold better over time and are much easier to remove if a single board needs replacing down the road.

### Step 5: Gates

The gates were the trickiest part of this project. The 12-foot double-drive gate needed to be wide enough for vehicle access but strong enough not to sag under its own weight. We built each leaf using a Z-frame design with diagonal bracing, heavy-duty gate hinges, and a wheel at the outer corner to take the load off the hinges when the gate is closed.

The two walk gates were simpler but still required precise framing to swing properly. We installed self-closing hinges on the walk gates for child safety—a feature the Johnsons specifically requested.

### Step 6: Final Cleanup

Once the fence was up and the gates were hung, we did a full site cleanup. We hauled away the excavated dirt, swept the driveway and sidewalk, and picked up every screw and wood scrap. A professional fence installation shouldn't leave your yard looking like a construction site.

## Cost Considerations for a Cedar Privacy Fence in Oakland County

Let's talk numbers, because this is the question we get most often from homeowners considering a cedar fence.

A Western red cedar board-on-board fence in Oakland County typically runs between $35 and $50 per linear foot installed. The exact price depends on height (6-foot vs. 4-foot), gate count and size, and site conditions (rocky soil, slopes, accessibility).

For the Johnsons' project—240 linear feet of 6-foot board-on-board cedar with three gates—the total investment was approximately $10,000 to $11,000. That includes:

- Premium Western red cedar lumber
- Pressure-treated rails and posts
- Concrete for post footings
- Heavy-duty gate hardware
- Professional installation with full cleanup
- Permit handling

Is that expensive? Compared to a chain-link fence at $15 to $20 per foot, yes. Compared to vinyl at $25 to $40 per foot? It's competitive, and cedar looks better. Compared to pressure-treated pine at $20 to $30 per foot? It's a premium. But here's the thing: that pressure-treated pine fence will likely need repairs in 8 to 10 years, and full replacement in 12 to 15. The cedar fence will still be standing strong at year 20 with nothing more than an occasional wash and optional stain.

For homeowners who plan to stay in their Rochester Hills home for the long haul, cedar isn't an expense—it's an investment that pays for itself over time.

### Factors That Affect Cedar Fence Cost in Southeast Michigan

- **Linear footage:** More fence = more materials and labor. Most Oakland County residential lots fall between 150 and 300 linear feet.
- **Height:** 6-foot fences use more material than 4-foot. Most privacy fences are 6 feet.
- **Gates:** Each gate adds hardware, framing, and labor. Walk gates run $200 to $400 installed; drive gates are $500 to $1,200 depending on width.
- **Site conditions:** Rocky soil, steep slopes, or limited access can increase labor time.
- **Demolition:** If there's an existing fence to remove, that adds $3 to $5 per linear foot.

## Rochester Hills Fence Regulations: What You Need to Know

If you're a Rochester Hills homeowner reading this and thinking about your own fence project, here's a quick overview of what the city requires:

- **Permits:** A zoning permit is required for any fence over 3 feet in height. Apply through the Rochester Hills Building Department.
- **Maximum height:** 6 feet in rear and side yards. Front yard fences (within the setback) are limited to 3 feet.
- **Setbacks:** Fences must be placed within property lines. Corner lots have additional visibility requirements at intersections.
- **HOA restrictions:** If you're in a planned community, check your covenants before buying materials. Some HOAs restrict fence styles, materials, or colors.
- **Materials:** No barbed wire, pointed barbs, or race wire in residential zones.

**Important distinction:** The City of Rochester (the downtown area) and Rochester Hills are separate municipalities with different ordinances. Make sure you're looking at the right set of rules for your specific address.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Cedar Privacy Fences

### How much does a cedar privacy fence cost in Rochester Hills, Michigan?

A 6-foot Western red cedar board-on-board privacy fence typically costs between $35 and $50 per linear foot installed in Oakland County. For an average 200-foot lot, that means a total investment of roughly $7,000 to $10,000, depending on site conditions, gate requirements, and design complexity.

### What's the difference between board-on-board and shadowbox fence styles?

Board-on-board uses overlapping pickets on alternating sides of the rails, creating a solid barrier with no gaps from any angle. Shadowbox places pickets with small gaps on both sides, allowing filtered light through while still providing substantial privacy. Board-on-board is the better choice for total privacy; shadowbox is a compromise between privacy and airflow.

### Is cedar worth the extra cost compared to pressure-treated pine?

Yes, for homeowners who plan to stay in their home for more than a few years. Cedar costs about 30 to 50 percent more upfront than pressure-treated pine, but it lasts nearly twice as long (20+ years vs. 10 to 12 years), requires less maintenance, and looks significantly better throughout its lifespan. The total cost of ownership favors cedar over time.

### Do I need a permit for a fence in Rochester Hills?

Yes. Any fence exceeding 3 feet in height requires a zoning permit from the City of Rochester Hills Building Department. BF Fence handles all permit applications for our customers as part of our standard service.

### How long does a cedar fence last in Michigan's climate?

A well-built Western red cedar fence installed by experienced professionals will last 20 to 25 years in Michigan's climate with basic maintenance. The natural oils and resins in cedar provide built-in protection against the rot, moisture, and freeze-thaw damage that can destroy lesser woods in half that time.

### What height fence is allowed in Rochester Hills?

The maximum fence height in Rochester Hills residential zones is 6 feet in rear and side yards. Front yard fences are limited to 3 feet. Corner lots have additional height and setback restrictions to maintain visibility for drivers.

### Does a cedar fence require staining or sealing?

No. Cedar naturally weathers to an attractive silvery-gray patina if left untreated. The wood's structural integrity is not affected by this color change. If you prefer the original reddish-brown tone, applying a clear sealant or semi-transparent stain every 3 to 5 years will preserve it.

### Will a cedar fence increase my property value?

An attractive, well-built privacy fence can increase your home's resale value, particularly in suburban markets like Rochester Hills where privacy and defined outdoor space are highly desired by buyers. Real estate studies consistently show that quality fencing recovers a significant portion of its cost at resale.

### How do I know if my HOA allows cedar fences?

Check your community's Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions (CC&Rs) documents, which are typically available from your HOA board or management company. Some HOAs restrict fence materials, heights, styles, or colors. Always get written HOA approval before proceeding, as we do for every BF Fence project.

### What's the best time of year to install a fence in Michigan?

Spring (April through June) and fall (September through October) are ideal. The ground is workable without being frozen, temperatures are comfortable for the crew, and the concrete has time to cure properly. Summer installations are fine but can be harder on the crew and the lawn. We install year-round, weather permitting.
