# Wood, Vinyl, Aluminum, or Composite? How to Choose the Right Fence Material for Your Oakland County Yard

*Published: August 15, 2026 | By BF Fence*

---

## Start With the Right Question, Not the Right Material

Every summer we get the same phone call. A homeowner in Auburn Hills or Rochester Hills has decided they need a fence, they've spent three evenings watching videos and reading articles, and they're stuck: wood, vinyl, aluminum, or composite? Each one has supporters who swear by it. Each one has horror stories attached. And the internet, unfortunately, treats the question like there's one right answer.

There isn't. There's the right material **for your yard**, and picking it has less to do with which brand has the best marketing and more to do with answering one honest question first:

**What does this fence actually need to do for you?**

That's the question we ask on every free estimate, before we ever talk about boards or panels. Do you need privacy from a two-story neighbor's deck? Do you need to keep a golden retriever and two kids in the yard? Do you need to define a front-yard boundary without blocking the view? Do you need to keep people out of a pool? The material that's right for one job is flat-out wrong for another.

Here's our promise for this guide: we're a local fence company, we install all four materials in Oakland County every year, and we're going to give you the straight talk — cost, lifespan, maintenance, and how each material handles a Michigan winter. If wood is the right call, we'll say so, even though wood means more maintenance for your (and our) future selves. If vinyl is the right call, we'll say that too. We've been doing this long enough to know that a fence you're happy with in year ten is the one that was matched to your yard in year one. That's what "built to last" actually means around here — not the material with the longest warranty sticker, but the one that's right for the job.

## The Four Material Families at a Glance

Before we dive into each material, here's the 30-second version. You'll find the full detail below, but this table is the honest starting point for any Oakland County comparison:

| Material | Typical installed cost (per linear ft, 6-ft privacy, 2026) | Lifespan in MI climate | Maintenance | Privacy | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wood (cedar) | $33–$53 | 15–25 yrs (maintained) | Stain/seal every 2–3 yrs | Excellent | Backyard privacy, property value, natural look |
| Wood (pressure-treated pine) | $25–$35 | 10–15 yrs | Stain/seal every 2–3 yrs | Excellent | Budget-conscious privacy, pet containment |
| Vinyl (PVC) | $35–$50 | 20–30 yrs | Wash occasionally | Excellent (panel styles) | Zero-maintenance seekers, HOA-approved looks |
| Aluminum (ornamental) | $25–$75 | 25–30+ yrs | Wash occasionally | None (see note) | Front yards, pools, property demarcation |
| Aluminum (privacy/slatted) | $75–$130 | 25–30+ yrs | Wash occasionally | Good | Premium privacy with modern look |
| Composite (WPC) | $40–$60 | 25–30+ yrs | Wash occasionally | Excellent | Wood look without the staining |

*Note: "aluminum privacy" means slat-and-rail systems that add boards into an ornamental frame — a real thing, but the most expensive residential option out there.*

Three things stand out from that table, and they'll matter the rest of this guide:

1. **There is no free lunch.** The materials that cost the least upfront (pine) demand the most care. The ones that demand the least care (composite, quality vinyl) cost the most upfront. That trade-off is the entire decision.
2. **Privacy is not a given.** Aluminum in its classic ornamental form is gorgeous and nearly indestructible — and you can see straight through it. If privacy is the job, aluminum is doing a different job.
3. **The boards aren't the whole story.** Every one of these fences is only as good as the posts under it. You'll hear us say it a dozen more times in this guide, because in Michigan, it's the truest thing about fencing.

## Wood: The Classic Choice, and Why It's Still the Default

Wood is the fence material most people picture when they say "fence." It's also the material we build more of than anything else at BF Fence, and not just because we're sentimental. In Oakland County, wood remains the best all-around value for the two jobs that dominate residential fencing: privacy and property value.

### The three woods that matter in Michigan

**Western red cedar.** This is the gold standard for Michigan privacy fences. Cedar is naturally rot-resistant — it contains natural oils that repel moisture and insects, which is exactly what you want in a climate that freezes, thaws, and rains for half the year. Properly maintained, a cedar fence in Oakland County will give you 15 to 25 years, and it ages beautifully: a well-stained cedar fence keeps that warm, rich tone for years, and even left to weather, it turns a handsome silver-gray instead of rotting out. Cedar takes stain and paint beautifully, which means it's the most customizable fence on the market — you can match it to your house, your deck, your landscaping. The trade-off is price: it's the most expensive wood, running $33–$53 per linear foot installed here in 2026.

**Pressure-treated pine.** This is the honest budget option, and for a lot of yards, it's the right one. Southern yellow pine is pressure-treated with preservatives so it resists rot and insects — the treatment is why it can survive Michigan ground contact for a decade or more. Installed cost runs $25–$35 per linear foot, which makes it the cheapest privacy fence you can build with a professional crew. The trade-offs: it's a less attractive wood than cedar (though a good stain closes most of the gap), and it has a shorter lifespan — 10 to 15 years in our climate. Treated pine also has a tendency to warp and check as it dries, so the boards need a little more babysitting during installation and their first year. For a homeowner who wants privacy, wants it done right, and wants to watch the budget, pressure-treated pine is a perfectly honest choice — with the understanding that you're trading longevity for price.

**Redwood.** We get asked about redwood often, and here's the short version: redwood is beautiful, rot-resistant, and expensive, and it's really a West Coast material. It ships well and performs fine in Michigan, but you're paying a premium for a tree that grew 2,000 miles away, and cedar gives you the same performance for less. If a customer specifically wants redwood, we'll build it. But cedar is the better Michigan value, and we'll tell you that before you write the check.

### The maintenance reality (be honest with yourself here)

Wood requires maintenance. That's the deal. To get 20-plus years out of a cedar fence, you should plan to stain and seal it every two to three years. That's a weekend project, or about $300–$600 per year if you hire it out — and we'll tell you the truth: **most people stop doing it after year three or four.** We can always spot those fences — they're the ones that look gray and tired by year seven, with a few boards starting to cup at the base.

Here's the part that surprises people: even a neglected wood fence usually isn't dangerous or falling over. It's just ugly. The structure — posts, rails, framing — holds up for a long time. What suffers is the look, and for a privacy fence, the look is most of the point.

That maintenance reality is exactly why this decision is personal. If you know, deep down, that you will not be on a ladder with a stain pad every couple of summers, wood may not be your best material — and that's a legitimate reason to look at the next three options.

## Vinyl: The Zero-Maintenance Promise, With a Michigan Caveat

Vinyl fencing — PVC, usually — is the material that sells itself. No staining. No sealing. No rot, ever. It looks clean and uniform the day it's installed and, in a lot of cases, the day it's removed 25 years later. The "set it and forget it" pitch is real, and for the right homeowner, vinyl is genuinely the best choice in this guide.

Installed cost runs $35–$50 per linear foot for a 6-foot privacy panel — roughly comparable to cedar, sometimes a touch more. Lifespan claims are 20 to 30 years, and honestly, that's mostly accurate: vinyl doesn't rot, doesn't warp, doesn't attract insects. It doesn't need to be painted because the color is molded in. It comes in a handful of colors (white, tan, gray, and a few wood-look finishes) and in panel styles that give you the privacy of a solid fence.

### The cold-weather truth about vinyl

Here's what the sales brochures leave out, and what any Michigan installer will tell you: **vinyl gets brittle in extreme cold.** At temperatures below about 20 degrees — and Oakland County sees plenty of those nights — cheaper vinyl panels lose flexibility, and a hard impact (a snowball fight, a shoveling mishap, a falling branch, a kid slamming a gate) can crack or shatter a panel. We've replaced more than a few vinyl panels after brutal winters, and the failures are almost always in the coldest weeks.

The other cold-weather issue is snow load. Vinyl panels are hollow; they're strong in one direction and surprisingly weak in others. Heavy, wet snow piling against a vinyl fence can bow or crack panels, especially if the fence is older or the panels are a budget grade. This is the classic "cheap vinyl" trap: the bargain vinyl you see at big-box stores is a completely different product from the heavy-gauge vinyl a real fence company installs. The difference between a $20 panel and a $40 panel is the difference between cracking in year two and standing for decades.

And one more thing nobody mentions: vinyl doesn't paint well. The color is in the material, so if your taste changes — or the tan starts to feel dated — you're living with it or replacing the fence. You can't refinish vinyl the way you can refinish wood.

**Who vinyl is for:** the homeowner who wants a clean uniform look with zero maintenance. **Who it's not for:** anyone who wants a natural look, a fence that can be refinished as taste changes, or the absolute toughest fence for a yard full of kids and dogs in a rough winter.

## Aluminum: Beautiful, Indestructible, and (Usually) Not Private

Aluminum fencing is the material people discover when they've ruled out wood maintenance and vinyl looks. And it's a great material — for the right job. The classic ornamental aluminum fence (those elegant black or bronze picket-and-rail styles you see around front yards and pools) is lightweight, rust-proof, and built to last 25 years or more with essentially no maintenance. It never rots, never warps, never needs painting. In Michigan's snow-and-salt winters, aluminum shrugs and asks for more.

Installed costs: ornamental aluminum runs $40–$75 per linear foot; picket styles $25–$50; pool fencing $30–$45. Split-rail aluminum (for acreage or boundary lines) runs $20–$40. And if you want *privacy* in aluminum — slatted panels built into an ornamental frame — that's the premium end of the entire residential market at $75–$130 per linear foot.

### The privacy problem

Here's the thing you need to hear clearly: **classic aluminum fencing provides almost no privacy.** It's designed to be see-through — that's the look. It marks a boundary, it keeps kids and dogs in (if the pickets are tight enough), it keeps people out, and it looks sharp doing it. But if your goal is to block the neighbor's view of your deck, aluminum is not your material. We've had customers pick aluminum for the look, then realize they can see the neighbor's patio furniture as clearly as before, and we've had to walk them back to wood or a slatted aluminum system.

Aluminum is at its best in three situations:
1. **Front yards** where you want curb appeal and a defined boundary without blocking sight lines (and without fighting a height ordinance).
2. **Pool safety**, where code requires a barrier and aluminum's open design satisfies inspectors while looking intentional.
3. **Property demarcation** — commercial lots, acreage lines, side-yard boundaries where privacy is irrelevant and durability is everything.

One honest caveat from the field: aluminum posts need to be set right — in concrete, below the frost line, just like wood. We've seen DIY aluminum installs where the posts were set in a few inches of gravel, and after one Michigan winter the whole run is leaning. The material doesn't forgive a bad post hole.

## Composite: The Wood Look Without the Staining — At a Price

Composite fencing — wood-plastic composite, or WPC — is the newest serious contender in this comparison, and it's the material that's quietly eating into both wood and vinyl sales. Composite boards are made from recycled wood fiber and plastic, molded into boards that look convincingly like real wood — often *better* than real wood, because the color is consistent and there are no knots or checks. The pitch: all the beauty of a wood fence, none of the staining.

Installed cost runs $40–$60 per linear foot for a 6-foot privacy fence — noticeably more than cedar, and in some configurations, more than vinyl. Lifespan claims run 25 to 30-plus years. Maintenance is essentially zero beyond an occasional wash.

### What the 20-year math actually says

This is where the numbers get interesting, and it's the closest thing this guide has to a "gotcha." Let's do the arithmetic for a 100-foot, 6-foot privacy fence, using realistic 2026 numbers:

| Material | Install cost (100 ft) | Maintenance over 20 yrs | 20-year total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pressure-treated pine | $2,500–$3,500 | $4,000–$8,000 (staining) | $6,500–$11,500 |
| Cedar | $3,300–$5,300 | $3,000–$6,000 (staining) | $6,300–$11,300 |
| Composite | $4,000–$6,000 | $0–$1,000 | $4,000–$7,000 |
| Vinyl (quality) | $3,500–$5,000 | $0–$500 | $3,500–$5,500 |

Read that table carefully, because there are two honest stories in it.

**Story one:** If you actually maintain a wood fence — stain every two or three years, every single cycle — wood's 20-year cost is competitive, and you got a natural material that looked great the whole time. The assumption buried in that math is that you *will* maintain it. The national data says most homeowners stop after about three years.

**Story two:** If you're honest that you won't maintain it, the "expensive" composite fence becomes the cheapest fence in this comparison over two decades — because you're not paying for staining you'll never do, and you're not paying to replace a rotted fence in year twelve. The same logic applies to quality vinyl. That's why we say the maintenance question is the *real* question — more than the material question.

### Michigan winter performance

Composite is generally winter-friendly: it doesn't absorb water the way wood does, so freeze-thaw cycles don't crack or split it, and it doesn't get brittle the way vinyl can. The two things to know: (1) composite expands and contracts with temperature more than wood, so the installation needs proper gapping — a detail a good crew handles and a sloppy one misses; (2) cheaper composite grades can fade in intense sun, and while Michigan doesn't have Arizona sun, our summer UV is real. Buy a grade with a fade warranty and you're covered.

## The Michigan Climate Reality Check

You've heard us reference winters throughout this guide, and now it's time to be direct about what Michigan does to fences — because this is the difference between a guide written by a national website and one written by the people who install fences here. National articles treat "climate" as a paragraph. For us, it's the whole story.

**Freeze-thaw cycles.** Oakland County's ground freezes and thaws repeatedly every winter — often 20-plus cycles in a bad year. Each cycle moves the soil, pushes on posts, and stresses any material that absorbs moisture. Wood absorbs moisture; freeze-thaw is why unmaintained wood cracks and cups. Vinyl and composite don't absorb moisture, which is their quiet superpower here. Aluminum doesn't care at all.

**The frost line.** This is the big one, and it's the most important technical fact in this entire guide: **in Oakland County, the frost line is 42 to 48 inches deep.** Fence posts must be set a minimum of 42 inches below grade — and gate posts deeper — in concrete, with the bottom of the post below the frost line. Do that, and your fence will stand for decades. Don't do it — set posts 18 inches deep like a handyman special — and the freeze-thaw cycle will heave them out of the ground within two or three winters. **Every leaning fence we tear out in this county is a fence whose posts weren't set deep enough.** Not one of them failed because the boards were the wrong material. The boards were fine. The posts moved.

**Snow and wind load.** Michigan snow is heavy — a single storm can drop a foot of wet snow against a fence run, and winter wind can hit 40-plus miles per hour across open lots. Wood, with proper posts and framing, handles this well. Quality vinyl handles it well too, though snow shoveling right against vinyl is how panels crack. Aluminum's open design lets wind and snow pass through, which is one more reason it's nearly indestructible here.

**The takeaway:** In Michigan, the material you choose matters less than *how it's installed*. A properly installed pine fence will outlast a badly installed composite fence every time. That's not a slogan — that's the frost line talking.

## Privacy and Security: Scoring the Materials

Since privacy and security are the two reasons most Oakland County homeowners build a fence at all, here's how the four materials actually perform:

| Job | Wood | Vinyl | Aluminum | Composite |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blocks sight lines | ✅ Excellent | ✅ Excellent (panel styles) | ❌ None (open) / ✅ slatted premium | ✅ Excellent |
| Keeps pets in | ✅ Good | ✅ Good | ⚠️ Only with tight pickets | ✅ Good |
| Deters intruders | ⚠️ Climbs with toeholds | ⚠️ Climbs | ✅ Hard to climb | ⚠️ Climbs |

If it's a pet-and-kids yard, any of them work as long as the posts are right and the gates are solid — which is the part people underestimate. A flimsy gate undermines the best fence in the world.

## Matching the Material to Your Yard

Enough theory. Here's how we actually walk Oakland County homeowners through this on estimates, yard by yard:

**The corner lot.** You have two exposed sides and neighbors on two more. Privacy matters on the sides; a front-yard fence has to work with the street view and the local ordinance (most communities cap front fences at 3 to 4 feet). Common play: wood privacy on the sides and rear, ornamental aluminum or a short picket across the front. Two materials, one coherent yard.

**The pool yard.** Building code requires a safety barrier (typically 4 feet minimum with a self-closing, self-latching gate). Aluminum is the classic pool fence — code-friendly, rust-proof, looks intentional. If you also want privacy, wood or composite around the rest of the yard with aluminum at the pool zone is a beautiful combo.

**The pet yard.** A dog that jumps, digs, or squeezes needs specifics: 6-foot height for jumpers, concrete or buried posts with a kickboard at the bottom for diggers, no gaps wider than the dog's head for squeezers. Wood, vinyl, or composite all work — the material matters less than the construction detail. (If you have a serious escape artist, ask us about the "puppy picket" spacing trick that works in aluminum too.)

**The small yard.** You're paying per linear foot, and a 60-foot run costs the same per foot as a 300-foot run. If you're fencing a small lot, the *look* matters more because the fence is a bigger percentage of what you see. Cedar or composite tend to win here — you're spending less total, so upgrade the material.

**The HOA or subdivision.** Read the rules *before* you fall in love with a material. Some communities restrict fence heights, materials, or colors. We see vinyl-heavy subdivisions, wood-heavy ones, and the occasional "aluminum only" front-yard rule. We'll pull your community's rules during the estimate and tell you what's actually buildable before you commit to anything.

**The front-yard boundary.** If you want a line, not a wall, aluminum is hard to beat: elegant, low-maintenance, and it doesn't make your front yard feel like a compound.

**The "I want privacy and I want it to look expensive" yard.** Composite. It's the material that says "quality" at first glance, holds its color, and never needs a stain appointment.

## Five Buying Mistakes We See Every Year

1. **Shopping price-per-linear-foot only.** The cheapest quote usually means the cheapest material grade, thinner boards, and — critically — shallower posts. A pine fence with posts set to 42 inches will outlive a "bargain cedar" fence with 18-inch posts. Ask every contractor you talk to how deep they set posts. The answer tells you everything.

2. **Forgetting the gates.** A gate is the most-used, most-abused part of a fence, and it's where cheap work shows up. A sagging gate ruins a $6,000 fence. Make sure your quote includes proper gate posts (deeper, bigger) and real hardware, not the flimsy hinge kit from a big-box store.

3. **Picking vinyl from a photo.** Budget vinyl looks fine in a showroom and fails in a Michigan February. If vinyl is your choice, ask for the panel gauge and the cold-weather warranty. There's a world of difference between cheap and commercial-grade.

4. **Ignoring the property line.** Half the fence disputes in Oakland County start with a fence built six inches over the neighbor's line. A survey is cheap insurance (and sometimes your municipality requires one). We'll help you read it, but don't skip it.

5. **Building the fence to the wrong height.** Your community's ordinance — often 6 feet in the rear, less in front — is the law, and we've seen too many homeowners pay to build, then pay to tear down. Ask about the height limit *before* you order materials.

## FAQ: Fence Material Comparison

### What is the best fence material for Michigan winters?
For Oakland County's freeze-thaw climate, the most reliable combination is wood (cedar or pressure-treated pine) installed on posts set at least 42 inches deep below the frost line — or quality vinyl or composite if you want zero maintenance. The single most important factor isn't the board material; it's proper post installation. Cheap vinyl can get brittle and crack in extreme cold, while wood needs regular sealing to resist moisture damage.

### Is a wood or vinyl fence cheaper in the long run?
If you actually maintain a wood fence — staining every two to three years — wood is competitive over 20 years. If you're honest that you won't maintain it, quality vinyl is usually cheaper over two decades because you skip thousands of dollars in staining. Upfront, pressure-treated pine is the cheapest; cedar and quality vinyl are similar; composite is the most expensive to install.

### Do vinyl fences crack in cold weather?
Cheaper vinyl panels can get brittle and crack at temperatures below about 20 degrees Fahrenheit, especially when hit by snow shovels, falling branches, or heavy impacts. Commercial-grade vinyl with proper panel thickness is much more cold-resistant. In Michigan, it's worth paying for quality vinyl or asking about the cold-weather warranty.

### How long do aluminum fences last?
Ornamental aluminum fences typically last 25 to 30-plus years with essentially no maintenance — they don't rust, rot, or warp. Aluminum is one of the most durable fence materials available, provided the posts are set properly in concrete below the frost line.

### Is composite fencing worth the extra cost?
If you would otherwise skip wood maintenance, yes — composite's 20-year total cost often beats wood because you avoid staining costs and early replacement. Composite costs more upfront ($40–$60 per linear foot installed) but needs almost no maintenance, doesn't rot, and doesn't get brittle in cold weather. If you'll genuinely maintain wood, wood can match composite's long-term value for less money.

### Which fence material needs the least maintenance?
Vinyl and aluminum are the lowest-maintenance options — neither needs staining, sealing, or painting; an occasional wash with a hose is all they require. Composite is also nearly maintenance-free. Wood is the only material in the comparison that requires regular upkeep: staining and sealing every two to three years.

### What fence material is best for privacy?
Wood (cedar or pressure-treated pine) is the classic privacy fence — solid sight-line blocking with a natural look. Vinyl panel fences and composite also provide excellent privacy. Classic ornamental aluminum does not block sight lines at all; only expensive slatted aluminum privacy systems do.

### Which fence material adds the most value to a home?
A well-maintained wood fence — particularly cedar — consistently adds the most perceived value to an Oakland County home because it looks natural and expensive, and buyers recognize the craftsmanship. Quality vinyl also appeals to buyers who want zero maintenance. The key is that the fence must look good and be structurally sound; a sagging fence of any material hurts value.
