## The Problem: An Open Yard with No Privacy

Every backyard has a story. This one started with a young family in Auburn Hills who had just bought their first home. The house was perfect — three bedrooms, a big kitchen, and a backyard that stretched out behind it with mature trees and plenty of green space. There was just one problem.

Everyone could see in.

From their kitchen window, the homeowners could see directly into the neighbor's family room. From the patio, passersby on the street had a clear line of sight to their grill, their kids' play set, and every dinner they ate outside.

"It felt like we were living in a fishbowl," the homeowner told us during the initial consultation. "We couldn't let our toddler play in the backyard without watching him every second, and forget about having friends over for a barbecue — you felt like everyone on the street was watching."

This is a story we hear all the time from Oakland County homeowners. Open property lines might work for sprawling farms, but in suburban Auburn Hills, Rochester Hills, and surrounding communities, the desire for privacy is one of the top reasons people call us for a fence estimate.

The family had looked into other options before reaching out to BF Fence. They'd considered vinyl fencing — cheaper upfront, they thought — but after talking to neighbors who'd gone that route, they heard the same story: vinyl gets brittle in Michigan winters, cracks appear within a few years, and the color fades unevenly in the sun. They didn't want to replace a fence in five years.

They wanted something built to last.

## The Plan: Custom Board-on-Board Privacy Fence

After the consultation, we put together a detailed plan. Here's what we recommended:

### Material: Premium Pressure-Treated Pine

For this project, we recommended pressure-treated pine with a premium-grade treatment rating. Here's why:

Pressure-treated pine is the workhorse of Michigan fencing. It's affordable, naturally rot-resistant when properly treated, and — most importantly — it can handle the freeze-thaw cycle that destroys lesser materials. In Michigan, the ground freezes and thaws an average of 50-60 times per winter. Each cycle puts stress on fence posts and panels.

The treatment process forces preservative chemicals deep into the wood fibers, protecting against rot, insects, and moisture damage. Premium-grade treatment means a higher retention level of preservative, which translates directly into longer fence life.

**Cedar** would have been the premium option — naturally rot-resistant without chemical treatment, with a beautiful reddish grain. But for a privacy fence of this size, pressure-treated pine delivered 90% of the performance at about 60% of the cost. The homeowner chose to invest the savings into additional height and a few design upgrades.

### Height: 6-Foot Standard

The City of Auburn Hills allows residential privacy fences up to 6 feet in height for backyards. We maxed it out. At 6 feet tall, a board-on-board fence blocks the view of someone standing on the ground — and even someone looking from a second-story window has their view significantly obstructed.

### Style: Board-on-Board (Privacy, Both Sides)

We recommended a board-on-board design. Here's the difference: a standard "good neighbor" fence has boards on one side of the rails, so one neighbor sees the finished face and the other sees the rails. Board-on-board alternates boards on both sides, creating a fully finished look from both directions with no gaps.

For this homeowner, board-on-board made sense because:
- The fence faced a street on one side and a neighbor on the other
- Both sides needed to look clean and finished
- The overlapping boards create a stronger, more rigid panel
- Zero gaps means zero visibility — true privacy

### Post Setting: 42 Inches Deep with Concrete

This is the most critical part of any fence installation in Michigan, and it's where cheap contractors cut corners. A fence is only as good as the holes it sits in.

We set every post 42 inches deep — well below the frost line in Oakland County. Each hole got 6 inches of gravel at the bottom for drainage, then the post, then concrete poured to just below grade. The gravel prevents water from pooling at the base of the post, which is what causes rot at ground level.

Setting posts this deep takes more time and more concrete, but it means the fence will still be standing straight a decade from now while fences with 24-inch-deep posts are leaning at 30-degree angles.

### Gate Configuration: Double Gate at Driveway + Single Walk Gate

We installed a 10-foot double gate at the driveway entrance for easy access with lawn equipment, and a 3-foot walk gate on the side for everyday use. Both gates got heavy-duty hinges and self-closing springs — small details that make a big difference in daily usability.

## The Installation: A Step-by-Step Look

### Day 1: Marking and Digging

The crew arrived at 8:00 AM. First step: double-check the property line markers. We always ask homeowners to provide a recent property survey or stakeout, and this family had one ready. We marked the fence line with string and spray paint, flagging each post location.

Then the digging began. For a fence of this size, we use a two-person auger for the straight sections and hand dig around obstacles. In Auburn Hills, the soil is mostly clay with a layer of topsoil on top. Clay drains slowly, which reinforces why that gravel base at the bottom of each hole is so important.

By the end of day one, all 20 post holes were dug, gravel was in place, and the corner posts were set and level.

### Day 2: Setting Posts and Framing

The remaining posts went in on day two — a mix of line posts (every 8 feet) and gate posts (beefier 4x4s with additional concrete for stability). Each post was plumbed, braced, and left to set overnight.

Meanwhile, we started cutting the horizontal rails that would connect the posts. For a 6-foot fence, we use three rails per section — top, middle, and bottom — for maximum rigidity.

### Day 3: Panel Assembly

This is where the fence starts to look like a fence. We attached the first layer of vertical pickets to the rails — every board individually cut, spaced, and nailed. Then the second layer went on the opposite side, staggered to cover the gaps.

Board-on-board is more labor-intensive than standard fencing. Each picket is individually placed rather than using pre-assembled panels. But the result is a stronger, better-looking, truly private fence.

### Day 4: Gates and Finishing

The double gate required the most engineering. At 10 feet wide, it needed extra bracing to prevent sagging over time. We used a diagonal truss system and heavy-duty strap hinges rated for 500 pounds.

We installed a gate latch with a padlock hasp, self-closing hinges, and a drop rod on the passive leaf to keep it stable when closed.

The walk gate got the same treatment — heavy-duty hinges, self-closing mechanism, and a thumb latch that works smoothly even in cold weather.

### Day 5: Cleanup and Walkthrough

Final day. We trimmed any boards that needed trimming, swept up sawdust, picked up scrap material, and did a full walkthrough with the homeowner.

They walked the entire perimeter, opened and closed both gates, checked the gaps, and stood in different spots in the yard to test the privacy.

"This is better than we imagined," the homeowner said. "We thought it would be nice, but it actually changes the whole feel of the yard."

## The Technical Details: Why This Fence Will Last

One of the most common questions we get is, "How long will this fence last?" The answer depends on three things: material quality, installation quality, and maintenance. Here's what this fence has going for it:

### Material Quality
- Premium-grade pressure-treated pine with .40 retention level (higher than the standard .25)
- Hot-dipped galvanized nails and screws (won't rust)
- Pressure-treated 4x4 posts (larger than the 3x3 posts many contractors use)
- Heavy-duty gate hardware with stainless steel fasteners

### Installation Quality
- Posts set 42 inches deep (below frost line)
- Gravel drainage base at bottom of every hole
- Concrete poured to just below grade (prevents water wicking)
- Three-rail construction per section (not two)
- Custom-built on site (not pre-fab panels)

### Maintenance Requirements
- The homeowners plan to apply a semi-transparent stain every 3-4 years
- Keeping vegetation trimmed back from the fence line
- Annual inspection for loose boards or nails
- Re-sealing the top of posts to prevent water infiltration

With this level of care, this fence should easily last 15-20 years. Some of our fences in Oakland County are still standing strong after 25 years.

## Lessons for Oakland County Homeowners Considering a Privacy Fence

If you're reading this and thinking about a privacy fence for your own backyard, here's what we want you to take away from this project:

### 1. Know Your Property Lines
Before you do anything, get a property survey. We can't stress this enough. A fence built even a few inches over the property line can lead to disputes, removal orders, and legal headaches. A recent survey is worth every penny.

### 2. Check Local Regulations
Every municipality in Oakland County has different rules. Auburn Hills allows 6-foot privacy fences in backyards but limits front yard fences to 3-4 feet. Rochester Hills has similar rules but different setback requirements. Farmington Hills requires permits for any fence over 3 feet. Check with your local building department before you start.

### 3. Invest in Good Posts
The posts are the foundation of your fence. Cheap posts mean a cheap fence that won't last. We set every post 42 inches deep in concrete — it takes longer and costs more, but your fence will still be standing a decade from now.

### 4. Think About Both Sides
A privacy fence is something you and your neighbors will look at every day. Board-on-board designs look great from both sides and prevent neighbor friction. Standard "good neighbor" fences leave one side showing rails — and guess which side your neighbor sees?

### 5. Consider Your Lifestyle
How will you actually use your yard? If you have kids, prioritize safety features like self-closing gates and secure latches. If you entertain, think about where gates should go for easy access. If you garden, consider how the fence will affect sunlight and airflow.

### 6. Don't Rush the Decision
A fence is a long-term investment. Take your time. Get multiple quotes. Ask questions. A good contractor will be happy to explain their process and material choices. If someone is rushing you to sign, that's a red flag.

### 7. Plan for Maintenance
Every wood fence needs some maintenance. Plan to clean and re-stain or re-seal every 3-4 years. Keep vegetation trimmed back. Fix small problems before they become big ones. A little maintenance goes a long way toward a 20-year fence life.
