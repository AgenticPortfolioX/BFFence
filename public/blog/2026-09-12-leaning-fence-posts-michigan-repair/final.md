## Why Do Fence Posts Lean in Michigan?

For three reasons, and they show up together more often than not: frost heave, rot at the ground line, and drainage that keeps the soil around the post wet all year.

### Frost heave: what's actually happening under your yard

Michigan's ground freezes and thaws repeatedly through the winter. When water in soil freezes, it expands — and that expansion pushes upward on anything sitting in the frost zone.

A fence post with a concrete footing is especially vulnerable, because concrete gives frost a big, flat surface to grab. The foothold doesn't just nudge the post sideways. It lifts the whole footing. Contractors who do this for a living will tell you the same thing: heaved footings can move an inch to four inches in a season. They don't always settle back where they started. Do that for two or three winters and the post that was dead plumb at installation is leaning, the panels are out of level, and the gate has started to drag.

### The 42-inch rule: Michigan's frost line and why shallow posts always lose

**Michigan Residential Code R403.1.4 is blunt about it: all exterior footings and foundation systems must extend 42 inches below actual grade.** That's the statewide baseline for residential construction, and it exists because of exactly this problem.

Forty-two inches is a floor, not a ceiling. Best practice in cold-climate construction — the approach adopted by building departments in Michigan, Minnesota, and Wisconsin — is to set the *bottom* of a footing a minimum of six inches below the published frost line, to account for soil variation and how dry the ground was the year the depth was measured. Frost depth in Michigan runs 42 to 48 inches depending on where you are.

What that means in practice: for a 6-foot privacy fence, many Michigan contractors use an 8-foot post and set the bottom 48 inches in the ground. That puts the base of the concrete below the frost line, in soil that doesn't freeze, which is the only reliable way to stop heave.

You'll see a lot of national fence and DIY sites quote a rule of thumb that the in-ground portion should be about one-third of the post's total length — roughly 36 inches for a 6-foot fence. That's a reasonable general guideline and it is not a Michigan standard. Thirty-six inches leaves your footing inside the frost zone in most Michigan soils. In heavy clay, it's a coin flip whether it survives the winter.

And here's the tell. **If your post has a concrete plug sitting above the grass, the post was set too shallow.** That plug got pushed up by frost, and the post went with it.

### Rot at the ground line: the number one post killer

Frost heave gets the attention, but rot is what actually kills the most posts — and it's the one homeowners can't see.

Wood posts don't rot evenly. They rot in a band right at and just below the soil surface that folks in the trade call the ground contact line. It's the worst possible place for wood to sit, because moisture there doesn't stay constant — it cycles. The soil saturates after every rain, then dries, then saturates again. That wet-dry cycling breaks wood down faster than constant moisture or constant dryness would.

The result is a post that looks solid when you knock on it at chest height and is completely hollow four inches below the grass. By the time it's visibly leaning, the decay has usually spread far enough to compromise the panels around it too.

A few hard truths about rot:

- **Pressure treatment helps. It does not make wood immortal.** Ground contact grades last far longer, but every post in soil eventually has a clock running.
- **Decay organisms need oxygen.** Below roughly six inches of burial there isn't enough oxygen for decay fungi to survive, which is why the top two inches above grade down through the top six inches below grade is the band that needs protecting.
- **Concrete set directly against bare wood can make rot worse**, not better. It traps water against the post right at the ground line. The installers who get this right crown the concrete slightly above grade and slope it away from the post so water sheds instead of pooling.

### Drainage, soil, and Oakland County clay

The third cause is the one you can actually fix around the yard: water.

Well-drained sandy soil has far lower frost-heave potential and drains away from wood. Poorly drained clay holds water against the post year-round, which accelerates both rot and heave. A lot of Oakland County yards have heavy clay, and a lot of them have a downspout discharging within a few feet of a fence line. Low spots that pond after a rain do the same thing.

If the soil around your fence posts stays wet days after a storm, you have found your problem — and you'll find it again on the next fence if you don't redirect that water.

### Wind load, gate load, and string trimmers

Three other things finish posts off early:

**Wind load.** A solid 6-foot privacy fence is a sail. Every gust pushes laterally on the post, and the taller and more solid the fence, the bigger the force. Underbuilt or shallow posts lose that fight.

**Gate load.** A gate post carries the weight of the gate plus the force of it swinging open and closing every day. Gate posts should be set 4 to 6 inches deeper than line posts at minimum, and many contractors go 8 inches deeper. Under-set gate posts are the most common reason a gate starts dragging.

**String trimmer damage.** Weed-whacking along the fence line repeatedly hits the same 6-to-12-inch band at the base of the posts, stripping the treated outer shell and exposing untreated wood exactly where rot starts.

## How to Diagnose a Failing Post in Five Minutes

You don't need tools to figure out whether a post needs a reset, a sister post, or a full replacement. You need your hands and about five minutes per post. Walk the line and run these five checks.

**1. The push test.** Push the post firmly at chest height, both directions. A sound post doesn't move. A post that rocks has lost its footing or its base.

**2. The probe test.** Take a screwdriver or a pocketknife and press it into the wood at ground level and just below. Sound, treated wood resists. If the tool sinks in easily — or the wood crumbles rather than splinters — you have active rot.

**3. The plumb test.** Hold a level (or your phone's level app) against the post, or just sight down the fence line from one end. One leaning post is easy to spot from thirty feet back.

**4. The concrete-plug check.** Look at grade around the base. A concrete collar sitting above the grass means frost has already lifted that footing. That's a depth problem, not a wood problem.

**5. The panel check.** Grab the panel next to the post and wiggle. If panels are loose, rails are popping, or fasteners are pulling, the post is failing and the fence is telling you.

## Your Repair Options, From Temporary to Permanent

There are five ways to deal with a leaning post. They are not interchangeable, and the cheapest one is usually the one you'll pay for twice.

### Option 1: Reset the post in place

**Best when:** the post is structurally sound and only the footing has moved.

This is the honest fix for a heaved post with good wood. The crew digs around the footing, breaks out or lifts the old concrete, pulls the post plumb, and re-sets it in fresh concrete with the base below the frost line. The fence line comes back true, the panels re-level, and the gate swings again.

The catch: this only works if the wood is sound. Resetting a rotted post is money in a hole — literally.

### Option 2: The sister post method

**Best when:** rot is confined to the bottom of the post and the upper section is solid.

A new post — the sister — is set in concrete right alongside the damaged one, then fastened to the original post with heavy-duty structural fasteners or purpose-made post repair hardware. The load transfers to the new post and the fence line stabilizes.

This is a genuinely good repair when it's done right, and it saves a lot of disassembly. It's also the repair most often done badly: a sister post set at the same shallow depth as the original will heave just like the original did. If somebody offers you a sister post fix, ask how deep they're going. The answer should be below the frost line.

### Option 3: Steel no-dig anchor spike (post repair kits)

**Best when:** you need to stabilize a post quickly, or the accessible part of the post is sound and you want a fast, low-cost stabilization.

These kits — usually a heavy-duty H-shaped galvanized steel channel about 34 inches long — get driven into the soil beside the post and screwed to the sound portion of the wood with structural screws. No digging, no concrete, done in minutes. Home centers sell them for $25 to $50.

**Here's the honest version of what they do.** A no-dig spike is a stabilizer, not a footing. It shares load with the existing post and stops the wobble. It does not get below the 42-inch frost line in most cases, it does not fix rot, and it will not hold a post whose base has already failed. Used on an early-stage lean with sound wood, it's a smart, economical call. Used as a permanent fix on a rotted post at the end of its life, it buys you a season at most — and often makes the eventual replacement harder because now there's buried steel in the way.

We install them when the situation calls for it and we tell people when it doesn't. That's the whole conversation.

### Option 4: Full post replacement

**Best when:** the post is rotted or broken below grade, or it has moved so far that resetting won't hold.

This is the permanent repair. The old footing gets dug out or broken up, the damaged post is removed, and a new pressure-treated or cedar post goes in — set below the frost line in fresh concrete, with a drainage layer at the bottom and concrete crowned and sloped away from the wood. Panels and rails come down and go back up around it.

Two details matter: concrete needs 24 to 48 hours to cure before panels go back on, and if the old hole showed rot or movement, dig fresh. Soil around an old footing has already been loosened by years of movement, and the base of a hole where a post rotted in place is no place for a new post.

### Option 5: Rebuild the fence run

**Best when:** three or more consecutive posts are failing, or the fence is 20-plus years old.

This is the one homeowners resist, and we understand why. But there's a real pattern: someone replaces one post, then another six months later, then another. By the time they've paid for three or four post replacements plus the panel repairs each one forces, they've spent most of what a new section would have cost — and they still have a 20-year-old fence. Replacing a section once beats nursing it annually.

## What Does It Cost to Fix a Leaning Fence Post in Oakland County in 2026?

Real numbers, so you can budget instead of guess.

**Fence post replacement runs $150 to $600 per post.** The spread comes from depth, soil type (clay is more work than sand), and access — a post behind a shed or a pool is harder to get at than one on an open lot line.

**A typical full fence repair job runs $300 to $1,200**, with a national average around $650. That covers things like resetting a leaning post and replacing a handful of pickets.

**Materials, if you're pricing it out yourself:** pressure-treated pickets run about $1.50 to $4 per linear foot, cedar pickets about $3 to $7, individual replacement boards $2 to $4 each compared to $15 to $25 per linear foot for a full panel. Bagged concrete at 2026 retail is roughly $5.00 for a 40-pound bag, $6.25 for 60 pounds, and $7.50 for 80 pounds — and a gravel drainage layer at the bottom of each hole costs well under a dollar per post.

**Gate repairs run $150 to $400** for a single wood gate and $400 to $800 for a double gate or one with decorative hardware. Gate posts are more expensive than line posts because they need to be set deeper and often replaced entirely.

### The cost of waiting

A leaning post fixed in the fall is a $150 to $350 reset. The same post two winters later is usually a $400 to $600 replacement — because rot finishes what the frost started, and the panels around it go with it. There's also wind risk: a leaning post isn't fully carrying its share of a solid 6-foot sail, and every storm between now and spring is a chance for the fence to come down in a section instead of a post.

**If you have storm damage, document it with photos before anyone touches the fence.** Homeowner's insurance often covers storm-related fence damage, and the photos are what you'll need.

## How Posts Get Set So They Don't Lean Again

Repair is half the job. The other half is making sure the replacement lasts longer than the original. Here's the standard we hold to, and the questions worth asking anyone who sets a post in your yard.

**Depth.** Below the frost line, full stop. In Michigan that means the bottom of the footing at 42 inches minimum, and 48 inches is common practice for 6-foot privacy fences. If a quote doesn't specify depth, ask.

**Footing shape.** Belling or tapering the bottom of the concrete footing widens the base and helps resist the upward pull when frost acts on the upper part of the plug. It's a small detail that makes a real difference.

**Hole diameter and concrete coverage.** Industry practice is several inches of concrete on all sides of the post inside the hole, sized to the post. A 4x4 dropped into a hole barely wider than the post has almost no lateral resistance. Larger posts need wider holes.

**Drainage at the base.** A gravel layer at the bottom of the hole gives water somewhere to go instead of sitting against the end grain.

**Concrete detail.** Crowned slightly above grade and sloped away from the post, so water sheds rather than pooling at the ground line. Not a mound that traps water — a crown that moves it.

**Rot barrier at grade.** A rot protection sleeve that covers the top two inches above grade down through the top six inches of burial seals the exact band where decay starts. It's inexpensive, and on a new post it's the best money you'll spend on the install.

**Gate and corner posts set deeper.** Four to six inches deeper than line posts at minimum; more on driveway gates. Corner posts take tension from two directions and deserve reinforcement.

**MISS DIG 811 before any digging.** Michigan law requires calling 811 at least three full business days before you break ground — including fence post work. It's free. Skipping it risks hitting a gas, electric, water, or telecom line, and that's a bad day for everybody.

## DIY or Call a Pro? An Honest Breakdown

We're a fence company, so take this with the appropriate grain of salt — but we'd rather tell you the truth than sell you a job you don't need.

**You can probably handle it yourself if:** the post is plumb-sound and only slightly loose, a no-dig steel anchor spike is the right call, you're replacing a handful of boards, or you're clearing debris and improving drainage around the base.

**Call a professional if:**

- The post needs to be reset below the frost line. That means excavating 42 to 48 inches in Michigan clay and handling a concrete footing — not a weekend project.
- The post is rotted below grade and has to come out. Removing old footings is the part that defeats most DIY attempts.
- It's a **gate post**. Gate posts carry dynamic load and need to be deeper and better footed than line posts. This is the repair most likely to fail if it's underdone.
- You're near a property line, utilities, or an easement and aren't sure where the line is.
- The fence is old and multiple posts are going. Individual fixes on a failing fence are a treadmill.

The reason it matters: a bad post repair doesn't fail immediately. It fails next March, when the ground thaws.

## Why Fall Is the Right Time to Fix a Michigan Fence

Here's the practical timing piece most guides skip.

In Southeast Michigan, September and October give you workable ground and enough warm weather for concrete to cure properly before the first hard freeze. Set a post now and its footing is locked in before the freeze-thaw cycle starts prying at it. Wait until November or December and you're either digging frozen clay or paying somebody to.

There's a second reason. Every winter, freeze-thaw working on an already-compromised footing makes it worse. Fixing a leaning post in the fall stops that cycle from compounding. Fixing it in the spring means letting it take one more Michigan winter first.

And a third: this is the season you notice. You're out closing up the patio and cleaning gutters, and the fence line is right there in front of you.

## Protecting What Matters: Keeping the Next Post Standing

Repairs are half the equation. These are the habits that extend post life in Michigan, and none of them cost much:

- **Grade the soil away from the post base.** Water that runs away from the post doesn't sit against the wood.
- **Redirect downspouts.** If a downspout discharges within a few feet of the fence line, extend it. This is one of the most common and most fixable causes of rot we find.
- **Don't pile snow against the fence.** Plowed or shoveled snow banks hold moisture against the base and set up freeze-thaw right where the post is weakest.
- **Skip the road salt near the fence line.** It degrades both wood and concrete.
- **Trim vegetation back.** Shrubs and vines against the fence trap moisture and hide early rot until it's advanced.
- **Stay off the base with the trimmer.** A string trimmer strips the treated shell at the worst possible elevation. Use a string guard, mulch, or hand-trim.
- **Walk the fence line twice a year** — once in spring after the thaw, once in fall before freeze-up. The push and probe tests take five minutes and catch problems while they're cheap.
- **Keep up with stain and seal.** A properly finished fence sheds water instead of drinking it. See our guide to staining and sealing in Michigan for the schedule.

A fence that's built to last in Michigan isn't an accident. It's a post set deep enough, footed well enough, drained properly, and inspected often enough — and it's why privacy you can see is worth doing right the first time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is my fence post leaning?

Almost always one of three causes. A footing set above Michigan's 42-inch frost line gets lifted by frost heave, and heaved footings can move one to four inches a season without settling back. Rot at the ground contact line — the band right at and just below the soil surface — hollows out a post below grade while the wood above still looks fine. Or poor drainage kept the soil around the base saturated, which accelerates both. In many yards, all three are working together.

### How deep should fence posts be set in Michigan?

Michigan Residential Code R403.1.4 requires exterior footings and foundation systems to extend 42 inches below actual grade, and Michigan frost depth runs 42 to 48 inches depending on region. Best practice is to set the bottom of the footing at least 6 inches below the published frost line. For a 6-foot privacy fence, many Michigan contractors use an 8-foot post with the bottom 48 inches in the ground. The common rule of thumb that one-third of the post should be buried works out to about 36 inches for a 6-foot fence — and that is not deep enough in Michigan clay.

### Can I fix a leaning fence post without replacing it?

Yes, if the post itself is sound. A post that's still solid when you probe it at ground level can usually be reset in place — dig out the footing, pull it plumb, and re-set it in fresh concrete below the frost line. If rot is confined to the very bottom, a sister post set alongside it and fastened with structural hardware can carry the load. If the wood is rotted or crumbling below grade, no amount of straightening will hold, and the post needs to be replaced.

### How much does it cost to fix or replace a fence post in Oakland County?

Fence post replacement typically runs $150 to $600 per post, depending on depth, soil type, and access. A typical full repair — resetting a leaning post plus replacing a handful of pickets — runs $300 to $1,200. Gate repairs run $150 to $400 for a single wood gate. Materials are inexpensive by comparison: pressure-treated pickets run $1.50 to $4 per linear foot, individual boards $2 to $4 each, and a bag of concrete is roughly $5 to $7.50. Labor on footing excavation is what drives the cost.

### Is a fence post repair kit (no-dig spike) a permanent fix?

No — it's a stabilizer, not a footing. A steel H-channel spike driven beside the post and screwed to sound wood shares load and stops the wobble quickly and cheaply, which is genuinely useful on an early-stage lean. But it doesn't get below the 42-inch frost line in most installations, it doesn't stop rot, and it won't hold a post whose base has failed. Used on a rotted post as a permanent solution, it buys a season at most.

### How do I know if my fence post is rotted below ground?

Probe it. Press a screwdriver or pocketknife into the wood at ground level and a few inches below. Sound treated wood resists; rotted wood lets the tool sink in or crumbles rather than splinters. Look for dark staining or gray discoloration at the base, mushrooms or white fungal threads near the soil line, and concrete collars sitting above grade. A post can look perfect above ground and be hollow just a few inches down, because that ground contact line is where moisture cycles most and decay accelerates fastest.

### Does a leaning fence post need to be fixed before winter?

Yes, and fall is the best window in Michigan. The ground is still workable, concrete cures before the first hard freeze, and the footing gets locked in before freeze-thaw starts prying at it. Every winter you wait, freeze-thaw works on an already-compromised footing and rot finishes what the frost started. A post that's a $150 to $350 reset this fall is often a $400 to $600 replacement after another Michigan winter — plus the panel damage that comes with it.

### How do I keep new fence posts from leaning again?

Set the footing below the frost line — 42 inches minimum in Michigan, 48 inches commonly specified for a 6-foot privacy fence — and bell the bottom of the concrete so it resists uplift. Add a gravel drainage layer at the base, crown the concrete and slope it away from the post, and use a rot barrier sleeve at grade covering the top two inches above ground through the top six inches of burial. Set gate and corner posts 4 to 6 inches deeper than line posts, redirect downspouts away from the fence line, keep snow and salt off the base, and walk the line with a push-and-probe check every spring and fall.
