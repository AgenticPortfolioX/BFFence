# The Dog-Proof Fence: What Actually Keeps a Dog in the Yard in Oakland County, Michigan
*Published: October 17, 2026 | By BFFence*

---

The gap under your fence isn't there yet. It will be by March.

That is the part of dog containment nobody warns you about in Michigan. You walk the perimeter in October, check the gate, everything is tight to the ground — and then the ground does what Michigan ground does. It freezes, heaves, and settles, and by the first thaw there's a four-inch opening under a fence that was sealed six months earlier. For a dog that has spent all winter staring at that line, four inches is an invitation.

We're BF Fence, an Oakland County fence company started by two best friends, Justin and Chris. We build wooden fences in Auburn Hills, Rochester Hills, Troy, Clarkston, Lake Orion, Novi, and everywhere between, and a lot of that work is really pet containment with a privacy fence wrapped around it. **Protecting what matters** stops being a slogan when the thing you're protecting has four legs and a scent drive.

So here's what genuinely keeps a dog in a yard — accounting for a 90-pound dog with a running start, a 12-pound dog that fits through a two-inch gap, a plow truck pushing snow against your back line, and a code that caps your fence at six feet either way.

## Three Ways Out, and Why the Fix Is Different for Each

Most homeowners diagnose their dog wrong. They see an escape and conclude "the fence is too short," then spend money on height when the real failure was a gate that didn't latch. Dogs leave yards three ways, and each one has its own fix. Get the diagnosis right before you spend a dollar.

| Failure mode | What it looks like | What actually fixes it |
|---|---|---|
| **Over the top** | Clears the fence with a running start, or climbs using rails, links, or nearby objects | Height matched to the dog, a climb-resistant surface with no toe holds, and removal of launch points like snow banks and woodpiles |
| **Under the bottom** | Dug tunnel, pushed-out bottom board, or a seasonal gap from frost heave | A below-grade barrier, a poured concrete mow strip, or a bottom rail reset to grade |
| **Through gaps and gates** | Squeezed between pickets, slipped under a gate, popped a latch, or pushed a sagging gate open | Picket spacing of 2 inches or less, a rigid frame, and self-closing, dog-rated latch hardware |

Read your own dog against that table honestly. A beagle that tunnels won't be stopped by an extra foot of height. A border collie that clears four feet won't be stopped by a dig guard. A husky that works the latch every day is telling you about hardware, not height.

If you don't know which one you have, the answer is visible at the fence line: disturbed soil and a depression along the bottom means digging, scratched boards and fur on the top rail means jumping, and a gate that swings a half inch low on the latch side means sag. Ten minutes on the perimeter beats a month of guessing.

## Height: What Actually Stops a Jumper

Height is the first thing people ask about and the last thing that should be decided alone.

| Dog profile | Practical fence height | What to know |
|---|---|---|
| Toy and small breeds, calm to moderate energy | 4 ft | Small fugitives are usually gap escapees, not jumpers — picket spacing matters more than height |
| Small breeds with real athleticism (Jack Russell, papillon, terrier mixes) | 5–6 ft | Documented clears of 4–5 ft are common; a 4 ft fence is decorative for these dogs |
| Medium breeds, average drive (most Labs, goldens, herding mixes) | 6 ft | The practical standard, and the residential cap in most Oakland County neighborhoods |
| Large, high-drive breeds (border collie, Belgian Malinois, kelpie, German shepherd, vizsla, weimaraner) | 6 ft solid plus a climb deterrent, or 8 ft where allowed | A determined dog can clear 6 ft from a running start; a solid panel with no toe holds is what makes 6 ft work |

Two things matter more than the number on the tape.

**A solid panel beats an open one at the same height.** A dog that can see through a fence can measure the jump. A dog facing a solid wall of cedar has no landing reference, no visible target, and no toe hold on a flat board. We've watched dogs clear a five-foot chain link run without touching it and refuse to challenge a six-foot solid privacy fence in the same yard.

**Height without a climb deterrent is a suggestion.** Dogs climb chain link like a ladder, and they climb horizontal-rail designs by finding the rail with their back feet. Vertical boards with no gaps, or a smooth vinyl panel, remove the ladder. Where a dog has already shown climbing behavior, a fence-topper is the honest answer — not another six inches of post.

And then there's the ceiling nobody mentions. Most Oakland County residential districts cap side and rear fences at six feet. Rochester Hills, for example, allows six feet along side and rear lot lines, holding front and side-street setbacks to three feet, with some districts reading closer to four in front. For most homeowners, "build it taller" isn't an option — which is why footing depth, gate hardware, and behavior matter more here than in a state with no height cap. If your dog can clear six feet, the fix isn't eight feet of lumber. It's a design that gives the dog nothing to see, nothing to grip, and nowhere to land.

## The Ground Problem: Digging, and What Actually Stops It

Digging is the most common escape method here, and the most misunderstood. People pour gravel along the fence and call it done. Two weeks later the dog has dug through the gravel.

Start with why the dog digs, because that decides whether hardware alone will hold. Veterinary behavior guidance is consistent on the drivers: boredom, prey drive following a scent along the far side, thermoregulation (cool soil in summer), caching, and anxiety. A fence removes the *consequence* of digging. It does not remove the *cause*. If your dog is digging because a woodchuck lives under the shed next door, no amount of concrete makes the yard more interesting than the woodchuck.

Hardware buys you time to work on the behavior. Three approaches, in ascending order of cost and effectiveness:

**The buried L-footer.** Wire mesh attached to the fence bottom and run outward horizontally 12 to 18 inches, just below the soil. A dog digging down hits wire, not open earth. It's the standard AKC and trainer recommendation, and it works — as long as the wire is anchored, dressed back over with soil, and the dog can't see daylight under the line. Modest cost, minimal disruption, no change to how the fence looks.

**The vertical barrier.** Galvanized steel dig barriers driven 12 to 15 inches into the ground along the fence line, spaced tightly enough that a dog can't nose between the spikes. Faster to retrofit than an L-footer and better on slopes. It won't stop a dog that digs two feet back from the fence, so placement matters.

**The poured mow strip.** A continuous concrete strip, typically 6 to 8 inches wide, with the fence set into or directly behind it. This is the permanent answer: it eliminates the soil-to-fence seam entirely, so there's no gap to dig and no gap for frost to open. It's also the most expensive option per foot and genuinely hard to undo, which makes it a natural fit when the excavation is already happening for a new fence.

What doesn't work: landscape blocks, rocks a determined dog can nudge aside, or boards buried a few inches deep. And for small dogs, the fence bottom should be flush to grade or slightly below — not floating two inches up "for drainage." A two-inch gap is a doorway for anything under about 15 pounds.

## The Winter Problem: Why Your Fence Opens Up in Michigan

Now the part that specifically costs Michigan dog owners their dogs. It isn't a marketing angle. It's physics, and it happens in almost every yard in this county.

Fence posts are set below the frost line — 42 inches below grade, the statewide minimum exterior footing depth under the Michigan Residential Code. That depth keeps the footing itself from being lifted. But the *fence* doesn't sit 42 inches down. The bottom board, bottom rail, and gate all live in the top six inches of soil, and that layer moves every winter. Soil expands as it freezes, lifts, then settles unevenly as it thaws from the surface down. On a fence line, the visible result is a bottom board that sat flush in October and hangs two to four inches off the ground in March.

That gap is small enough that most people never notice it. It's also exactly the size a dog needs.

Three ways to close it before it opens:

**Set the bottom board lower and overlap it.** A bottom board lapping an inch or two below grade, rather than resting on top of it, keeps the visual barrier intact through a normal heave cycle. It costs nothing extra if it's decided before the posts go in.

**Use a mow strip or a fixed bottom barrier.** Concrete or steel decouples the fence bottom from the soil. The ground can move; the barrier doesn't. This is what we recommend for any yard with a known digger or a dog that has already escaped once.

**Plan a March perimeter check.** Walk the line before the dog does. Push down on the bottom board at each post, look for daylight, and check the gate for square. Ten minutes in March prevents the April call.

Then there's snow. A plow piles it against the fence, the pile freezes into a ramp, and a dog that has never jumped in its life suddenly has a launch pad that cuts effective fence height by a foot or more. That sits behind a huge share of "he never did that before" escapes in January and February. If snow gets pushed against a particular run, that run needs more height, a solid panel with no grip, or a shovel plan that keeps the pile off the line.

## Gaps, Pickets, and the Two-Inch Rule

If you have a small dog, your containment problem may not be height at all. It's the gap between boards.

A standard dog-ear privacy fence is built with small gaps between pickets so the wood can move with the seasons. That's correct construction. But a gap wide enough for a dog's skull is wide enough for the dog, and one wide enough for a jaw is a bite risk in a fence-reactive yard. Guidance from the AKC and from trainers is blunt: small dogs squeeze through spacing that looks tight to a human eye, and dogs wedge their heads in gaps too small to pass but large enough to catch.

The working rule is **two inches or less**, measured at the widest point. Three ways to get there:

- **Board-on-board.** Alternating boards on both sides of the rails, overlapping so there's no straight-through sightline and no gap wider than the board thickness. The tightest standard wood design, and what we recommend for small dogs and privacy.
- **Puppy pickets.** A picket fence with the lower section built at reduced spacing — typically half the normal gap for the bottom two feet. Keeps the look, closes the route.
- **Shadowbox.** Alternating boards with a deliberate gap each side. Good for airflow and looks, but not the tightest containment: the offset creates a visual gap even when the pass-through is narrow. Fine for a calm large dog, wrong for a determined 12-pounder.

Whatever you choose, walk the finished fence with a tape at dog height, not eye height. The gap that matters is the one in the bottom two feet, where the nose and shoulders actually are.

## Gates: Where Containment Actually Fails

If we had to bet where a containment failure will happen, we'd bet on the gate nine times out of ten. Not the fence line. The gate.

A gate is the only part of the fence designed to move, so it's the only part with hinges, a latch, and a swing arc. It puts more load per post than any other section, and it's the part humans touch daily — which means it gets propped, dragged, and left unlatched.

Four things separate a gate that holds a dog from one that doesn't:

**Self-closing hinges.** A spring hinge that pulls the gate shut behind you removes the most common human error in dog containment. For a dog that pushes, self-closing hinges plus a positive latch are the difference between containment and a coin flip.

**Latches rated for the dog, not the hardware aisle.** A standard thumb latch is fine for a calm retriever and useless for a husky that has learned to nose it. For latch-workers, use a two-point latch, a locking pin, or hardware mounted above muzzle height.

**A rigid frame that resists racking.** Gate sag is the slow failure: the gate drops a half inch, the latch stops engaging cleanly, the bottom corner drags, and eventually there's a gap on the latch side. A properly braced frame holds its shape. Four boards and two hinges do not.

**The right width.** Wider gates rack more and sag faster. A 4-foot walk gate outlasts a 10-foot opening built as one leaf. For vehicle access, use a double gate with a drop rod, not one enormous panel.

One more gate note: a gate is easier to clear than a fence. A six-foot fence with a four-foot gate in it is a four-foot fence with extra steps. Gates should match the fence height, and the latch should sit where the dog can't reach it from the hinge side.

## Material by Dog, Not by Trend

Material usually gets chosen on looks and budget, which is fine — but if containment is the goal, the dog should get a vote.

| Material | Dog-specific strength | Dog-specific weakness | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Western red cedar** | Naturally rot-resistant, dense enough to resist chew damage, solid visual barrier | Higher cost; can splinter at chewed edges over time | Chewers, reactive dogs, yards where privacy and longevity both matter |
| **Pressure-treated pine** | Lowest-cost solid privacy fence; strong enough for large dogs | Softer wood; chew damage likelier; copper azole preservative makes ingestion a real concern | Budget projects with calm dogs and no chew history |
| **Vinyl** | Smooth and slippery, so hard to climb; rot-proof, no splinters | Brittle in deep cold and can crack on impact; chew damage isn't repairable | Climbers, low-maintenance households, non-chewers |
| **Chain link** | Strong, inexpensive, see-through enough to supervise | Climbable like a ladder; cut risk from broken wire ends; zero privacy; visually stimulating | Large calm dogs on a budget, or a barrier inside a barrier |
| **Ornamental aluminum** | Very strong, long-lived, clean sightlines | Bar gaps pass small dogs and wedge heads; see-through design amplifies reactivity | Calm large dogs, decorative runs |
| **Composite** | Dimensionally stable, no rot, holds hardware well | Highest cost; still chewable; not repairable in place | Long-horizon installs where maintenance decides it |

**Chewing is a training and barrier problem first.** A dog that chews is usually bored, teething, or working one spot where something on the far side is interesting. Blocking that sightline and adding a barrier — a rail, a wire panel, a planting bed — solves most of it. Material is the second line of defense.

**On treated lumber:** modern pressure-treated wood uses copper azole or ACQ preservatives rather than the old arsenic-based CCA treatment. It's still not something you want a dog eating, and veterinary guidance treats ingestion as a genuine concern — GI upset at minimum, worse with repeated exposure. If your dog chews, cedar is the better substrate.

## Invisible Fence vs. a Real Fence: The Honest Version

We build physical fences for a living, so weigh our opinion accordingly. But the buried-wire question comes up on nearly every dog estimate, and the honest answer is more nuanced than either side admits.

| | Buried wire system | Physical fence |
|---|---|---|
| **How it contains** | A receiver collar corrects the dog near a buried boundary line | A barrier the dog cannot pass |
| **Works on** | Trained dogs with moderate drive and a recall foundation | Any dog, trained or not |
| **Fails on** | High-prey-drive dogs, dogs that bolt through the correction, untrained dogs, deaf dogs | Almost nothing, if height and footing are right |
| **Stops animals coming in** | No | Yes |
| **Creates privacy** | No | Yes |
| **HOA and view impact** | Minimal — often the only option where fencing is prohibited | Subject to height and setback rules |
| **Michigan winter behavior** | Wire sits in the frost-active layer; heave and aeration break it, and repairs commonly run $200–$300 per incident | Frost heave affects the bottom gap, not the barrier's function |
| **Installed cost** | Roughly half a physical perimeter | Higher upfront, no recurring repair cycle |
| **Maintenance** | Batteries, wire breaks, collar fit as the coat changes | Stain or seal on wood, hardware checks, seasonal walk |

Where the wire genuinely wins: a view to protect, an HOA that prohibits fencing, a large property where a full perimeter is prohibitive, and a dog that is already trained, responsive, and low-to-moderate drive. In those cases it's reasonable, and we say so.

Where it fails: a dog with high prey drive who will trade a correction for a squirrel, a dog with no training foundation, or a dog that has learned the boundary is optional when the collar is off. And any dog that needs protection *from* something, because buried wire does nothing to stop a coyote, a loose dog, or a person walking into your yard. Published comparisons of the category typically land around 70 percent real-world effectiveness — a very different number from a physical fence's near-certainty.

Our honest recommendation: if the dog's safety is the priority and the yard can take a fence, build the fence. If a fence is genuinely impossible, a wire system with real training beats an uncontained yard — but call it what it is, a training tool with a barrier's marketing.

## Reactive Dogs, and Why a Solid Fence Calms Them

A see-through fence gives a reactive dog a continuous stream of triggers — the neighbor's dog, kids on bikes, the mail truck — at nose height, with no escape route. The result is fence-running, barrier barking, and fence fighting, where two dogs on opposite sides of chain link work into a frenzy neither can resolve. Trainers and veterinarians consistently recommend solid visual barriers for reactive dogs, because removing the trigger beats correcting the reaction.

**Privacy you can see** is how we put it. A six-foot solid cedar run gives your dog a quiet yard and gives you a backyard that isn't a stage. For a reactive dog, that's the difference between a dog that can be outside alone and one that can't.

## Coyotes, Raccoons, and the Wildlife Interface

October 15 is a date worth knowing if you have a small dog in Oakland County. That's the start of Michigan's open coyote season, running through March 1, with a management season from March 2 through October 14. The dates matter less than the underlying fact: coyotes live here, they move through residential neighborhoods, and Oakland County residents have reported increasingly bold daytime behavior in recent years.

This isn't a crisis; encounters are rare, and coyotes avoid people. But a fence is the part of the equation you control, and it works while you're asleep.

- **Six feet of solid fence.** Coyotes jump and dig well. A four-foot decorative fence is not a barrier; a six-foot solid run with the bottom closed is.
- **Close the bottom.** No gap, no entry — another argument for the mow strip.
- **No food, no attraction.** Bowls left out overnight, fallen fruit, unsecured garbage, birdseed on the ground.
- **Supervise small dogs at dawn and dusk.** No fence replaces a human watching a 10-pound dog at 6 a.m.
- **Know the law.** Under Michigan's Wildlife Conservation Order, a pet owner or authorized agent may kill a coyote without a written permit if the pet is being attacked. That's a last resort, not a strategy. Exclusion is the strategy.

## Local Rules, and What They Mean for a Dog Fence

Fence height is a municipal question, not a county one, and the rules vary enough across Oakland County that it's worth checking before you buy lumber.

Rochester Hills is a useful example because it's typical of the county's denser residential districts: six feet along side and rear lot lines, with front and side-street setbacks held to three feet and some districts reading closer to four. That's the same ceiling most homeowners here run into. It means the six-foot solid privacy fence is the standard dog fence in Oakland County — not because six feet is perfect, but because six feet is what the code allows and, for most dogs, what works.

Auburn Hills, where we're based, runs fence permitting through Community Development and publishes its zoning ordinance and permit requirements. Most communities here require some form of fence permit or zoning review at six feet, with timelines from a few days to a couple of weeks.

Two rules that apply everywhere. **MISS DIG 811 requires three full business days' notice before digging** — free, and mandatory, because that's how homeowners find gas lines with a post-hole digger. And **your property line is yours to confirm**: a fence built two feet onto a neighbor's lot is a dispute waiting to happen. If there's no recent survey, get one. It's cheap compared to moving a finished fence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will a 4-foot fence keep my dog in?

Only for a calm small or medium dog. Terriers, herding breeds, and any dog with real jumping ability will clear it — Jack Russell terriers and papillons have been documented clearing four to five feet. A four-foot fence is containment only for a dog that isn't trying.

### What is the best fence for a dog that digs?

A solid privacy fence with the bottom closed: a buried L-footer running 12 to 18 inches outward below grade, a steel dig barrier driven 12 to 15 inches down, or a poured concrete mow strip. Height is irrelevant to a digger — closing the ground line is the whole fix.

### Can a dog jump a 6-foot fence?

Some can. Border collies, Belgian Malinois, Australian kelpies, German shepherds, vizslas, and weimaraners can clear six feet from a running start, especially with a snow bank or fence corner to help. For those dogs, a solid panel with no toe holds plus a climb deterrent beats adding height.

### How tall should a fence be for a large dog?

Six feet is the practical standard for most large breeds, and it's also the residential height cap in most Oakland County communities. If your dog has cleared six feet, the answer is design — solid panels, closed bottom, secure gate — not eight-foot posts the code may not allow.

### Is an invisible fence as good as a physical fence?

No, though it isn't useless. Buried wire works for trained dogs with moderate drive and is often the only option where an HOA prohibits fencing. It doesn't stop other animals coming in, provides no privacy, and typically achieves far less reliable containment than a physical barrier.

### Why is there a gap under my fence in spring?

Frost heave. The top layer of Michigan soil freezes, expands, lifts, and settles unevenly through the thaw, and the fence bottom sits in that active layer. A board flush to grade in October can hang two to four inches up by March. A mow strip or a board lapped below grade prevents it.

### Do I need a permit for a 6-foot fence in Oakland County?

Usually. Most communities here require a fence permit or zoning review at six feet, and requirements vary by city. Plan on a few days to two weeks for review, and remember MISS DIG 811 requires three full business days' notice before digging.

### What is the cheapest dog-proof fence?

Chain link with a buried bottom barrier is the least expensive option that holds most dogs, though it offers no privacy and is climbable. Pressure-treated pine privacy fencing at $25 to $35 per linear foot installed is the cheapest solid barrier.

### What fence material is safest for a dog that chews?

Cedar. It's naturally rot-resistant, denser than treated pine, and free of the copper azole or ACQ preservatives used in pressure-treated lumber. No fence is chew-proof, so blocking the sightline at the chew spot and redirecting the behavior matters as much as the material.

### How do I keep coyotes away from my dog in Michigan?

A six-foot solid fence with a closed bottom, no food or garbage left out, and supervision for small dogs at dawn and dusk. Michigan's coyote season runs October 15 through March 1, with a management season March 2 through October 14, but exclusion — fencing — is the only measure that works every night.

## The Bottom Line

No fence is dog-proof. Any contractor who says otherwise is selling certainty he can't deliver. What a good fence does is remove the three things a dog needs to get out: a height it can clear, a gap it can exploit, and a gate it can work.

In Oakland County that means six feet of solid fence — because that's the code and, for most dogs, the effective height — on posts set below the 42-inch frost line, with the bottom closed against digging and against the frost heave that opens a gap every March, and a gate with self-closing hinges and a latch your dog can't nose open. Add a March perimeter walk and a shovel plan, and you've done what can be done.

That's what **Built to last** means in practice. Not a taller fence — a fence designed around the animal that's going to test it.
