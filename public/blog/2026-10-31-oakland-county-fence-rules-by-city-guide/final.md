# Fence Rules by City in Oakland County: What Your City Actually Requires Before You Build
*Published: October 31, 2026 | By BF Fence*

Here is the short answer, and it holds for almost every residential lot in Oakland County: **six feet is the ceiling along your side and rear lot lines.** Every city we work in lands on that number. What changes from city to city is everything else — where your fence has to stop in the front yard, how tall it can be on a corner lot, what a permit costs, whether an easement runs through your fence line, and whether your subdivision association has a say on top of the city.

And yes, you need a permit in all six jurisdictions below. A six-foot wood fence is exempt from a *state building* permit under the 2015 Michigan Residential Code, but that exemption has nothing to do with your city's zoning permit. That single distinction is where most homeowners get sideways with their building department, and it is why a fence a neighbor built without paperwork is not proof that you can.

Treat this as a working guide, not legal advice — ordinances get amended. Every rule below is quoted from the city's own code or permit packet, with the section number.

## Why the Rules Differ at All: State Code vs. City Code

Michigan does not set a statewide height limit for private residential fences. Height, placement, materials and permitting are local zoning matters, which is why Auburn Hills and Novi can hold identical lots to different front-yard rules.

The state weighs in on one thing: when a fence becomes a *building* code structure. Under the 2015 Michigan Residential Code, **§R105.2** exempts fences under seven feet from a building permit. At seven feet and above, the fence falls under the Michigan Building Code and needs a building permit with plan review from both the building and planning departments — which is exactly what Troy prints on its own fence application.

**The state building-code exemption does not exempt you from the city's land-use permit** — the city is not checking the structure of a six-foot fence, it is checking that the fence is where zoning allows it to be. Different question, different permit, different office.

Frost depth rides alongside all of it. Michigan Residential Code **§R403.1.4** puts footings at 42 inches below grade, measured from the lowest adjacent grade — which is why a fence built correctly here has posts deeper than most homeowners expect, and why a fence on a slope needs deeper posts on the downhill face, a detail we walked through in our [Rochester Hills sloped-lot build](/blog/2026-10-28-rochester-hills-sloped-lot-cedar-fence-grade-change).

## Auburn Hills: The Berm Trap

Auburn Hills handles fences in Article XIX of the zoning ordinance, and it is one of the cleaner sets of rules in the county — until the height measurement.

**§1904(1)** governs the front yard: opaque fences, hedges, berms or walls are capped at **two and a half feet**; non-opaque fences are allowed up to **four feet** within a required front yard.

**§1904(2)** governs side and rear: fences, hedges, berms or walls **not more than six feet**, opaque or non-opaque, may be built in a residential district within a required rear or side yard.

Then **§1904(4)** does something most cities leave vague. It defines height as the vertical distance from **average surrounding grade** — *not including a berm* — to the highest point of the fence. And it adds the sentence that catches people: a berm may be used in combination with a fence, provided **the highest point of the combination** does not exceed the permitted height.

In plain English: you cannot build up the grade three feet, stack a six-foot fence on top, and call it six feet — the berm and the fence are measured together. We see this on rear lines backing onto drainage swales, where a homeowner berms to level the yard and then wants privacy on the new grade. Combined, it still has to land at six feet.

Corner lots get their own rule. **§1902** prohibits any fence, wall, shrubbery, sign or other obstruction to vision **above 30 inches** from established street grade inside the triangular area formed at a street intersection, measured **25 feet** along each right-of-way line.

**Permits:** fence work runs through the city's building/zoning permit process, now handled through the BS&A online portal. Bring a scaled plot plan and call for the current fee.

## Rochester Hills: Six Feet, Three Feet, and Nothing Obscuring In Between

Rochester Hills is the most prescriptive of the six, and its three numbers are easy to remember.

**§138-10.107.C.2:** fences along side and rear lot lines are a **maximum of six feet**, and may not extend closer to the front lot line than **the front of the dwelling or the minimum front setback, whichever is less**. Then the sentence that governs more fences than any other in this article: *"In no instance shall an obscuring fence over three feet high be placed between the front of a residence and minimum front setback line."*

**§138-10.107.C.3:** fences **not exceeding three feet** are permitted within a required front yard setback or side-street yard setback, provided corner clearance under §138-5.204 is met.

So: six feet along the sides and back, three feet in front of the house, and nothing solid taller than three feet between your porch and the setback line. If you have been told "four feet in the front yard in Rochester Hills," check before ordering material — third-party permit sites publish at least two different values for this, and the ordinance text says three.

Materials are equally specific. **§138-10.107.C.4** allows wood, metal, brick, masonry, or another solid all-weather-resistant material designed for permanent fencing or screening. Fabric-type materials are prohibited. So are **wire fences, barbed or razor wire, spikes, nails, and any other sharp pointed instruments** — which rules out the decorative spear-top steel panels that look great in a catalog and fail an inspection here.

Electric fences are prohibited with a narrow exception: **§138-10.107.C.5** lets the building department approve an electrified fence for retaining animals only if there is sufficient proof it will not harm people or animals, the power source is a listed electric fence controller, and warning signs are conspicuously posted. If you have a dog that tests boundaries, that exception is worth knowing — and worth pairing with our [dog-proof fence guide](/blog/2026-10-17-dog-proof-fence-oakland-county-michigan) before assuming an invisible fence is the answer.

Non-residential property gets a different ceiling: eight feet generally, up to **ten feet** for ornamental security fencing at Planning Commission discretion. Standard chain link is prohibited unless it will not be visible from an abutting roadway, and then it must be vinyl-coated.

**Corner clearance** lives in §138-5.204: no obstruction to vision above **30 inches** within the triangle formed at intersecting street right-of-way lines, measured **25 feet** along each line. Rochester Hills adds a second triangle most people never hear about — **15 feet** where a nonresidential driveway meets a right-of-way, same 30-inch cap. Canopy trees are allowed in the triangle if the first branch is at least eight feet above grade.

## City of Rochester: The Stricter Neighbor

Rochester is a separate city from Rochester Hills, and homeowners on the boundary routinely assume the rules match. They do not.

Rochester's Building Department publishes a short, hard list: a **zoning permit is required**; fences must be in the **rear or side yards**; the **maximum height is six feet**; fences may be solid or chain link, but **no pointed barbs or race wire**; and the **good side must face the neighbor's property**. No electrical fences. And the line that puts the risk where it belongs: **identifying your property lines is the property owner's responsibility.**

If your lot straddles the Rochester/Rochester Hills line, build to the stricter rule across the whole run — it costs nothing and eliminates the argument.

## Troy: The 30-Inch Line and the Grade Holds

Troy's ordinance is Chapter 83, revised in pieces through 2003, and it uses language most modern codes have dropped — language that is more restrictive than it first appears.

**§83-2(A):** all residential fences separating properties must be of an **ornamental type** — metal, wood or masonry — using only new or inspector-approved material. No residential fence **more than six feet** above existing grade. And no fence **more than 30 inches** above existing grade **in that portion of the property in front of the front building setback line.**

Thirty inches is not a typo. A three-foot decorative fence in a Troy front yard sits above the line unless it is placed behind the front building setback. Plenty of homeowners install one and never hear about it; that does not make it compliant, and a complaint-driven inspection does not care that the neighbors have one too.

Corner lots get a specific allowance: where a double front setback is required and the lot has a common rear yard relationship with a lot in the same block, a **non-obscuring fence no greater than 48 inches** may be installed along the common street line, from the front building setback line to the rear property line. That is a corridor-specific rule, not a general four-foot permission.

Height maximums can be waived by the **Building Board of Appeals**, the same body that can approve fences over six feet around school grounds, playgrounds, tennis courts and public pools.

No electrically charged fence is allowed anywhere in the city, and no barbed wire in residential districts except to block an attractive nuisance at six feet or more above ground. Non-residential fencing has its own rules — no fence between the building and a frontage street unless it encloses a utility, and a decorative masonry obscuring wall of not less than six feet where non-residential abuts residential, decorative side facing the homes.

Permitting has two gates that stop projects cold. **§83-5:** a permit is required before any fence construction; **no permit issues before final lot grading is approved** for the lot; and **no permit issues for any fence within an easement without approval from the Director of Public Works.** On new-construction and regraded lots, those two sentences can cost weeks.

The application (rev. 5-27-2024) is blunt. Fees are **$150 for fences under 300 feet and $200 for over 300 feet**. A **scaled site drawing** is required, with distances to property and lot lines. Fences under seven feet fall outside the 2015 Michigan Building Code, while **fences seven feet or taller require a building permit** reviewed by both building and planning. Commercial fence and gate projects of any height go to the Fire Department for emergency-access review.

Best of all, the city says out loud what every contractor knows and few cities admit: *the issuance of a permit is not a determination of the correct, valid, or legal location for the fence.* Placement errors are the applicant's, the owner's agent's, and the fence company's responsibility. The city does not inspect for placement. Neighbor disputes over placement are a **civil matter.** A permit means your *paperwork* is right; it does not mean your *line* is right — which is why we walk through [how to confirm your property line](/blog/2026-10-03-fence-property-lines-surveys-michigan) before quoting a run.

## Novi: Six Feet, With a Front Yard That Is Usually Off Limits

Novi's residential rules sit in Zoning Code §5.11, and the city publishes a one-page summary better than most municipalities' entire websites.

**Height:** six feet maximum. **Location:** rear yard and along side yards toward the front of the house, **up to the minimum front yard setback** — if the existing home already extends into that setback, the fence may extend to the front of the home.

**Corner lots:** no fencing beyond the front yard setback nor beyond the exterior side yard required setbacks. That is stricter than a sight-triangle cap — on a Novi corner lot, the fence simply does not go past those lines.

**Front-yard exception:** fences are permitted in the front yard on lots **in excess of two acres**, or lots with **at least 200 feet of frontage within a recorded plat**, still at the six-foot maximum. If your lot fails both tests, a front-yard run is not happening without a variance.

**Waterfront:** front-yard fences are allowed on water frontage lots if they are no higher than **four feet** and do not obstruct the lake view. Hedge rows are prohibited from the front of the house to the lake.

Novi also regulates appearance. Fences must be **uniform in material, finish and color** along a property line of any parcel under 150 feet; where a fence has a finished and unfinished side, **the more decorative side faces outward**. Prohibited materials include scrap tires, canvas, cardboard, asphalt-style shingles, chicken wire or similar fine mesh, and corrugated or sheet metal. No barbed wire, no electric current.

One more Novi rule reads like it was written by someone who has maintained a fence: where a new fence abuts an existing one, it must be installed so the new owner **can maintain the vegetation growing between the two fences.** Two fences six inches apart with volunteer maple between them is a real problem, and Novi put it in the code.

**Pools** must be enclosed by a fence meeting the 2015 Michigan Residential Code and the city's pool permit handout. **HOA approval** is required where applicable, and the city notes plainly that many associations are more restrictive than Novi is.

## Bloomfield Township: The Dog-Fence Outlier

Bloomfield Township permits fences under Zoning Ordinance **Chapter 42, Sec. 5.7**, and requires a permit for every installation. A proposed fence that cannot meet the ordinance can be heard by the **Zoning Board of Appeals** — but the township makes you call the Planning Division at 248-433-7795 *before* applying, to find out whether a ZBA appearance is required. An application will not be accepted until the ZBA approves.

The packet asks for a completed building permit application (with the ZBA date if there is one), **subdivision association comments**, three copies of detailed specs with dimensions, three copies of a site plan showing the fence location, and a note on whether the fence is for dog containment. Fees are published: **$30 plan review plus $120 permit** for a fence or dog-containment installation, same for a pool fence building permit. A **final inspection** is required after installation.

Then the rule that makes Bloomfield different from every other city here. **Fences for dog containment must be set back a minimum of 16 feet from side lot lines and 25 feet from the rear lot line** — applying to **regular and invisible fences alike.** An invisible fence may continue around the front of the house to complete the circuit, but only within five feet of the house.

The approval path splits by fence type. A standard dog-containment fence that meets every setback **still requires ZBA approval plus an ordinance permit** under Township Code Chapter 8, Sec. 8-26. An invisible fence that meets the setbacks needs **only** the ordinance permit. In other words: the visible fence goes through the board even when it is perfectly legal.

Sixteen feet off a side line is a lot on a suburban lot, and it is the most common reason a Bloomfield dog fence plan comes back to the drawing board. If containment is the goal, price both options against the same setbacks.

**Pool fences** carry additional requirements: compliance with the township swimming pool ordinance (Ch. 10, Art. VIII, Sec. 10-228) **and** the 2015 International Swimming Pool and Spa Code **Section 305** barrier requirements; alarms on every door entering the pool area per ISPSC §305.4; specifications for all gate components; and HOA comments plus dog-containment standards where applicable.

## Corner Lots: The 25-Foot Triangle, Explained

If your lot sits at an intersection — including a lot that wraps a cul-de-sac corner or a subdivision entrance — you have a sight triangle whether or not anyone has mentioned it.

The geometry is nearly identical in Auburn Hills (§1902) and Rochester Hills (§138-5.204): from the point where two street right-of-way lines intersect, measure **25 feet** along each right-of-way line, connect the two points with a straight line, and you have a triangle. **Nothing above 30 inches** from established street grade goes inside it. Not a fence, not a hedge, not a berm, not a sign. Rochester Hills adds a 15-foot version where a nonresidential driveway meets a right-of-way.

Why 30 inches? Because a driver at a stop sign needs to see a child, a cyclist, or a car before the vehicle's nose clears the corner — and a six-foot board fence inside that triangle blocks exactly that sightline.

Corner-lot fences fail two ways: the fence stops well short of the corner, leaving a gap the homeowner hates, or it runs to the property corner and draws a complaint. The fix is a design decision made *before* the build — stop the tall run at the triangle, step down to a three-foot section that carries the line visually, or wrap the privacy fencing around the house side and leave the street side open.

## The Permit Package: What the Clerk Actually Wants

Across all six jurisdictions, the same four items show up — and having them ready is the difference between days and three weeks.

1. **A scaled plot plan or survey** showing lot lines, the proposed fence location, and distances to each property line and to the house. Troy requires it; Bloomfield requires three copies. A mortgage survey with the fence line drawn on it is often enough; a sketch reading "about 6 ft from the line" is not.
2. **Fence specifications** — material, height, linear footage, post spacing, gate locations. Bloomfield wants specs and dimensions in writing; Troy captures type, height and footage on the form itself.
3. **Contractor credentials.** Michigan requires the licensing and insurance attestation under MCL 125.1523a: license number, expiration, workers' compensation carrier, federal employer ID. A homeowner pulling their own permit signs the homeowner affidavit instead.
4. **Payment and processing.** Where cities publish fees they are modest — Troy at $150/$200 by length, Bloomfield at $30 plus $120. Where they do not, call. Nobody should be guessing at a permit fee inside a quote.

Two gates kill schedules: final lot grade approval (Troy will not issue a fence permit before it is approved) and easements (no permit inside one without the Director of Public Works' approval).

Before any post hole is dug, Michigan law requires a dig notice. Under **MCL 460.725** (the MISS DIG Underground Facility Damage Prevention and Safety Act, PA 174 of 2013), an excavator must notify MISS DIG 811 **at least 72 hours and no more than 14 calendar days** before digging — in practice three business days, since nonbusiness hours do not count. The ticket is valid for 21 days, digging must start within 14 days of the work-legal start date, and marked facilities must be exposed by **soft excavation within the four-foot caution zone** on either side of the marks. Calling 811 is free and available around the clock. Private lines — a landscape circuit, a gas line to a grill, an old septic — are **not** marked by the utilities and need a private locator.

## HOA and Subdivision Rules: The Layer Above the City

Every city here treats association rules as an overlay, not a substitute. Novi requires HOA approval where one applies and notes that many associations are more restrictive than the city. Bloomfield requires subdivision association comments in the permit file; Troy "strongly recommends" the check.

Michigan has no statewide statute governing whether an HOA can dictate fence style. Subdivision deed restrictions and bylaws are private covenants enforced civilly — so a city permit does not protect you from your association, and an association approval does not protect you from the city. You can be legal on one and in violation of the other on the same Saturday morning.

The practical hierarchy: **the most restrictive rule wins.** If the city allows six feet and the covenants cap fences at five, you build five. If the covenants require a specific picket profile in the front yard and the city allows a four-foot open fence there, you build the profile.

Where this gets expensive is style. A board-on-board privacy fence — our standard build, the reason it delivers *privacy you can see* from your own yard — presents a flat, dense face to the neighbor, and some associations approve only shadowbox or spaced-picket designs on lines facing a street. That is a decision to make before material is ordered, not during install week.

Two more items to check while the covenants are open: whether architectural review committee approval is required with a submission deadline — several Novi and Bloomfield subdivisions run monthly cycles that add two to six weeks — and whether the covenants specify stain color.

## Pool Fences: A Separate Inspection

If a pool is involved, the conversation changes. Pool barriers are governed by the 2015 Michigan Residential Code and, where adopted locally, the **2015 International Swimming Pool and Spa Code §305** — Bloomfield Township cites both, plus §305.4 for door alarms. The core requirements are consistent statewide: a barrier at least **48 inches** high, **self-closing and self-latching** gates, and no openings that would pass a four-inch sphere.

The failure mode is mundane: the fence measures six feet, looks perfect, and fails because the gate swings the wrong way, the latch sits too low, or the gap under the bottom rail is over the limit. A six-foot privacy fence is not automatically a compliant pool barrier.

## Six Ways a Legal Fence Still Ends Up Rebuilt

We have been called to fences that were built, inspected, and then shortened or taken down. The causes repeat.

**1. Height measured from the wrong grade.** Six feet from your side can be seven from the neighbor's side if the ground falls away. Auburn Hills measures from average surrounding grade; most cities use existing grade at the fence line. The highest ground point along the run is what counts.

**2. A berm or retaining wall underneath.** Auburn Hills wrote it into §1904(4) because people try it. The berm counts.

**3. The front-yard line was never established.** The front building setback line is not where the porch is — it is the setback distance required for that zoning district, measured from the right-of-way. A fence built to the porch and a fence built to the setback can be fifteen feet apart.

**4. The corner triangle.** Covered above, and it is the most common complaint-driven removal in the county.

**5. Fence in an easement.** Legal height, legal placement, wrong location. The DPW approval requirement exists because the utility has to get in, and a fence over a drainage easement gets cut by a crew with a saw, not a letter.

**6. The finished side faced the wrong way.** Novi says it outright, Rochester says it plainly, Bloomfield's plan review catches it. A fence installed backwards is not a violation you argue your way out of; it is a rebuild.

Then there is the slow-motion failure: six feet tall, no rot board, posts at 30 inches, pine boards on a south-facing run. It passes inspection and it fails the county — which is why we build to the [material specs that decide whether a fence lasts 15 years or 30](/blog/2026-09-23-wood-fence-materials-spec-guide-michigan) and why every post we set goes to frost depth, not to whatever the hole happens to be.

## The November Window: Why This Is the Week to File

Permits are administrative work, and administrative work can happen while the ground is still open. Once frost drives in, post holes stop being diggable without equipment most crews do not carry in January — so late October and early November are the last stretch where a homeowner can go from "I wonder what my city allows" to "the posts are in the ground" without waiting for April.

The sequence: confirm your city's height and front-yard rules against the ordinance; pull your plot plan and find the easements; call the building department and ask whether a permit is required and whether final lot grade is approved; file the MISS DIG 811 notice three business days before excavation; build at or below frost depth with the rot board and finished face where the code says. Do that and the fence is a fence. Skip the first or third step and it becomes a project with a case number.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How tall can my fence be in Oakland County, Michigan?

Six feet along side and rear lot lines is the standard across Auburn Hills, Rochester Hills, Rochester, Troy, Novi, and Bloomfield Township. Fences seven feet and taller leave the state's building-permit exemption under the 2015 Michigan Residential Code §R105.2 and require a building permit with plan review.

### Can I put a fence in my front yard?

Sometimes, and the limits are tight. Auburn Hills allows opaque fences to 2½ feet and non-opaque to four feet in the required front yard. Rochester Hills allows three feet in the front or side-street setback and no obscuring fence over three feet between the house and the setback line. Troy caps front-of-setback fences at 30 inches. Novi generally prohibits front-yard fencing except on lots over two acres or with 200+ feet of plat frontage.

### Do I need a permit for a six-foot wood fence?

Yes, in every city covered here. The state building-code exemption for fences under seven feet does not replace the local zoning or land-use permit, and each city requires its own application, plot plan, and inspection — several with a final inspection after installation.

### How much does a fence permit cost in Oakland County?

It depends on the city. Troy publishes $150 for fences under 300 linear feet and $200 over 300 feet. Bloomfield Township charges $30 plan review plus $120 for a fence or dog-containment permit. Other cities publish fees on their own schedules, so call the building department before budgeting — and never treat an aggregator website's number as current.

### What are the fence rules for a corner lot?

Auburn Hills and Rochester Hills both prohibit anything above 30 inches from street grade inside a 25-foot sight triangle measured along each right-of-way line at an intersection. Novi prohibits corner-lot fencing beyond the front and exterior side setbacks. Troy allows a non-obscuring fence up to 48 inches along the common street line on qualifying double-setback corner lots. Corner lots are the most common source of fence complaints in the county.

### Does my HOA have to approve my fence?

If your subdivision has deed restrictions or bylaws covering fencing, yes — and the city permit does not substitute for it. Novi requires HOA approval where applicable, Bloomfield Township requires subdivision association comments in the permit file, and Troy recommends the check in writing. Where the association is stricter than the city, the association's rule controls in practice.

### How far from the property line does a dog fence have to be in Bloomfield Township?

Sixteen feet from side lot lines and 25 feet from the rear lot line, for both standard and invisible dog-containment fences. An invisible fence may run around the front of the house to complete the circuit if it stays within five feet of the house. Standard visible dog fences require Zoning Board of Appeals approval as well as an ordinance permit.

### Can I build a fence inside an easement?

Not without approval. Troy will not issue a fence permit for construction inside an easement without the Director of Public Works' approval, and the same practical restriction applies almost everywhere — a utility with an easement can remove a fence built over it. Check your plot plan for drainage and utility easements before finalizing the fence line.
