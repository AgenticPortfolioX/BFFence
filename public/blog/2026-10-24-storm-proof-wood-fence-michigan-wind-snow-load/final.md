# Storm-Proof Wood Fencing in Oakland County: Wind Load, Snow Load, and Why Some Fences Fail While Their Neighbors Stand
*Published: October 24, 2026 | By BFFence*

---

There is a street in Rochester Hills where two fences went up the same summer, by two different crews, out of the same grade of cedar. They are the same height, the same style, the same color. They are forty feet apart.

After the storms of August 24, 2023 — the night the National Weather Service recorded 60 to 80 mph gusts across southeast Michigan and close to 500,000 customers lost power — one of those fences was still standing. The other was lying in the grass in three pieces, with two posts snapped off at the ground line and a third pulled clean out of its hole.

The homeowner with the standing fence assumed he had gotten lucky. He hadn't. He had gotten a deeper hole, a wider footing, and screws instead of nails.

This is the part of fencing nobody sells you on. Style is what you see from the curb. Load is what decides whether you are picking up boards in the spring.

## What Storm Failure Actually Looks Like

Wind does not break fences. Wind loads them, and the load finds the weakest link in a chain that runs from the top board, through the rails, through the fasteners, through the posts, into the concrete, and down into the soil. Whichever link is weakest fails first — and the failure signature tells you which one it was.

That distinction matters, because the fix is different for each. A fence full of loose boards does not need new posts. A fence whose posts rotated in the ground does not need new boards.

| What you see | What actually happened | What fixes it |
|---|---|---|
| Whole sections tipped over, posts still attached, concrete plug still in the post | Post rotated in a footing too shallow or too narrow to resist the lever arm | Reset or replace posts deeper and wider; bell the footing base |
| Posts standing, rails still nailed on, pickets scattered across the yard | Fastener withdrawal — smooth nails pulled out under reversing load | Rebuild panels with ring-shank nails or structural screws |
| Posts snapped off at or just above grade | Rot at the ground line, or a post undersized for the bay it carries | Replace posts; treat cut ends; check grade drainage |
| Concrete collar lifted out of the ground with the post inside it | Frost heave gripped the footing and pushed the whole assembly up | New footing below the frost depth with a gravel base |
| One section down, everything else fine, limb on the ground nearby | Impact, not load — a branch or a falling tree | Panel and rail replacement only |
| Gate sagging, latch no longer lining up, rest of fence straight | Gate post cantilever failure — a gate post carries a lever no line post does | Reset or replace gate posts, widen footing, add a brace |

Read down that list and you can see the pattern. Almost every one of them is decided before the first board goes up.

## The Force You Are Actually Fighting

A solid privacy fence is a sail. It is a 6-foot-tall, 8-foot-wide, immovable sheet of surface area and the wind has nowhere to go but through it or around it — and wood does not let it through.

The math is not complicated. Dynamic pressure in pounds per square foot is roughly **q = 0.00256 × V²**, where V is wind speed in miles per hour. A standard 6-foot panel between posts at 8-foot spacing presents **48 square feet** of sail. Multiply the two and you get the force on that one bay.

| Wind speed | Pressure | Force on one 8-ft bay of 6-ft solid fence |
|---|---|---|
| 40 mph | ~4 psf | ~192 lb |
| 60 mph | ~9 psf | ~432 lb |
| 80 mph | ~16 psf | ~768 lb |
| 100 mph | ~25 psf | ~1,200 lb |
| 120 mph | ~36 psf | ~1,728 lb |

Sit with the 80 mph row for a second. That is 768 pounds pushing sideways on a bay of fence that weighs maybe 120 pounds. The posts are not holding up a fence at that point. They are holding back a lever.

And that force reverses. A summer front does not push steadily in one direction for ten minutes; it gusts, backs off, and hits again from a slightly different angle. Every reversal works the fastener and the soil a little more. That is why a fence that has survived five ordinary storms can come apart in one bad one: the capacity was already spent.

The lever arm is the reason post spacing matters more than most homeowners realize. Cutting bay spacing from 10 feet to 8 feet reduces the load carried by each post by roughly 20 percent. Going from 8 feet to 6 feet cuts it by about 40 percent. You are not making the fence stronger. You are asking each post to carry less.

## What Southeast Michigan Actually Delivers

Here is where national fence content stops being useful. Most wind-load guidance is written for hurricane country and cites 130 mph and up, which reads as irrelevant to a Michigan homeowner. It is the wrong comparison in both directions.

The regional design wind speed for the Detroit and Oakland County area under ASCE 7 sits in the **105 to 115 mph** range. That is the number engineers use for structures that matter. A residential fence is not an engineered structure and nobody is stamping drawings for one — but that range tells you what the atmosphere here is capable of, and it is well above the 60 to 80 mph gusts that actually do the damage.

The record is not subtle:

- On **August 24, 2023**, NWS Detroit recorded **60 to 80 mph gusts** across southeast Michigan, with extensive tree damage and close to **500,000 customers** without power in southern lower Michigan. Oakland County was included in the federal disaster declaration that followed.
- **Oakland County** carries **1,239 NOAA storm records across 571 days since 1950**, including **36 tornadoes** and **15 FEMA disaster declarations**.
- **Michigan** has logged **13,171 severe thunderstorm wind events** between 1955 and mid-2026 — roughly **326 a year** since 2000.

Read that as a design brief. You are not building for the average thunderstorm. You are building for the one night every few years when a squall line crosses the county at 70 mph with the ground already saturated from two inches of rain.

That last detail is the one that catches people. On August 24, 2023, the rain came first. Saturated soil provides dramatically less lateral resistance around a post than dry soil does. So the wind arrived at the exact moment the ground was weakest. Two failures stacked into one night, which is why so many fences that had stood for a decade went down together.

## The Ground Is Half the Engineering

Michigan settles the frost question once for the whole state. **Michigan Residential Code R403.1.4.1** requires exterior footings to extend **42 inches below finished grade**. Under the Stille-DeRossett-Hale Act, local units enforce the code but cannot amend that number up or down. Auburn Hills, Rochester Hills, Troy, Clarkston, Lake Orion — same 42 inches.

Actual measured frost penetration in southeast Michigan typically runs **30 to 36 inches**. So the code figure carries margin here. Good. Take the margin.

A building footing is held down by the weight of a house. A fence post is held down by nothing but its own footing and the soil gripping it. It is a standalone cantilever with 48 square feet of sail on top. That is why a competent fence crew sets posts to the code depth and often past it — not because an inspector is watching, but because the post has no dead load helping it stay put.

Three things decide whether that footing survives:

**Depth.** Below the frost zone, the soil does not expand and contract. Above it, water in the soil freezes, expands, and grips whatever is buried in it. A footing that stops short gets pushed upward a fraction of an inch a year, and every push loosens the soil around it a little more.

**Shape.** A straight-sided concrete plug is a handle for frost to grab. Belling the bottom — flaring it wider at the base — gives the frozen ground above something to pull against and makes uplift much harder. Six inches of compacted gravel under the footing gives water somewhere to go instead of sitting against the concrete.

**Drainage.** This is where Oakland County stops being one place. The county splits roughly in two. The southeast corner — Royal Oak, Ferndale, Berkley, Troy, Southfield — sits on an old glacial lake plain: level, clay-rich, slow to drain. The northwest — Waterford, West Bloomfield, Orion, Clarkston, Highland, Milford — climbs onto moraine and outwash, with sand, gravel, dense till and organic muck all appearing within a few miles of each other. Across the county, roughly **28 percent of the land stays wet for long stretches**, and groundwater comes within two feet of the surface under about **30 percent** of it.

What that means at the post hole is simple and unforgiving. Clay holds water. Water is the fuel for frost heave. A post set in a wet clay pocket will move more than an identical post twenty feet away in ground that drains. Two fences on the same street, built the same year, can age completely differently because one yard takes a downspout and the other does not.

The county's most common soil, Marlette fine sandy loam, covers about 13 percent of the land and drains well — which is fine until you dig into the clay subsoil beneath it. Nobody knows what is in the hole until the hole is open. That is why we look at what comes out of the ground before we decide the footing.

## Post Size, Spacing, and the One-Third Rule

The old rule of thumb is that a post should be buried one-third of its total length. It is a starting point, not a specification, and in Michigan it is frequently the minimum rather than the target.

| Specification | Standard practice | What we specify in Oakland County |
|---|---|---|
| Post size, line | 4x4 | 4x4 minimum; 6x6 on runs over 6 ft, all corners, and every gate |
| Post size, gate | 4x4 | 6x6, always |
| Bay spacing | 8 ft | 8 ft on sheltered lots; 6 ft on exposed runs and tall privacy |
| Embedment | 24–30 in | 42 in minimum, 48 in on gate posts and low-draining ground |
| Hole diameter | 8–10 in | 10–12 in line, 14–16 in gate, minimum 3× post width |
| Base | none or soil | 6 in compacted gravel, footing belled at the base |
| Rail fastening | toe-nailed | through-bolted or structural screws at every connection |
| Pickets | nail-gunned | ring-shank or screwed, two fasteners per rail per picket |
| Concrete crown | flat or below grade | crowned and sloped away from the post |

A 4x4 post is a reasonable choice for an 8-foot bay of 6-foot fence on a protected lot. It is the wrong choice at a corner, at a gate, or on the west side of a yard with nothing between it and the prevailing wind. A 6x6 costs more per post and returns it in resistance to bending — the failure mode that snaps posts at grade.

Closer spacing does something subtle and valuable: it shortens the span of every rail. A 2x4 rail across 8 feet flexes under load. The same rail across 6 feet barely moves. When rails flex, fasteners work. When fasteners work, they withdraw.

## Fasteners: Why Some Fences Explode Instead of Tipping

This is the least glamorous section in the article and the one that decides the most outcomes.

Smooth-shank nails hold by friction. Under a load that reverses direction — gust, relax, gust — friction is exactly what fails first. The nail backs out a fraction of a millimeter per cycle, and after a few seasons the board is held by almost nothing. When the panel finally goes, it does not tip as a unit. It comes apart, and the yard is full of pickets.

Ring-shank nails and structural screws hold by mechanical resistance. Ring-shank nails have annular rings that grip the wood fiber. Structural screws have threads engineered for withdrawal resistance in lumber. Neither is exotic. Neither costs meaningfully more. Both change the failure mode from "exploded" to "held."

The other fastener decision is where the rail meets the post. Toe-nailing a rail into a post puts two or three nails on a diagonal into end grain, which is the weakest possible connection in wood. Through-bolting, or using a structural screw driven straight through the rail into the post, converts that joint from friction to bearing. On an 8-foot bay carrying 768 pounds at 80 mph, that joint is where the load concentrates.

One honest caveat: fasteners cannot rescue a bad footing. If the post rotates, the panel goes with it, however well it was screwed. Fasteners are the second half of the answer. Depth is the first.

## Style Is a Wind Decision

Nobody chooses a fence style by wind rating. They choose it by how much of the neighbor's kitchen they want to see. That is the right instinct — privacy is the point. But two styles can deliver nearly identical privacy with very different wind behavior, and it is worth knowing which is which before you sign.

| Style | Wind catch | Privacy | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solid board-to-board privacy | Highest | Total | Maximum sail area; needs the deepest footings and closest spacing |
| Board-on-board (overlapped) | High | Total | Overlap lets a little air pass; still needs serious footings |
| Shadowbox / alternating board | Moderate | Good, with gaps at eye level | Relieves a meaningful share of pressure; more visible seams |
| Lattice top or open upper section | Lower | Good at eye level, open above | Breaks the solid sail and the lever arm at the top |
| Picket with spacing | Low | Minimal | Air passes through; unsuitable where privacy is the goal |

The practical move for a homeowner who wants full privacy and lives on an exposed lot is not to abandon the solid fence. It is to build a board-on-board or shadowbox fence on **6-foot bays with 42-to-48-inch footings and 6x6 posts**. You keep the privacy. You move the failure point out of reach.

The other move, and it surprises people: a **lattice or open top section** on an otherwise solid fence reduces the effective sail area at the top of the panel, which is exactly where the lever arm is longest. A solid 6-foot panel takes more load than the same panel with an 18-inch lattice band. You give up a strip of privacy above eye level and buy real structural margin.

Whatever the style, one detail matters more than it looks: the top cap. A cap keeps water out of the end grain of the pickets, which is where rot starts. It also stiffens the top of the panel against racking. It is a small line item with an outsized effect on how the fence looks in year twelve.

## Snow, Ice, and the Weight Nobody Plans For

Wind gets the attention. Snow does quieter, slower damage, and in Oakland County it is not optional.

The design ground snow load for this area is about **25 psf** — a figure that applies to roofs and is a reasonable mental reference for anything holding snow. Michigan's statewide average is 44.3 psf, dragged upward by the northern counties, so southeast Michigan sits on the lighter end. That does not mean light.

Snow weight depends entirely on water content. New dry powder runs around **5 pounds per cubic foot**. New damp snow, about **12**. Settled snow, **18**. Wet, firm snow can reach **50 pounds per cubic foot**. One inch of water equivalent equals **5.2 pounds per square foot**. A foot of wet snow sitting on a fence rail is a real load, and it is sitting on the part of the fence with the least support.

| Winter load | Where it lands | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Wet snow on the top rail and cap | Top of panel, longest lever arm | Bows rails, racks panels, stresses post tops |
| Snow packed into board gaps | Between pickets, above the rails | Freezes solid, expands, pries boards apart |
| Roof shedding from a valley or steep pitch | Concentrated dump, often a few feet wide | Breaks rails and pickets outright; the most common winter failure we see |
| Plow piles and street push-back | Bottom of fence near the road | Crushes lower boards, pushes posts out of plumb |
| Ice at grade | Around the footing collar | Holds water against the post; accelerates rot at the ground line |

Two of those are preventable, and both are cheap. Keep the fence line clear of the roof's snow shed path — if your roof valley dumps onto the fence, that fence is going to lose boards and no fastener choice will save it. And keep snow off the bottom rail rather than piling it there with a shovel.

Ice at grade is the one to watch in a thaw. When the temperature swings above freezing for two days in January and then drops to ten below, meltwater runs into the soil around the footing and refreezes. That cycle is the engine of frost heave, and it runs hardest in the clay ground of the county's southeast corner.

## Gates, Trees, and Water

Three things cause more fence damage in Oakland County than wind load, and none of them show up in a wind table.

**Gates.** A gate post is a cantilever with a swinging weight bolted to it. Every time the gate opens, it loads the post laterally, and every time it slams, it loads it again. A 4x4 gate post in a 10-inch hole will be out of plumb within a few seasons — the gate drags, the latch misses, and the owner assumes the gate is the problem. It is the post. Gate posts get 6x6 material, 14-to-16-inch footings, and depth past 42 inches. Two gate posts braced to each other, or to the first line post, is even better.

**Trees.** A limb on a fence is impact damage, not load damage, and it is the single most common cause of a destroyed panel after a storm. Oakland County's mature tree canopy and its dead and dying ash are both relevant. Walk the fence line before storm season and look up, not just across. A branch that overhangs the fence is a branch that will eventually land on it.

**Water.** Saturated soil loses lateral resistance, which is why storm damage clusters in the yards that already had drainage problems. Downspouts discharging at the fence line, low spots that hold snowmelt, and gutters that dump at a corner post all shorten the life of the footing. Moving a downspout three feet is a free repair. Replacing a heaved footing is not.

## The First 48 Hours After a Storm

If a storm has already taken part of your fence down, the order of operations matters more than the speed.

**First, safety.** Downed lines, sharp broken pickets, and unstable sections are the actual hazards. Keep kids and pets out of the yard until the section is secured.

**Second, photographs — before anything is moved.** Wide shots of the whole fence line, close shots of every broken post at the ground line and every pulled fastener. If you think you may file a claim, this is the documentation your adjuster will ask for, and it cannot be recreated later. Photograph the undamaged sections too; they establish the pre-storm condition of the fence.

**Third, secure the opening.** If a dog or a pool is behind that fence, close the gap now with a temporary panel, a section of construction fencing, or a braced sheet of plywood. This has nothing to do with repair quality and everything to do with Tuesday morning.

**Fourth, brace — do not concrete.** A leaning post can be held with a diagonal 2x4 screwed to a stake. What you should not do is pour fresh concrete around a broken post to stiffen it. That locks the failure in place and adds demolition to the eventual bill.

Then call for an assessment, and be suspicious of anyone who quotes a full replacement from the driveway without walking the whole run. Wind exposes weaknesses several bays away from the section that fell.

## What Insurance Actually Covers

Fences are covered under the **"other structures"** portion of a homeowners policy — Coverage B, the same bucket as a detached garage or a shed. That coverage usually has its own limit, separate from the dwelling limit, and it is often lower than people assume.

Wind damage and falling-tree damage are typically covered when the event is sudden and accidental. Rot, termites, wear, and gradual deterioration are typically not — and adjusters look for evidence that the fence was already failing. This is the practical argument for keeping photographs and receipts from maintenance and staining: proof of condition is proof of coverage.

Payout depends on four things: your other-structures limit, your deductible, whether there is a separate wind deductible, and whether the policy pays replacement cost or actual cash value. Actual cash value depreciates the fence, which on a twelve-year-old fence can mean a check that covers a fraction of the rebuild.

Our honest guidance: a single-section repair typically runs **$250 to $900** in Metro Detroit depending on material and how many posts are involved. If your deductible is $1,000 or $2,500, filing a claim on one bay usually costs more in premium risk than it recovers. Widespread damage — multiple posts, several bays, a gate — is a different math problem, and that is when the photographs earn their keep. Read your own policy and talk to your agent before you file. We will give you an itemized written estimate either way, because adjusters want line items and so do you.

## Repair or Rebuild: The Honest Ladder

There are three ways to fix a failed post, and the right one depends on what is still sound.

| Situation | Approach | What it involves | When it is the wrong call |
|---|---|---|---|
| Post solid above grade, footing failed or heaved | **Reset** | Dig out, re-plumb, new footing below frost depth with gravel base | If the post is soft at grade — you will be back next spring |
| Post solid, hole intact, minor lean, no rot | **Sister** | Steel post mender or ground-contact post driven beside and bolted to it | As a permanent fix; it is a bridge, not a cure |
| Post rotted or snapped | **Replace** | Break out old footing, new post at correct depth | If more than a fifth of the run is in the same condition |
| Widespread lean, multiple failing posts, gate out of alignment, boards softening | **Rebuild the run** | Full removal and re-set with corrected specifications | Only if the fence is otherwise sound and under about ten years old |

The pattern matters more than any single post. One failed bay after a 70 mph gust is a repair. Four leaning posts spread across a run means the footings were all set the same way, and patching them one at a time is the most expensive way to end up replacing the fence in three years.

## The Pre-Winter Walk-Through

You have a few weeks before the ground freezes hard enough to make digging miserable. This takes an afternoon and costs nothing.

1. **Walk the whole line and push on every post.** A post that rocks in the ground is a post that will lean by March.
2. **Check the base of each post with a screwdriver.** If the tool sinks into the wood at grade, that post is rotting. Rot plus frost equals a spring repair.
3. **Look for soil pulled away from the post** or an enlarged opening around it. That is a footing that has already moved.
4. **Open and close every gate.** A latch that has drifted is a gate post that has moved.
5. **Look up.** Any limb overhanging the fence gets trimmed now, not in February.
6. **Fix the water.** Reroute downspouts that discharge at the fence line. Clear the soil away from the bottom rail so it can dry.
7. **Replace loose pickets and tighten loose rails** before the reversing loads of winter start working on them.
8. **Do not pile snow against the fence** when you shovel. Push it into the yard, not onto the bottom rail.
9. **Photograph the fence.** Clean, dry, intact. It takes four minutes and it is your condition record if a claim ever comes up.
10. **Get an assessment if anything on this list failed.** A post reset in October is a two-hour job. The same post in April, after a winter of heave, is a panel rebuild.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why did my neighbor's fence survive the storm when mine didn't?

Almost always post depth and fastener choice. A fence with posts set 42 inches or deeper in sound concrete, with rails screwed or bolted rather than toe-nailed, resists a 70 mph gust that will flatten a fence on 24-inch posts with nailed rails. Age matters less than most people think. A well-built ten-year-old fence routinely outlasts a poorly built three-year-old one.

### How much wind can a wood privacy fence take?

A solid 6-foot panel catches about 9 psf at 60 mph and 16 psf at 80 mph — roughly 432 and 768 pounds on an 8-foot bay. A properly built Michigan fence with 42-inch footings and 8-foot bays handles ordinary 40-to-60 mph storms comfortably. Sustained 70-to-80 mph gusts with saturated ground are where design and installation quality separate.

### How deep should fence posts be in Michigan?

At least 42 inches below grade, which is the frost depth set statewide by Michigan Residential Code R403.1.4.1. We set 48 inches on gate posts and in low-draining yards. Measured frost penetration in southeast Michigan is usually 30 to 36 inches, so 42 inches carries margin — and a fence post, unlike a house footing, has no dead load holding it down.

### Does a solid privacy fence blow down more easily than an open one?

Yes. A solid board-to-board fence presents the maximum sail area and takes the full wind load. A picket fence, a lattice top, or a shadowbox style lets air pass and relieves a meaningful share of the pressure. You can keep full privacy and still reduce load by choosing board-on-board or shadowbox and shortening bay spacing to 6 feet.

### Is board-on-board stronger than solid board-to-board in wind?

Board-on-board is generally more wind-tolerant because the overlap between alternating boards allows a small amount of air passage, and because the double-layer construction stiffens the panel. It is not a free pass — it still needs 42-inch footings and close spacing — but on an exposed lot it is the better choice than flat solid panel.

### How much does wet snow weigh on a fence?

Snow ranges from about 5 pounds per cubic foot for dry powder to as much as 50 for wet, firm snow; one inch of water equivalent is 5.2 psf. A foot of wet snow on a top rail is a serious concentrated load at the point of maximum leverage. The bigger winter risk in Oakland County is roof shedding dumping onto the fence line all at once.

### Does homeowners insurance cover fence wind damage in Michigan?

Usually yes, under the "other structures" coverage — Coverage B — when the damage is sudden and accidental, such as a windstorm or a fallen tree. Rot, termites, and gradual deterioration are typically excluded. Payout depends on your other-structures limit, deductible, any separate wind deductible, and whether the policy pays replacement cost or actual cash value.

### How much does storm damage fence repair cost in Oakland County?

Single-section repairs in Metro Detroit typically run about $250 to $900 depending on material and how many posts are affected. Resetting or replacing posts pushes toward the upper end because breaking out an old concrete footing is real labor. Widespread damage is quoted on site after a full walk of the run.

### Should I brace my fence before winter?

Brace only a post that has already started to lean; bracing a sound fence adds nothing. For a leaning post, a diagonal 2x4 screwed to a stake will hold it through the winter. Do not pour concrete around a broken or rotted post as a winter fix — it locks the failure in and adds demolition cost to the spring repair.

### What is the frost line in Oakland County, Michigan?

42 inches below finished grade. Michigan sets that depth statewide in the Residential Code at R403.1.4.1, and local municipalities cannot amend it. Measured frost penetration in southeast Michigan typically runs 30 to 36 inches, so the code figure provides margin for a fence post with nothing but soil holding it in place.
