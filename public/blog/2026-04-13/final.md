# Spring Fence Inspection Checklist: 7 Things Every Oakland County Homeowner Should Do Before Summer

*Published: April 13, 2026 | By BFFence*

---

Winter just wrapped up in Oakland County, and if you're like most homeowners in Auburn Hills, Rochester, or Troy, you've been eyeing that backyard fence wondering what the snow, ice, and freeze-thaw cycles left behind. Good instincts. Michigan winters are brutal on fences — and the damage doesn't always announce itself right away.

Here's the deal: catching fence problems in spring, before they get worse through summer storms and humidity, can save you thousands of dollars. We've seen homeowners dodge $2,000+ replacement costs just by fixing a leaning post or resealing a few panels in April instead of waiting until August when the damage is done.

This checklist is built specifically for Oakland County homeowners. We're talking about your climate, your soil, your frost line — not some generic advice from a national fence company that thinks Michigan and Mississippi have the same problems.

Let's get into it.

## 1. Do the Post Push Test — Check Every Single Post

This is the first thing you should do, and it's the most important. Walk your fence line and give each post a firm push with both hands. Not a gentle nudge — actually lean into it.

**What you're looking for:**

- **Rock-solid posts:** Great. Move on to the next one.
- **Slight wobble:** This is your warning sign. The post might have shifted during the freeze-thaw cycle. Mark it with a piece of tape and plan to address it soon.
- **Obvious lean or looseness:** This needs immediate attention. A loose post puts stress on every panel connected to it, and one bad post can pull an entire section down by fall.

### Why This Matters in Oakland County

Here's something a lot of homeowners don't realize: Oakland County's frost line sits at about **42 inches deep**. That means your fence posts need to be set at least 42 inches below grade to stay stable through winter. If your fence was installed by a contractor who cut corners on post depth — and yes, it happens — frost heave is going to find those posts every single year.

Frost heave happens when moisture in the soil freezes and expands, literally pushing anything in its path upward. If your post hole wasn't dug deep enough or wasn't belled out (wider at the bottom) to anchor against that upward pressure, your posts are going to shift. It's not a matter of if — it's when.

**The BF Fence standard:** We dig every post hole to 42 inches minimum and bell the bottom to lock the concrete footing against heave. If your posts are moving every spring, the original installation probably didn't meet that standard. It's worth getting a professional assessment before you pour more money into a foundation that's fundamentally flawed.

## 2. Inspect Every Picket and Panel for Winter Damage

Once you've checked the posts, walk the entire fence line again — slowly this time. Look at each panel individually. Michigan's freeze-thaw cycle is the single biggest enemy of wood fencing, and it works from the inside out.

**What to look for:**

### Cracked or Split Pickets
Small cracks in wood are normal as it ages, but splits that run the full length of a picket are a problem. Water gets into those cracks, freezes, expands, and makes the split worse. By summer, you'll have a broken picket. Replace cracked pickets now while it's a five-minute job instead of a five-panel headache.

### Warped or Twisted Boards
Wood expands when it absorbs moisture and contracts when it dries. After a full Michigan winter of repeated freeze-thaw cycles, some boards will warp or twist out of alignment. Minor warping is cosmetic. Severe warping compromises the fence's structural integrity and creates gaps that defeat the whole purpose of a privacy fence.

### Loose or Missing Fasteners
Check where the pickets attach to the rails. Nails work loose over time, especially with the expansion and contraction cycle. If you spot nails backing out, replace them with exterior-grade screws. Screws hold significantly better than nails in Michigan's climate and they're easier to replace down the road.

### Knot Holes
Knots in wood are natural weak points. When water gets into a knot and freezes, it can pop the knot right out of the board, leaving a hole. Those holes are open invitations for insects — carpenter ants, termites, and wasps love them. Patch any knot holes with exterior wood filler or replace the affected picket entirely.

**Pro tip:** If you're replacing more than a handful of pickets, it might be time to think about a full panel replacement or a reseal. Patching individual boards on a fence that's nearing end-of-life is like putting new tires on a car with a blown engine — it buys you time, but it doesn't solve the real problem.

## 3. Clear All Debris and Vegetation From the Fence Line

This one's simple but critical. Walk your fence line and clear everything that's accumulated against or near the fence over winter.

### What to Remove

- **Leaf buildup:** Wet leaves trapped against the base of your fence create a constant moisture barrier. That moisture accelerates rot at the most vulnerable point of the fence — where wood meets soil.
- **Fallen branches and sticks:** These hold water against the fence and can physically damage panels during windstorms.
- **Overgrown bushes and shrubs:** Vegetation touching your fence does three things wrong: it traps moisture, scratches and damages the finish, and creates highways for insects to move from the plant onto the fence.
- **Vines:** If anything is growing on or through your fence, remove it. Vines look nice for about one season. After that, they're holding moisture against the wood, adding structural stress, and pulling the fence apart as they grow.

### Trim Tree Branches

Look up. Any branches hanging directly over your fence are a ticking clock. A heavy branch loaded with ice or broken by a summer thunderstorm will come down on your fence with enough force to snap posts and shatter panels. Trim back any branches that are within striking distance of your fence.

**The rule of thumb:** If a branch is directly above your fence and thicker than your wrist, it needs to come down or be trimmed back far enough that a falling limb would miss the fence entirely.

## 4. Clean the Fence — The Right Way

Spring cleaning isn't just for the inside of your house. Your fence needs it too, and doing it wrong can cause more damage than doing nothing at all.

### Wood Fence Cleaning

**Do:** Use a soft-bristle brush with a solution of warm water and mild dish soap. Work in sections, scrubbing with the grain of the wood. Rinse with a garden hose.

**Don't:** Pressure wash your wood fence. We know it's tempting — it looks fast and satisfying on YouTube. But pressure washing drives water deep into the wood grain, causes splintering, and strips away any remaining protective finish. You're essentially fast-tracking the damage you're trying to prevent.

**For mildew and algae:** Mix a solution of one part white vinegar to three parts water. Apply it, let it sit for 15 minutes, then scrub and rinse. This is gentler than bleach and won't damage the wood or harm your landscaping.

### Post-Cleaning Inspection

After you've cleaned the fence, do another walk-through while everything is wet. Water makes cracks, splits, and damage more visible because it darkens the wood unevenly around problem areas. Note anything you missed during the dry inspection.

## 5. Check the Gate Hardware and Alignment

Fence gates take more abuse than any other part of the fence. They're the moving parts — they swing, they sag, they get slammed, and they bear uneven loads every time they open and close.

### What to Check

**Hinges:** Are they rusted? Are the screws tight? A rusty hinge is a weak hinge, and loose screws will let the gate sag. Replace rusted hinges entirely — don't just oil them and hope for the best.

**Latch mechanism:** Does the latch engage properly? Spring-loaded latches lose tension over time, and gravity latches can become misaligned if the gate has shifted. A gate that doesn't latch securely is a security issue — it's an open door for pets, kids, and anyone else you're trying to keep in or out.

**Gate sag:** Close the gate and look at the gap between the gate and the post. Is it even from top to bottom? If the gap is wider at the bottom than the top, the gate is sagging. This is usually caused by hinge failure or post movement. An anti-sag gate kit (a simple diagonal brace with a turnbuckle) can fix minor sag without replacing anything.

**Alignment:** Does the gate swing freely, or does it drag on the ground? A gate that drags is putting stress on the hinges and the latch post. It usually means the post has heaved (frost damage again) or the gate has warped. Either way, address it now before the hardware fails completely.

## 6. Evaluate Whether It's Time to Reseal or Restain

If your wood fence is more than two years old and hasn't been sealed or stained since installation, this is the year to do it. Michigan's climate is especially punishing on unprotected wood — the combination of UV exposure in summer, moisture in spring and fall, and freeze-thaw in winter will break down bare wood faster than almost any other region in the country.

### How to Tell If Your Fence Needs Resealing

**The water test:** Sprinkle or spray water on the fence surface. If the water beads up and rolls off, your sealant is still doing its job. If the water soaks into the wood immediately and darkens it, the protective barrier is gone and the wood is absorbing moisture freely.

**Visual cues:** Gray or faded color, rough texture, visible grain raising, and any signs of mildew are all indicators that the finish has broken down.

### Resealing vs. Restaining

- **Sealant** is clear or lightly tinted and focuses on moisture protection. It's the right choice if you like the natural wood color.
- **Stain** adds color while also providing moisture and UV protection. It's the better choice if your fence has faded unevenly or if you want a consistent appearance.

**Timing:** The best time to seal or stain a fence in Michigan is late spring — after the spring rains have passed but before summer heat sets in. The wood needs to be dry for at least 24-48 hours before application, and the temperature should be between 50°F and 85°F. April through early June is the sweet spot in Oakland County.

**Don't seal or stain in direct sunlight.** Apply in the morning or late afternoon when the surface isn't hot to the touch. Sealing in direct sun causes the product to dry too fast, preventing proper absorption and leading to a blotchy, short-lived finish.

## 7. Decide: Repair, Restore, or Replace

After going through the first six items on this checklist, you should have a clear picture of where your fence stands. Now comes the honest assessment.

### Repair If:
- Damage is limited to a few pickets, one or two posts, or minor hardware issues
- The fence is less than 10 years old
- The overall structure is sound and the foundation (posts) is solid
- You're spending less than 30% of replacement cost on repairs

### Restore If:
- The fence structure is solid but the finish is failing
- Multiple panels need attention but the posts and rails are in good shape
- A thorough cleaning, sanding, and reseal would bring the fence back to good condition
- You want to extend the fence's life by 3-5 more years

### Replace If:
- More than 25-30% of the fence shows significant damage
- Multiple posts are leaning or heaved, indicating a systemic installation problem
- The fence is 15+ years old and showing its age across the board
- Repair costs are approaching 50% or more of new installation
- You're fighting the same problems every spring (this usually means the original installation didn't meet Oakland County's 42-inch frost line standard)

### The Real Cost of Waiting

Here's something that's hard to hear but important: **procrastinating on fence repairs almost always costs more than acting quickly.** A $150 post replacement in April becomes a $600 panel-and-post replacement by September when the leaning post pulls down an entire section. A $300 reseal in spring becomes a $2,000 partial replacement when unprotected wood rots through two summers later.

We've been doing this long enough in Oakland County to see the pattern over and over. The homeowners who do a spring inspection and address issues immediately get 5-10 more years out of their fence than the ones who wait until something falls over.

## Oakland County-Specific Considerations

A few things that are particular to our area that national fence advice usually misses:

### Clay Soil
Much of Oakland County — especially Auburn Hills, Rochester Hills, and Troy — sits on heavy clay soil. Clay holds more moisture than sandy or loamy soil, which means more moisture contact with your fence posts and more aggressive frost heave in winter. If your fence posts weren't set in concrete (and belled at the bottom), clay soil will move them more than soil in other regions.

### Wind Exposure
If your property is on a hill or in an open area without natural windbreaks, your fence takes more wind load than a fence in a sheltered neighborhood. Check wind-facing sections more carefully for loose fasteners and leaning posts.

### Salt and Chemical Runoff
If your fence is near a driveway or road, road salt and de-icing chemicals can leach into the soil and accelerate wood rot at the base of the fence. Rinse the base of the fence with fresh water in early spring to dilute salt concentrations in the soil.

### Municipal Regulations
Most Oakland County municipalities require permits for new fence installation and have specific rules about fence height, setback from property lines, and material restrictions. If your spring inspection reveals that you need a significant replacement, check your local ordinances before starting work. Auburn Hills, for example, has specific requirements for fence permits that differ from Rochester or Troy.

## When to Call a Professional

Not every fence issue is a DIY project. Here's when it makes sense to bring in a professional:

- **Post replacement:** If posts need to come out and be reset, especially in clay soil at 42-inch depth, this is heavy equipment work. Renting a post hole digger and fighting clay soil by hand is a weekend you'll never get back.

- **Structural assessment:** If you're not sure whether the fence is worth repairing or should be replaced, a professional opinion saves you from throwing good money after bad.

- **Full panel replacement:** If more than a few panels need replacing, matching new wood to old wood (color, grain, picket dimensions) is trickier than it looks. A professional will get a better result.

- **Frost heave correction:** If your posts are heaved and the original installation didn't meet Oakland County's frost line depth, simply pushing the posts back in and adding more concrete is a temporary fix. The problem will come back next winter. The post holes need to be re-dug to proper depth with belled bottoms.

## Your Spring Fence Inspection Checklist (Summary)

Here's the quick-reference version. Print this out, grab a cup of coffee, and walk your fence line this weekend:

- [ ] **Posts:** Push test every post. Mark any that wobble or lean.
- [ ] **Pickets & Panels:** Check for cracks, splits, warping, and knot holes.
- [ ] **Fasteners:** Look for loose or missing nails and screws.
- [ ] **Debris:** Clear leaves, branches, and vegetation from the fence line.
- [ ] **Vegetation:** Trim bushes and tree branches touching or overhanging the fence.
- [ ] **Clean:** Wash with mild soap and water. No pressure washers on wood.
- [ ] **Gates:** Check hinges, latches, sag, and alignment.
- [ ] **Sealant:** Water test to check if resealing is needed.
- [ ] **Overall assessment:** Repair, restore, or replace?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should I inspect my wood fence in Michigan?
At minimum, twice a year — once in spring after the snow melts and once in fall before winter sets in. Spring inspection is the more critical of the two because it catches winter damage before it gets worse through summer.

### What causes fence posts to lean after winter?
The most common cause is frost heave. When moisture in the soil freezes, it expands and pushes upward on anything in the ground — including fence posts. Posts that aren't set below the 42-inch frost line in Oakland County are especially vulnerable to this.

### How deep should fence posts be in Oakland County?
At least 42 inches. Oakland County's frost line is approximately 42 inches deep, which means fence post holes need to reach that depth to prevent frost heave. The bottom of the hole should also be belled out (dug wider) to anchor the concrete footing against upward pressure.

### When should I reseal my wood fence in Michigan?
Late spring through early summer — roughly May through early June — is the ideal window. The wood needs to have dried out from spring rains, and the temperature should be consistently between 50°F and 85°F for proper sealant absorption.

### How much does spring fence repair cost in Michigan?
Minor repairs (a few pickets, hardware replacement, resealing) typically run $200-$500. Post replacement can range from $150-$400 per post depending on access and soil conditions. If more than 25-30% of the fence needs work, it's worth getting a full replacement estimate — the per-unit cost of repairs often exceeds the value of the remaining fence life.
