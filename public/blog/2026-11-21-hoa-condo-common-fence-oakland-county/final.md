# Who Owns the Fence in an Oakland County Subdivision? The HOA and Condo Association Guide to Common Fencing, Reserve Funding, and a Spring Replacement

Every November, a board somewhere in Auburn Hills or Rochester Hills puts the same item on the agenda: the fence along the common area is falling down, and nobody can agree on who is supposed to pay for it. One owner is certain the association owns it. Another is certain it is the responsibility of the four lots that back up to it. A third has a copy of the deed restrictions from 1998 that may or may not say anything about fencing at all. Three contractors have walked the property, and the three quotes disagree by $11,000 because none of them were pricing the same scope.

This is not a fencing problem first. It is a documents problem, and it gets settled long before a shovel touches the ground. What follows is the order we walk a board through: which legal box the neighborhood sits in, which document decides ownership, what Michigan law says when the documents are silent, how the money gets found, and how to write a scope tight enough that the bids you compare are actually comparable.

## The Question Every Board Gets in November

The reason this question lands in November is not coincidence. Most associations adopt next year's budget between November and February, and a common-fence replacement that has to be built in April or May needs to be scoped, bid, contracted, and funded before then. Waiting until March means competing for a crew during the six busiest weeks of the fencing year, and in Michigan it means digging footings in ground that has not finished thawing.

So a board that wants a new fence next spring is really making four decisions this winter: who owns the asset, what the scope is, where the money comes from, and which contractor signs the contract. Get the first one wrong and the other three collapse. A board that replaces a fence the association does not own has spent common money on private property, and that is the kind of decision that shows up in the next election.

## Three Legal Boxes, Three Different Answers

"Subdivision" is a word homeowners use and Michigan law does not. There are three different structures that all look like a neighborhood with a shared fence, and the ownership answer is different in each.

**A deed-restricted subdivision.** Homes on separate platted lots, with recorded covenants, conditions, and restrictions (CC&Rs) plus association bylaws. Michigan has no single homeowners association statute; a non-condominium HOA is governed by its recorded restrictions, its bylaws, its articles of incorporation, and the Michigan Nonprofit Corporation Act if it is incorporated. If the fence sits on private lots, it is a private fence, and the CC&Rs may or may not create an association obligation to maintain it.

**A site condominium.** This is the one that surprises boards. These are detached single-family homes on separate lots that look exactly like a subdivision, and they are condominiums under the Michigan Condominium Act, 1978 PA 59, MCL 559.101 et seq. The "unit" is generally the house and the land under and around it, and roads, entrances, and open space are common elements. A board that has been treating its site condominium like a subdivision for twenty years is working from the wrong statute.

**An attached condominium or townhome association.** Here the split between general common elements and limited common elements decides almost every fence and screen question, because the association holds the shared structure and the owner typically holds the patio, deck, or assigned exterior area.

| The box | How to recognize it | What a fence usually is | What decides |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deed-restricted subdivision | Platted lots, recorded CC&Rs, no master deed | Private property unless the restrictions say otherwise | Recorded restrictions and bylaws |
| Site condominium | Detached homes, registered condominium project, master deed | Common element if on common area, unit responsibility if inside the unit boundary | Master deed and condominium bylaws |
| Attached condominium | Shared walls, general and limited common elements | General common element, or limited common element if reserved to a unit | Master deed, bylaws, MCL 559.169 |

The practical takeaway: the word on the sign at the entrance means nothing. The recorded documents mean everything, and they control over any habit, precedent, or verbal understanding the board has been operating on.

## The Document Trail That Settles It

Twenty minutes with the right paperwork ends most of these arguments. Ask for the current recorded version of each, not the copy that came in a closing packet a decade ago, because declarations get amended and bylaws get restated.

1. **The legal description on the deed or title commitment.** If it references a condominium master deed and a unit number, you are in a condominium. If it references a lot and a plat, you are in a subdivision.
2. **The master deed and bylaws** if it is a condominium. Look for the maintenance and insurance allocation language, which is where fence responsibility lives.
3. **The recorded declaration or CC&Rs** if it is a subdivision. Search the document for "fence," "wall," "screen," and "maintenance."
4. **The architectural guidelines or rules and regulations** adopted under the declaration. These are usually where height, style, material, and color requirements actually sit.
5. **The plat map**, which shows common areas, drainage easements, and typically a rear-lot-line screening easement in newer subdivisions.

If the answer still is not clear after that, the question is a legal one, and the association's attorney should read the same five documents. A contractor can tell you what a fence costs and how long it lasts. A contractor cannot tell you whose asset it is, and any contractor who volunteers a confident answer to that question without reading the master deed is guessing.

## The Fence Between Two Lots: Michigan's Fence Viewers Act

Between two private lots, Michigan's rule is older and simpler than most people expect. Michigan is a closed-range state, which means no one is required to fence their property. But once someone builds a fence on a boundary, the Fence Viewers Act, MCL 43.51 through 43.55, takes over.

Under MCL 43.53(1), the owner who constructs a fence pays for its construction and maintenance. That is the default. Subsection (2) is where neighbors get surprised: if an adjoining owner or a tenant on the adjoining property **uses** that fence, the adjoining owner may owe proportional compensation for construction and maintenance, or may construct a fence in lieu of compensation. Under MCL 43.55, a fence viewer determines whether the adjoining owner is using the fence and what percentage of the cost is owed, and that decision can be appealed.

Two practical notes. First, this is a municipal or township process, not an association process. A fence viewer is engaged through local government, and it is a remedy of last resort between two private property owners. Second, inside a subdivision with recorded restrictions, those restrictions usually speak to boundary fences directly, and they control before the statute becomes relevant. The statute is the backstop when the documents are silent or when the two owners are not in an association at all. If the line itself is in question before any of that, start with our guide to [property lines and surveys in Michigan](/blog/2026-10-03-fence-property-lines-surveys-michigan).

What we see work, repeatedly: a one-page written cost-sharing agreement signed before any material is delivered. It costs nothing, it survives the sale of either house, and it eliminates the argument about the percentage. A fence viewer hearing costs both owners time, and the outcome is a number neither of them chose.

## Common Elements, Limited Common Elements, and Who Actually Pays

For condominiums and site condominiums, the vocabulary in the Act does the work. MCL 559.103(7) defines common elements as the portions of the project other than the units. MCL 559.106(5) defines general common elements as common elements other than limited common elements. MCL 559.107(2) defines limited common elements as portions of the common elements reserved in the master deed for the exclusive use of less than all of the co-owners.

Then comes the allocation rule. Under MCL 559.169, expenses for maintenance, repair, renovation, restoration, or replacement of a **limited** common element are specially assessed against the unit or units to which that element was assigned, unless the condominium documents provide otherwise. Everything else is assessed in proportion to percentages of value or as the master deed provides.

For a fence, that produces three possible answers:

- **On the common area** — a general common element, paid from association funds, which means reserves or an assessment across all units.
- **Reserved to specific units** — a limited common element, specially assessed to those units under 559.169.
- **Inside the unit boundary** — in a site condominium, the yard is often part of the unit, which makes the fence private property and the owner's expense, even though it looks like community property from the street.

That third case is the expensive misunderstanding. In a site condominium, the condominium plan and master deed determine where the unit ends and the common area begins. If the map puts the rear lot line inside the unit, the four owners backing up to the "common fence" may each own their own run, and the association has no obligation to replace any of it. Reading that map before the board votes is the difference between a $24,000 association project and four private projects.

## What the Reserve Rule Says, and Why 10% Isn't a Plan

Michigan requires a reserve fund. MCL 559.205 states that a reserve fund for major repairs and replacement of common elements shall be maintained by the association of co-owners. The administrative rule behind it, Mich Admin Code R 559.511, restricts the fund to major repair and replacement and sets a floor: the reserve must be at least 10% of the association's current annual budget, on a noncumulative basis.

That floor is an accounting minimum, and the rule says so itself. Ten percent of a $180,000 annual budget is $18,000. A 400-foot board-on-board cedar run at $60 per linear foot is $24,000, before gates. So a fully compliant association can hold the legal minimum and still be unable to replace one fence line without a special assessment. That is not a scandal; it is just what the law is: a floor, not a funding plan.

Non-condominium HOAs should not import the 10% rule, because it does not apply to them automatically. The same arithmetic problem exists anyway, and it is solved the same way: a funded schedule tied to the actual remaining life of the asset.

Two funding models do the work here. **Full funding** keeps the reserve near the ideal balance at all times, which means higher dues and a much lower chance of a special assessment. **Baseline funding** keeps the balance above zero and accepts a real chance of an assessment when a component fails early. A board should pick one deliberately, in a minuted meeting, and write down why. A board that drifts into baseline funding without saying so is the board that gets blamed when the fence falls over in year 22.

## Where a Fence Belongs in a Reserve Study

A reserve study is built from three pieces: a component inventory, a condition assessment, and a life and cost estimate for each item. The output that matters most to a board is the percent-funded ratio, which compares what is actually in the reserve against what should be there given the age and remaining life of the components.

Fencing gets under-modeled in most studies because it is entered as one line: "Fencing — $30,000." That single line hides everything a board needs. A better entry breaks the fence by run and by component:

- Rear common fence, north run, 420 linear feet, board-on-board cedar, installed 2006, 15 to 20 year service life, replacement cost $25,000.
- Pool enclosure, 180 linear feet with two gates, installed 2014, replacement cost $13,000.
- Dumpster screening, three-sided, six panels, installed 2019, replacement cost $4,200.

Those replacement numbers should come from current local pricing, not a national calculator. Our own [Troy cedar rebuild](/blog/2026-11-07-troy-pine-fence-rebuild-cedar-project-spotlight) came in at $56.69 per linear foot all-in for 176 feet of board-on-board cedar, and that is the figure a board should sanity-check a study against.

Michigan service life matters here and national averages get it wrong. A cedar privacy fence with good heartwood and a stain cycle every three to five years is a 15 to 25 year asset. Treated pine posts in ground contact in heavy clay, with a plow contractor pushing snow against the same end posts every winter, are closer to 10 to 15 years, and the end posts go first. A study that assigns every fence in Oakland County a flat 25 years is not wrong on paper. It is wrong in a parking lot in February.

## The November-to-April Calendar a Board Has to Work Backward From

A spring fence project is scheduled backward from the build window, not forward from the meeting.

**November to December:** walk the line with someone who builds fences, count posts, photograph the failures, and get a written scope. This is also when the board confirms ownership from the documents, because everything downstream depends on it.

**December to January:** issue the bid package to three contractors and require the same scope from all three. Two to three weeks for site visits and quotes is realistic.

**January to February:** review bids, check insurance, select, and sign. This is the point where a deposit secures a place in the schedule.

**February:** order material. Cedar supply tightens as spring approaches, and a fence package ordered in February is a fence package that gets built in April.

**March:** permits, if the municipality requires them. Requirements vary by city, and an approval from the association does not substitute for one from the building department. Our [city-by-city fence rules guide](/blog/2026-10-31-oakland-county-fence-rules-by-city-guide) covers how height, setback, and permit rules differ across Oakland County.

**April to May:** build. Footings have to reach below Michigan's frost line, roughly 42 to 48 inches with a margin, so the ground has to be workable before a crew can set posts.

The failure mode is predictable: a board that approves the project in March gets a May quote at April prices and a June or July install, right in the middle of the busiest stretch of the season, when every crew in the county is triaging.

## The Nine-Line Bid Package That Makes Bids Comparable

The reason three quotes on the same fence disagree by five figures is almost never dishonesty. It is scope. One contractor priced 6-foot board-on-board cedar with posts at 8 feet on center and a concrete footing at frost depth. Another priced 6-foot dog-ear pickets, 4x4 posts at 8 feet, and a footing "as needed." Both are legal bids. Only one is the fence the board thinks it is buying.

Send the same nine lines to every bidder and require a line-by-line response:

1. **Measurement.** Linear feet by run, with a marked site map or plat excerpt. Bidders measure independently, then reconcile.
2. **Height and style.** Six feet, board-on-board, dog-ear or flat top, and which face is finished toward the neighbors. Height and setback still have to satisfy the municipal ordinance.
3. **Posts, rails, and pickets.** Post dimensions (4x4 or 4x6), post spacing, rail count, picket thickness. Vague language here is where cheap bids hide.
4. **Species and grade.** Name the species and the grade. "Cedar" alone is three different woods with three different lifespans, and our [cedar species guide](/blog/2026-11-18-cedar-species-northern-white-western-red-michigan) covers which one belongs where.
5. **Footings.** Depth below frost, hole diameter, concrete specification, and gravel. This is the line item that decides whether the fence stands straight in year eight.
6. **Gates.** Count, width, hardware, swing direction, and self-closing hardware where a pool enclosure requires it.
7. **Removal and disposal.** State plainly whether tear-out, concrete removal, and haul-away are included, and whether dumpster cost is inside the number.
8. **Finish.** Stain and seal or natural, product named, number of coats, and whether it is included or a change order later.
9. **Insurance, permits, schedule, warranty.** Certificate of insurance with limits and the association named as additional insured, proof of workers compensation, permit responsibility, start and completion windows, workmanship warranty in years.

A bid that will not answer all nine in writing is not a bid yet. It is an estimate with a logo on it, and comparing it to a real bid is how boards end up paying twice.

## Insurance, Certificates, and the Signature on the Contract

The board signs as an agent for the association, which means the association's exposure is the board's decision. Three documents belong in the file before anyone starts digging.

A **certificate of insurance** showing general liability limits, with the association named as an additional insured, not just a certificate holder. Michigan requires employers to carry **workers compensation**, and a fence crew that shows up with a subcontractor and no certificate for that subcontractor is a liability sitting on common property. And a **written contract** that matches the bid line for line, including the disposal and finish items that get quietly dropped when a schedule slips.

One number worth knowing: MCL 559.157 requires an independent CPA audit or review annually for associations with annual revenues over $20,000, subject to an annual majority opt-out, and MCL 559.154 requires an annual financial statement to each co-owner. A five-figure fence project sits inside a budget that is already being reviewed. Keeping the bid package, the minutes, and the contract together is not bureaucracy; it is the record that answers the question at the next annual meeting.

## Architectural Review Without Becoming the Fence Police

Most fence disputes inside an association are not about the fence. They are about inconsistent decisions. One owner is approved for a six-foot cedar privacy fence, the neighbor is denied the identical fence, and now every rule in the community is negotiable because enforcement looks personal.

Three practices prevent that. First, the criteria should exist in writing before the first request: maximum height, permitted materials, colors, setback from the property line, and whether the finished side faces out. Second, a stated decision window, so requests do not sit for months and get approved by silence. Third, consistent application, with any variance documented and the hardship explained in the minutes.

Grandfathering deserves its own note. A fence built before the current rules may be allowed to remain while requiring compliance whenever it is replaced. Boards should know which of those two positions the documents take before an owner asks, because the answer at the counter should not depend on who is standing there.

And a reminder that cuts both ways: an association approval does not override the city. Height limits, front-yard restrictions, corner-lot sight triangles, and permit requirements are municipal questions, and a fence that satisfies the architectural committee can still fail inspection.

## Matching 18-Year-Old Cedar When You Can Only Replace Half

Cedar weathers to a silver-gray. New cedar arrives honey-colored and stays visibly warmer for two to three years before it catches up. If a board replaces 200 feet of a 400-foot run, the seam will be obvious from the street, the parking lot, and every drone photo the property manager takes for the listing.

There are four honest ways to handle it:

- **Replace the whole run.** Highest one-time cost, cleanest result, and the only option that produces a uniform fence in year one.
- **Phase at a natural break.** A gate, a corner, a building line, or a change in height hides a seam that a mid-panel cut exposes. Never phase in the middle of a straight run.
- **Stain the entire fence after installation.** A single stain coat over old and new narrows the gap substantially, and it buys the new material a few years of protection at the same time.
- **Accept the seam and say so in the minutes.** Owners tolerate a visible phase line when it was explained before the work started. They do not tolerate discovering it.

One rule to hold firm: do not mix species across a phase line. Northern white cedar and western red cedar age differently, weather differently, and will read as two different fences even after a decade. A uniform run is privacy you can see from the driveway. A patched run is a reminder of the year the board ran out of money.

## What Common-Area Fencing Actually Does

Boards sometimes struggle to justify common fencing because they describe it as decoration. It is not.

Common fencing screens dumpster pads and mechanical equipment from units, which is the single most common complaint a property manager fields. It defines the edge of parking so headlights do not sweep into first-floor windows. It creates a boundary around a pool or playground that supports supervision and satisfies the enclosure expectation most insurers want to see. It controls where a plow contractor can push snow, which protects both the fence and the landscaping. And at an entrance, it creates the sight line that makes signage and lighting work.

What it does not do is stop a determined intruder, and no honest contractor will tell a board otherwise. Fencing is one layer. Lighting, cameras, and trimming sight lines are the others, and a board that buys a fence instead of a security program has bought a boundary, not a guarantee. Protecting what matters is a program, not a purchase, and on a commercial or multi-family site the same logic drives the [commercial perimeter decisions](/blog/2026-09-26-commercial-fence-installation-oakland-county-guide) we walk business owners through.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who is responsible for the fence between two homes in a Michigan subdivision?

It depends on the recorded documents first and Michigan law second. If the fence sits on private property, the owner who built it pays for it under MCL 43.53, and an adjoining owner who uses it may owe a proportional share determined by a fence viewer. If the association's restrictions or master deed assign the fence to the association, the association pays.

### Does a Michigan HOA have to approve my fence?

Only if the recorded declaration, bylaws, or architectural guidelines give it that authority, which most do. The approval criteria should be written, applied consistently, and decided within a stated window. A city permit may also be required, and an association approval does not replace it.

### Can my HOA make me remove a fence that was already there when I bought the house?

Possibly, depending on the documents. Many declarations grandfather pre-existing fences until replacement, while others require compliance on any change of ownership or reconstruction. The board should be able to cite the specific provision, and the answer should be the same for every owner.

### How much should an HOA budget for fence replacement?

Budget from measurement, not from a rule of thumb. A six-foot board-on-board cedar run in Oakland County in 2026 runs roughly $55 to $70 per linear foot installed, including tear-out and disposal and excluding gates and staining. A 400-foot common run is therefore a $22,000 to $28,000 line item.

### Is a fence a common element or a limited common element?

It depends on the master deed and the condominium plan. A fence on common area is generally a general common element paid by the association. A fence reserved to specific units is a limited common element specially assessed to those units under MCL 559.169. In many site condominiums, the yard is part of the unit, which makes the fence private.

### Can an HOA use reserve funds to replace a fence?

Yes, if the fence is a common element being repaired or replaced and the documents permit it. Under MCL 559.205 and Mich Admin Code R 559.511, the reserve fund exists for major repair and replacement of common elements and must be at least 10% of the current annual budget, noncumulative. Using it for routine upkeep is not allowed.

### How long does an HOA fence replacement take from approval to finished fence?

Plan on four to six months. Bidding takes two to three weeks, contract and material ordering another four to eight weeks, permits vary by city, and construction of a 400-foot run with tear-out is typically three to five days of on-site work once the ground is workable.

### What should be in an HOA fence bid package?

Nine items: measured linear feet by run, height and style, post and rail specifications, species and grade, footing depth below frost, gate count and hardware, removal and disposal, finish specification, and insurance, permits, schedule, and warranty. Requiring all nine in writing is what makes three bids comparable.

## The Bottom Line

The fence belongs to whoever the recorded documents say it belongs to, and Michigan's statutes fill in the rest. A board that resolves ownership in November, scopes the work in December, bids it in January, and contracts it in February gets a fence built in April at a known price, by a crew that is not triaging six emergencies. A board that waits gets the same fence later, at spring pricing, with a visible seam and a special assessment nobody planned for.
