# Rochester Hills Sloped Backyard: Stepping a Cedar Privacy Fence Down a Two-Foot Grade Change
*Published: October 28, 2026 | By BF Fence*

---
Most fence quotes in Oakland County are built on an assumption nobody says out loud: that the ground is flat. Then you get out of the truck, walk the property line, and watch the lawn fall away from the house by two feet over the length of a driveway. That is when the number on the estimate moves, and the homeowner wants to know why.

This post answers that with a real project. A Rochester Hills backyard in a 1990s subdivision, roughly 96 feet of run along the west side, dropping 28 inches from the back corner of the house to the rear lot line — plus a drainage swale crossing the middle of it that falls 14 inches in a single 8-foot panel. We built 168 linear feet of 6-foot cedar board-on-board privacy fence on that ground. Some of it is racked. Three sections are stepped. The whole thing sits on posts measured from the low side, not the high side.

If your yard has a grade and your last quote felt light on detail, this is the post for you.

---

## What the Lot Actually Looked Like

The house sits high on the lot with a walk-out grade at the back — typical of the subdivisions built off the Rochester Hills roll between Adams and Livernois in the late 1980s and 1990s. The backyard is mowed lawn on top, a bank of about 18 inches, then a flatter lower lawn that carries water east toward a swale before it disappears into a storm drain at the side lot line.

Four things mattered more than any style question:

**The fall was not uniform.** Twenty-eight inches over 96 feet averages out to about 3.5 inches per 8-foot panel — gentle enough that a wood fence racks it invisibly. But averages hide the problem. At the swale, the ground dropped 14 inches across a single panel. Rack that panel and the rails would sit at an angle steep enough to look broken, and the boards would fight the rails all the way down.

**The swale is a water path, not a decoration.** Every hard rain in this part of the county sheets across clay and finds that low line. A fence post in the swale gets more water than any other post on the run, and it gets it from uphill, all year, forever.

**The neighbors' fences told the story.** Two doors down, a fence installed level from the high side stood with a 10-inch gap under the downhill panel and a dog that had learned to use it. One street over, a racked run from a crew that knew what it was doing had held its line for nine years. Same county, same weather, different method.

**Nothing was over 6 feet.** Which matters, because Rochester Hills caps residential fences at 6 feet along side and rear lot lines, and the measurement gets interesting on a slope. More on that below.

We walked it twice. The first walk set the line and found the swale. The second walk, with a string and a level, gave us the number that decided everything.

---

## The Number That Decides Everything: Fall Across One Panel

Forget the overall slope of your yard. The number that picks the method is the **fall across one panel width at the steepest point of the run**.

Here is how to get it in about two minutes with a helper. Drive a stake at the uphill post location. Pull a string from the top of that stake, level, to a point directly above the downhill post location — a line level clipped to the middle of the string is enough. Measure straight down from the string to the ground at the downhill end. That measurement is your fall across one panel.

On this project, measured in 8-foot bays:

| Run section | Fall across one 8-ft panel | Grade | Method chosen |
|---|---|---|---|
| North run along the side yard (48 ft) | 2.5–3.5 in | ~3–4% | Racked |
| West run, upper stretch (32 ft) | 3–4 in | ~4% | Racked |
| West run, swale crossing (24 ft) | 12–14 in | ~13–15% | Stepped, 3 sections |
| Lower rear run to the corner (64 ft) | 3–4 in | ~4% | Racked |
| Gate area | <1 in | level pad | Gate set here |

Two numbers, two completely different jobs, on one property. That is the whole point. A single "we can rack it" or "we'll have to step it" answer for an entire yard is almost always wrong somewhere along the line.

A racked panel pivots at the picket-to-rail connection so the rails follow the ground while the posts and pickets stay plumb. The top line follows the lawn in one continuous stroke and there is no gap underneath. It is the better-looking answer wherever the fence can do it, and it is the only answer that keeps a small dog from finding the wedge under a stepped panel.

Site-built wood racks essentially without limit, because the rails are cut to the actual grade and boards are fastened one at a time. Pre-built cedar panels are a different animal — a stock dog-ear panel has a little give at the fasteners and not much more. Forcing one past its capacity does not produce a slightly crooked fence. It produces racked-out rail pockets, split routing, and a panel that never sits square again.

## Rack or Step? The Trade-Off Nobody Puts in the Quote

| | Racked | Stepped |
|---|---|---|
| **Top line** | Follows the grade continuously | Stair-steps, level sections |
| **Gap under fence** | None | Wedge opens at the downhill post |
| **Posts** | Standard length | Longer — height plus the drop absorbed |
| **Labor premium over flat ground** | +5–15% | +10–20% |
| **Best on** | Gentle to moderate grade, site-built wood | Steep grade, pre-built panels, formal frontage |
| **Weakness** | Rails get visually steep on a hard bank | Containment gap; more cutting at every step |
| **Containment (dogs, kids)** | Better | Needs rot boards or backfill |

Stepping is not the cheaper-or-lazier option, and racking is not automatically the premium one. Both carry a labor premium over flat ground — 5 to 15% for racking, 10 to 20% for stepping, per the American Fence Association's installation standards — and stepping adds cutting and fitting at every single step. What decides it is the fall across the panel, plus whether the gap the step creates is acceptable to you.

On a formal frontage, a level top line along a house often reads better than a racked one, even where racking is possible. On a backyard where a beagle lives, a step with a 9-inch wedge under it is a problem no matter how good the top line looks.

## Where the Swale Crosses: Why Three Sections Got Stepped

The swale section was 24 feet — three 8-foot bays — and it fell 14 inches across the first panel, 9 across the second, 5 across the third. Racking that would have put the rails at roughly a 12-degree angle on the first bay and flattened out toward the bottom, which reads as a mistake even though it is structurally fine. It also would have put the bottom rail's uphill end close to grade and its downhill end 14 inches in the air.

So we stepped those three panels and racked everything else. The transition happens at a post, which is deliberate: a stepped-to-racked transition at a post is clean, because the post is plumb either way and the two rail heights meet at the same member. A transition mid-panel is not a transition, it is a break.

The three stepped sections also got longer posts. This is the part homeowners never hear about until the material list shows up. **On a stepped run, each post carries the fence height plus the drop it absorbs.** A 6-foot fence on a 14-inch step needs a post that stands 6 feet above grade at the low side and holds a panel whose top rail is 14 inches higher — so the post has to be bought longer, set deeper, and it does not get to be the same 8-footer as the rest of the run. On this project, three posts went to 10-foot stock.

That is a real cost. It is also the difference between a stepped fence that looks intentional in year three and one that sags at the joints in year two.

## Closing the Wedge Under a Stepped Panel

Every stepped section leaves a triangular gap under it — widest at the downhill post, closing to nothing at the uphill post. On 14 inches of drop, that wedge is big enough for a rabbit, a raccoon, or a determined terrier. There are four ways to deal with it, and we only recommend three.

| Method | What it does | When it is right | When it is not |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rot board scribed to grade** | A treated board cut to follow the ground line, closing the wedge | Almost always, on a backyard with pets | Where a design calls for an open bottom |
| **Regrade or backfill** | Raise the ground to meet a level section | Drops of a few inches, away from a drainage path | Across a swale — you would be filling the water's route |
| **Cut the panel to grade** | Bottom of the panel follows the ground, top stays level | Visible, prominent runs where the line matters | Cost — it is genuine custom work |
| **Leave it** | Nothing | Ornamental or ranch fence where containment is not the point | Any yard with a dog, a toddler, or a pool |

We used rot boards on all three stepped sections — pressure-treated 2x8, scribed to the ground at each post and fastened to the downhill face of the bottom rail. They are the honest solution on a swale: they close the gap, they take the wear at grade so the fence itself does not, and when one rots out in 10 to 15 years you replace a board instead of a panel.

Backfilling was not an option here. The swale is where the water goes. Raise the grade in a drainage path and you do not solve a fence problem, you create a basement problem for somebody downhill — possibly you.

## Posts on a Slope: Measure From the Wrong Side and It Fails

Michigan requires exterior footings to extend **42 inches below actual grade**. That is R403.1.4 in the Residential Code, and R403.1.4.1 sets the depth statewide, which is why the number is the same in Rochester Hills, Troy, and Clarkston. Measured frost penetration in southeast Michigan usually runs 30 to 36 inches, so 42 carries margin — and a fence post, unlike a house footing, has no dead load sitting on top of it holding it down.

Now put that post on a slope. If the ground falls 3 inches across the post's location, a hole that measures 42 inches on the uphill side is 39 on the downhill side. Fall 14 inches and the same hole is 28 inches deep where it counts. That is how a fence ends up leaning in year four while the posts next to it are fine.

The rule we build to:

> Measure depth at each individual post, from grade at the **downhill** face of that post. Never from the uphill side, never from the average, never from the high corner of the yard.

On this run, that meant:

| Post type | Count | Depth below grade (measured downhill) | Footing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard line posts, upper runs | 12 | 42 in | 10–12 in diameter, crowned |
| Swale posts (stepped sections) | 6 | 48 in | 12 in diameter, gravel base |
| Corner and end posts | 3 | 48 in | 12 in diameter |
| Gate posts | 2 | 48 in, 6x6 | 14 in diameter |

Two other code details that matter on a grade. Footing tops have to be level — R403.1.5 — which on a slope means you do not pour a tilted plug of concrete and call it done. And where a footing's bottom surface would slope more than 1 unit in 10, the footing has to be stepped. Practically, that means a post at the bottom of a bank gets a level, wider footing, not one that follows the hill.

Downhill posts also carry less soil bracing them, because there is less soil on the downhill side to push against. That is why the swale posts went to a 12-inch footing and 48 inches of depth rather than the standard 42. It is cheap insurance at the one place on the property where water and grade both work against the fence.

## Concrete, Water, and the Post That Rots First

Slopes move water. On this lot, everything that fell on the upper lawn ran toward the swale and along the bottom of the fence line. That makes the concrete pour the second half of the job, not an afterthought.

Three rules we follow:

**Crown the concrete above grade.** A footing finished flush with the dirt, or worse, dished, holds a puddle against the post forever. A slight dome sheds it. On a slope you also pour toward the low-grade side so the crown drains away from the fence, not into the neighbor's yard.

**Keep the post base out of the water table.** A 12-inch hole with a gravel base drains; a hole filled solid with concrete in clay does not. In the swale sections we dug deeper, put 6 inches of compacted 21AA gravel at the bottom, and set the post on that before pouring. The gravel gives water somewhere to go and gives frost something to heave against that is not the concrete collar.

**Expect the downhill post to fail first.** On every sloped run we have pulled apart, the first rotted post is a downhill post in a low spot — the one with the most water, the least soil, and the shortest effective embedment. Nine to twelve years is typical, and it is almost never the uphill post that started the problem. Knowing that going in is why the swale posts got the extra depth and the gravel base.

## The Gate Cannot Rack or Step

A gate hangs from two posts and swings on hinges. It cannot follow a grade, and it cannot step. It needs a **level pad**, and the time to decide where that pad goes is before the line is final, not after the posts are in.

On this property the flattest 6 feet of the entire run happened to be the side-yard gate location near the front corner of the house — under an inch of fall across the opening. That is close to ideal, and it is why the gate sits there instead of 20 feet further back where the homeowner originally pictured it. Moving a gate 20 feet puts it on 8 inches of fall, which means either a regraded landing, a gate hung high with a gap underneath, or a gate that drags.

Two details worth knowing. Gate posts get 6x6 stock, not 4x4, set 48 inches deep in a wider footing, because a gate is a cantilever: the weight of the leaf pulls at the top and the load lands entirely on two posts. And a level pad on a slope should be a genuine landing — a small flat area graded to drain away from the opening — not a set of stacked pavers hiding 6 inches of drop.

## Height, Corners, and the Rochester Hills Paperwork

Two separate rulebooks apply here: the state building code and the city zoning ordinance. They do different jobs, and homeowners routinely confuse them.

**The state permit exemption.** The Michigan Residential Code exempts a fence that is **not more than 7 feet high** from a building permit (R105.2). That is why a standard 6-foot residential fence usually does not need a building permit in Michigan. It does not exempt you from zoning. It does not exempt you from deed restrictions or a subdivision association. Those still bind you, and they are often stricter.

**The city ordinance.** Rochester Hills regulates fences under Zoning Ordinance § 138-10.107. The parts that matter on a residential lot:

| Rule | What it says |
|---|---|
| Side and rear height | Maximum **6 feet** along side and rear lot lines |
| Front and side-street | Maximum **3 feet** in a front or side-street setback |
| Placement toward the front | The fence may not extend closer to the front lot line than the front of the dwelling or the minimum front setback, whichever is less |
| Obscuring fence near the front | No obscuring fence over 3 feet high between the front of the residence and the minimum front setback |
| Corner lots | Subject to the city's corner clearance rule — sight distance at the intersection wins |
| Materials | Wood, metal, brick, masonry, or a comparable all-weather solid material. Fabric fencing prohibited. Wire, barbed or razor wire, spikes, and nails prohibited. Electric fences prohibited except a narrow Building Department exception for containing animals |
| Non-residential | Building Official approval required before erecting; capped at 8 feet, 10 feet for discretionary ornamental security fencing |

Here is where the grade bites. **Measure fence height from the downhill side.** A fence that measures exactly 6 feet at the high post can measure 7 feet 2 at the downhill post of the same stepped section, because the panel is level and the ground is not. Measure from the uphill side and you will build something you have to cut down after a complaint. We measure at both ends of every panel and take the maximum, then set the panel height so the maximum stays at 6 feet.

On a 14-inch step, that means the fence is effectively 4 feet 10 at the uphill end of the transition panel. That is the trade you accept for a level top line. If you want a hard 6 feet of privacy on both ends of a stepped section, you need a taller fence — which, in Rochester Hills, you cannot have along a side or rear line.

Also worth saying plainly: a fence that has to be under a height limit is a fence where "we'll just add a lattice topper later" is a bad plan. Check first.

## Why Cedar Board-on-Board on This Ground

Three reasons, in order of how much they mattered here.

**Cedar racks.** Site-built cedar is the most forgiving material on a grade because the rails are cut to the actual slope between posts and the boards are fastened one at a time. Nothing is being forced into a pre-manufactured angle. On a run that changes method three times, that flexibility is the whole game.

**Board-on-board stiffens the panel.** Alternating overlapping boards on both faces make a panel that resists racking and wind better than a single flat layer, and it is finished on both sides — which matters on a side-yard run where the neighbor sees the back. The trade-off is material: board-on-board uses more boards per foot than a stockade or a single-sided privacy panel.

**Cedar handles Michigan's wet-dry cycle.** Western red cedar takes stain, resists rot and insects without chemical treatment, and holds up to freeze-thaw without the checking you get in a lesser softwood. It is not immortal — a cedar fence in a swale with no rot board and a shallow post will still fail. But when the skirt is right and the posts are deep, cedar is what we would put on our own yards. Built to last is not a slogan on this lot; it is a material list and a depth measurement.

## Mistakes We See on Sloped Lots

**Measuring the slope once, at the middle.** The middle of a run tells you nothing about the panel with 14 inches of fall in it. Measure at every post.

**Setting every post to the same depth below the string.** The string is level. The ground is not. Depth comes off the ground at each post, not off a level line.

**Racking a panel past its capacity.** Site-built wood will take almost anything. A stock panel will not, and when it lets go it lets go at the rail pocket where you cannot fix it.

**Leaving the wedge under a stepped panel because "the dog can't fit."** The dog cannot. The raccoon can. So can the leaves, the water, and the snow.

**Putting the gate on the steepest part because that is where the old gate was.** A gate needs a level pad. Move it to flat ground before you set posts.

**Backfilling across a swale to make a stepped fence look tidy.** You are moving the drainage problem, not solving it.

**Building to the height limit measured from the high side.** That is how a compliant fence becomes a violation.

## Why This Build Went In During Late October

There is a real deadline in Oakland County, and it is the ground. Footings need to be dug into soil that can be excavated and concrete that can cure, and once the frost goes in, a run like this waits until spring. Late October is still workable — the ground is cold but diggable, and concrete cures slowly rather than not at all.

The upside of a fall build on a sloped lot is that the grade work gets done while the lawn is dormant and the swale is running. We watched that swale move water in real time during the build. In July it would have been dry and we would have been guessing.

The trade-off is staining. Fresh cedar in October does not get stained until spring — the wood needs to dry, and a fall application in cold, wet conditions is a waste of material. We tell homeowners to plan on a spring stain and to expect the fence to weather to a soft gray-brown over the winter, which is normal and does not hurt the wood.

## First Winter, First Spring

Two things to watch on a sloped run, and neither needs a tool.

**After the first hard freeze and thaw, walk the downhill posts.** Frost heave shows up on a slope before it shows anywhere else, because the downhill post has the least soil holding it and the most water around it. A post that has lifted a quarter inch will show as a gap between the bottom rail and the post, or a panel sitting proud of its neighbor.

**In March, look at the swale sections.** Rot boards take the wear at grade by design. Look for soil that has washed out from under one and top it off with a shovel of dirt or a little gravel. That is a five-minute job that adds years.

Everything else is normal care: keep the fence line clear of mulch piled against boards, keep sprinklers off the wood, and stain when the wood is dry and the temperature is going to hold above 50 degrees for a couple of days.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you build a privacy fence on a slope?

Yes. Site-built wood is the most capable material for it, because the rails are cut to the actual grade and the boards are fastened one at a time. What changes is the method: gentle grade gets racked, steep grade or a pre-built panel gets stepped, and one yard often needs both.

### How much does a fence cost on a sloped yard?

Expect 5 to 30% more than the same fence on flat ground, depending on steepness: about 5 to 10% on gentle grade, 10 to 20% on moderate, and 20% or more on steep ground. On our 168-foot Rochester Hills project the grade itself added $766, roughly 9% of a $9,202 total.

### How deep do fence posts need to be on a hill?

42 inches below grade in Michigan, and it has to be measured at each post from the downhill side. A hole that is 42 inches deep on the uphill face of a post can be 28 inches deep on the downhill face where the ground falls 14 inches. We go to 48 inches at swales, corners, and gate posts.

### Is racking or stepping better for a fence on a slope?

Racking looks better and closes the gap, and it works up to the material's capacity — generous for site-built wood, very limited for pre-built panels. Stepping is right when the fall across a single panel exceeds what the material can rack, when the panel is rigid, or when a level top line is the design goal.

### How do you close the gap under a stepped fence?

Four ways: leave it, scribe a rot board to the ground line, regrade or backfill under the section, or cut the panel to grade so the bottom follows the ground while the top stays level. For a backyard with pets, a rot board is the practical answer — replaceable, cheap, and it takes the wear at grade.

### Does a sloped yard need a retaining wall for a fence?

Usually not. A fence follows grade; a retaining wall holds soil. You need a wall only where you must create level ground — a terrace, a patio, a play area — and not merely to carry a fence. Note that Michigan exempts retaining walls up to 4 feet from a building permit, but a wall supporting a surcharge is a different review.

### Can you put a gate on a sloped section of fence?

A gate needs a level pad, because a gate cannot rack or step. Put it on the flattest stretch of the run and decide that before the line is final. Where that is not possible, regrade a small landing or set the gate between two steps with a level pad on each side.

### How do you measure fence height on a slope?

From the downhill side, at both ends of every panel, taking the maximum. A fence that measures 6 feet at the uphill post of a stepped section can measure 7 feet 2 at the downhill post. Rochester Hills caps residential side and rear fences at 6 feet, so the downhill measurement is the one that matters.

### Do you need a permit for a fence on a slope in Michigan?

Michigan's Residential Code exempts fences not more than 7 feet high from a building permit, and slope does not change that. Zoning still applies — in Rochester Hills, § 138-10.107 sets 6-foot side and rear limits, 3 feet in front and side-street setbacks, corner clearance rules, and material restrictions. Deed restrictions and HOA rules can be stricter than both.

### What is the maximum slope you can build a fence on?

There is no hard maximum for site-built wood, because the fence can step indefinitely. Practically, once the fall across a single panel passes about 12 inches, racking stops looking deliberate and stepping or building to grade becomes the right call. Past a 1-in-3 slope, footing design needs more than a standard post hole.
