# Pool Fence Code in Michigan: What a Compliant Pool Barrier Actually Requires in Oakland County — and Why a Gate That Latches in October Won't in February

The pool is closed. The cover is strapped, the pump is drained, and the leaf net is hanging in the garage. For the next five months the only thing standing between a curious four-year-old and twelve thousand gallons of water is the fence — and one gate.

Here is what homeowners find out in February: a gate that swung shut and latched on a seventy-degree afternoon in October does not necessarily do it anymore. The hinge post has moved a quarter inch. The strike plate sits an eighth inch low. The gate closes, but it does not close *itself*, and a gate that will not close itself is not a barrier. It is a wooden door with a latch on it.

Michigan has a specific, measurable set of rules for pool barriers, and almost none are explained in plain language by anyone who builds fences for a living. This is that explanation: which code edition is in force, the nine measurements an inspector takes, the wood details that quietly fail, the gate geometry behind most failed inspections, the permit path through Oakland County, and what it all costs.

## Michigan Doesn't Have a Pool Fence Law. It Has a Building Code — and Right Now It's the 2015 One

Michigan has no standalone pool-fence statute. The requirement comes through the construction code: MCL 125.1508a establishes one statewide code, and local building departments enforce it. For a house, that book is the Michigan Residential Code; for a residential pool, it sends you to the International Swimming Pool and Spa Code, where Section 305 holds every barrier rule.

Which edition? Here is where most of the internet is wrong. Michigan filed a 2021 Michigan Residential Code on May 1, 2025, effective August 29, 2025. Then, on July 7, 2025, the Michigan Court of Claims entered a stipulated order in litigation against the Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs, pausing implementation while the case continues. LARA's rules page says the 2015 editions remain valid and in effect while the order stands, and the City of Troy's building department says the same on its "codes currently in effect" page. A pool permitted in Oakland County today is reviewed against the **2015 Michigan Residential Code and the 2015 ISPSC**, and any page telling you the 2021 code governs is quoting a date that never took effect.

Two practical consequences: ask the department which edition it is enforcing the day you pull the permit, because the order could be lifted; and if a contractor cites the 2021 edition as settled law, ask to see the department's page.

Community pools sit under a separate rule — Michigan Administrative Code R 325.2128 requires a four-foot enclosure with self-closing, latching, and locking entrances and no sliding doors. If you are on a board dealing with an association pool, start there.

## The Nine Measurements That Decide Whether Your Fence Is a Barrier

An inspector does not read your fence. They measure it. These are the measurements from ISPSC Section 305, in the order they usually get checked.

| # | Requirement | The number | Where it is measured from |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Barrier height | 48 inches minimum | Grade on the side facing *away* from the pool, and that height must hold for 3 feet horizontally beyond the barrier |
| 2 | Openings | No passage of a 4-inch-diameter sphere | Anywhere in the barrier |
| 3 | Solid surfaces | No indentations or protrusions that form handholds or footholds | The exterior face |
| 4 | Bottom clearance, soil or gravel | 2 inches maximum | Grade on the outside |
| 5 | Bottom clearance, concrete or deck | 4 inches maximum | Solid surface on the outside |
| 6 | Horizontal members closer than 45 inches apart | Rails on the pool side; vertical spacing 1.75 inches maximum | Vertical gap between members |
| 7 | Horizontal members 45 inches apart or more | Vertical spacing 4 inches maximum; rails may face either side | Vertical gap between members |
| 8 | Chain link | 1.75 inches maximum opening, with or without slats | Mesh opening |
| 9 | Diagonal members | 1.75 inches maximum opening, no more than 45 degrees from vertical | Lattice and diagonal patterns |

Add the gate rules and the clear zone and you have the whole inspection. It is not complicated. It is just unforgiving, and every one of those numbers is a design decision that has to be made before the first post goes in the ground, not after.

The height measurement in row one catches people. Forty-eight inches is measured on the **outside**. On a sloped yard — and Oakland County has plenty of them, which is why we wrote about stepping a fence down a grade change in Rochester Hills — a fence that is fifty inches tall at the pool side can be forty-four inches tall where the neighbor stands. That is a failed barrier, and the fix is taller posts on the low side, not a longer talk with the inspector.

## Which Side Do the Rails Go On? The Rule That Decides How Your Fence Gets Built

This is the single most useful rule in the code and almost nobody explains it.

A standard wood fence has horizontal rails with pickets or boards fastened to them. Where those rails sit matters, because rails are ladder rungs to a four-year-old. The code handles it with a 45-inch threshold:

- **If the tops of the horizontal rails are less than 45 inches apart**, the rails must be on the pool side of the fence, and the gap between vertical members cannot exceed 1.75 inches.
- **If the tops of the horizontal rails are 45 inches apart or more**, the rails can face either direction, and vertical gaps can go up to 4 inches.

Now run the math on real fences. A four-foot fence with a rail near the bottom and a rail near the top puts roughly 40 inches between rail tops — under 45, so the rails must face the pool and the pickets have to be tight, at 1.75 inches. A six-foot privacy fence with rails eight inches off the ground and eight inches from the top puts about 60 inches between rail tops: it clears 45, so the rails can face the street and pickets can be spaced up to four inches.

This is why a four-foot design copied from a six-foot installation fails inspection, and why the cheapest fix is usually not "start over" — it is flipping the rails to the pool side and adding pickets, or converting the run to a solid board-on-board panel that has no through-openings at all.

Two more details. Solid surfaces — masonry, solid board, anything without openings — may not contain indentations or protrusions that create handholds or footholds, which makes a wide cap overhang worth a second thought. And board-on-board, where each board overlaps its neighbor, is the easiest wood design to keep compliant: no through-openings to measure, and no reachable rail once the boards are on.

## The Details That Actually Fail Inspection

We have walked fences with homeowners who were certain they were fine. These are the failures that show up over and over, in rough order of frequency — most of them the same geometry problems that govern [child-safe backyard fencing](/blog/2026-09-05-child-safe-backyard-fencing-guide-oakland-county) generally.

| What failed | Why it fails | The fix | Rough cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hog wire or welded wire stapled to 2x4 frames | The wire is usually 2-inch by 4-inch mesh; a 4-inch sphere passes the long dimension, and the frame creates handholds | Replace panels with pickets at 1.75 inches, or back the wire with a solid panel | Panel replacement, priced per section |
| Standard 2-inch chain link | 305.2.7 caps chain link openings at 1.75 inches | Chain-link slats, or a different material | Slats are the cheapest path |
| Lattice with 2-inch or larger diagonal openings | Diagonal members are capped at 1.75 inches | Lattice with 1-inch openings, or solid panel | Small |
| Four-foot fence with rails facing out | The rails are a ladder, and the closely-spaced rule is triggered | Flip the rails to the pool side | Labor, no new material |
| Pickets spaced 3 to 4 inches on a four-foot fence | Only allowed when rails are 45 inches apart or more | Add pickets to 1.75 inches, or go to a taller fence | Moderate |
| Gate with a visible gap at the hinge stile | Gap becomes a foothold and the latch does not engage | Rehang, add hinge-side filler | Small |
| AC condenser or pool heater within 36 inches | Clear-zone violation, and a very good climbing platform | Move the equipment or the fence line | Varies |

The pattern is consistent: the failures are almost never "the fence is too short." They are geometry problems — openings, rails, gaps — created by copying a design that was never checked against Section 305.

## Gate Geometry: The Nineteen Inches That Fail Most Pools

If rail placement is the most common design failure, gate hardware is the most common *maintenance* failure, and it is the cheapest thing on this page to fix.

The code requires a pedestrian gate to swing outward, away from the pool, self-closing and self-latching. Where the release mechanism sits is the part people miss. Two acceptable arrangements:

1. **Latch release at least 54 inches above grade**, measured from the outside of the gate. A five-year-old cannot reach it.
2. **Release lower than 54 inches** — then the release must be on the pool side, at least 3 inches below the gate top, with no opening larger than a half inch within 18 inches of the release. Rochester Hills' pool guidebook spells this out on its gate detail sheet, and it is why you see a small panel of plexiglass screwed to the inside of a gate around the latch.

The gate also has to be lockable, whether or not you use the lock.

In wood, this is straightforward. A gravity latch with a spring-loaded pawl on the pool side and a self-closing hinge set is compliant, durable, and cheap. A hasp and padlock is not, because it is not self-latching and a hasp is a foothold. The honest failure for most pools is not the hardware at all — it is the brick holding the gate open, which is the first thing an inspector or an adjuster notices. Our [guide to fence gates in Michigan](/blog/2026-10-10-fence-gates-guide-michigan) covers the hardware side in more depth.

Gate sag is worth catching early. A wood gate is a rectangle with a hinge on one side, and it will drop. A diagonal brace with adjustable hinges holds it for years; two light strap hinges drag on the strike plate by the second winter.

## When the House Is Part of the Barrier, the Doors Are the Barrier

Most backyard pools use the back wall of the house as the fourth side. That is allowed — the house is a legitimate part of the enclosure. But it converts every door into a barrier component, and that is where compliance quietly breaks.

If the house wall is part of the barrier, each door from the house to the pool area needs one of three things: an audible door alarm, self-closing and self-latching door hardware, or a powered safety cover over the pool that meets the ASTM F1346 standard.

The alarm is the usual choice, and it has performance requirements. CPSC's barrier guidelines, which track UL 2017, describe an alarm that sounds within seven seconds of the door opening, runs at least thirty seconds, and produces 85 decibels at ten feet — distinguishable from the phone and the smoke detector, with a deactivation switch giving an adult about fifteen seconds, mounted at least 54 inches up.

A battery-powered alarm meeting those numbers costs fifteen to forty dollars per door and installs in ten minutes. Sliding doors need the same treatment or a self-closing, self-latching screen. Pet doors leading to the pool area will get flagged on inspection, and they should be — a pet door is a child door for a determined toddler.

CPSC's guidance is worth quoting here: a barrier that completely surrounds the pool beats a three-sided fence that leans on the house. If your yard allows a four-sided enclosure, that is the stronger build, and it does not depend on a two-dollar battery every time a nine-year-old walks out the back door.

## Above-Ground Pools: When the Wall Counts and When It Doesn't

An above-ground pool's own wall can serve as the barrier, but only under conditions, and Michigan departments read them strictly.

The wall counts when the pool manufacturer approves it as a barrier and it stands at least 48 inches above grade around the entire perimeter. Bring the manufacturer's specification sheet to the permit counter — several Oakland County townships ask for it. The ladder is the loophole everyone forgets: steps have to be removed, locked, or surrounded by their own compliant barrier, because a ladder left in place is a staircase over a four-foot wall.

If you add a barrier on top of the pool structure instead, the vertical gap between pool top and barrier bottom cannot exceed four inches. Waterford Township allows exactly that, and requires a four-foot fence around any fixed or removable ladder.

White Lake Township adds two local layers: pools in the rear yard only, at least ten feet from rear and side lot lines, and a powered safety cover may **not** serve as the barrier — even though the state code allows one. That is the clearest example of why the department, not the code book alone, is the authority you build to.

Temporary and inflatable pools land in the same logic sooner than people expect. Some departments permit at 24 inches of water or 5,000 gallons; Ann Arbor publishes both thresholds. If it holds enough water for a toddler to go under, treat it as a pool.

## Hot Tubs and Spas: The Cover Exception, and Why You Shouldn't Count On It

The code contains an exception: ISPSC 305.1 allows a spa or hot tub with a lockable safety cover meeting ASTM F1346 — and a pool with a powered safety cover meeting the same standard — to stand in place of a barrier.

Read that as a question to ask, not a plan. Departments apply it unevenly: White Lake does not accept a powered cover as the barrier for a pool, and Rochester Hills requires the full enclosure for hot tubs and spas not provided with a protective cover. The exception also has a physical catch — a cover is only a barrier while it is on and locked. Straps go slack, gas struts fail, snow load sags a mesh cover into the water, and a cover off since the Fourth of July protects nobody in February.

Our default recommendation for a Michigan hot tub is the same as for a pool: build the fence. A four-foot enclosure around a spa is a small, affordable run, it does not depend on a cover being deployed, and it doubles as privacy — which is the other thing hot tub owners ask for.

## The 36-Inch Clear Zone: The Rule Your AC Unit Is Violating

Here is a rule that almost never appears on a contractor's sales page and always appears on an inspector's list: the code requires a clear zone of at least 36 inches between the outside of the barrier and any permanent structure or equipment that could be used to climb it.

In a Michigan backyard that means the air conditioning condenser, the pool heater, the filter and pump pad, the firewood rack, the kids' playset, a stacked retaining wall, the neighbor's shed, and — in winter — the snowbank a plow leaves against the end of the fence. A condenser cabinet is about 30 inches tall with a flat top; set 14 inches off a four-foot fence, it is a step stool.

The fix is rarely dramatic: shift the fence line twelve inches, move the equipment pad (a pool contractor's job, best decided before the concrete goes in), or add a non-climbable transition panel. What it never is, is nothing.

Walk your barrier from the outside with one question: *if I were seven years old and wanted in, what would I stand on?* Anything you can answer is a clear-zone problem.

## Permits in Oakland County: What Each Department Actually Wants to See

A pool and its barrier are permitted work, and the sequence matters more than the paperwork: pool permit, barrier in place before the pool is filled or finished, barrier inspection, final. Some departments issue a separate barrier permit; some fold it into the pool permit. Neither the water nor the plaster goes in until the barrier passes.

What the local departments publish, so you know what to expect:

- **Rochester Hills** permits any residential pool, hot tub, or spa over 24 inches deep, with a permanent recirculation system, or with structural materials. Pools sit at least 5 feet from side and rear lot lines, 10 feet from the house, never in the front yard. Overhead wires within 10 feet of the water's edge must clear 22.5 feet. The submittal includes a surveyor's plot plan and a fence and gate detail. Deed restrictions are the homeowner's problem, not the city's.
- **Waterford Township** requires the enclosure to withstand a 200-pound concentrated load on one square foot, keeps pools in the rear yard at least 5 feet from rear and side lot lines, and allows a barrier mounted on top of an above-ground pool with no more than a 4-inch gap.
- **White Lake Township** requires pools in the rear yard only, at least 10 feet from rear and side lot lines, and rejects a powered safety cover as the barrier.
- **Troy** confirms on its own codes page that the 2015 residential code remains in effect while the 2021 update is paused — useful when someone tells you otherwise.
- **Auburn Hills, Rochester, Clarkston, Lake Orion, and Bloomfield Township** publish their own setback and submittal expectations. Call with the address before ordering material — fifteen minutes on the phone has saved more projects than any permit expediter.

One more: if the enclosure is on a property line, [the fence rules by city](/blog/2026-10-31-oakland-county-fence-rules-by-city-guide) apply on top of the pool rules, and if it is a common-area fence in a subdivision or condominium, [ownership and funding](/blog/2026-11-21-hoa-condo-common-fence-oakland-county) get decided by the association documents before anyone measures anything.

## What Frost Does to a Code-Compliant Gate Between December and March

Michigan's residential code requires exterior footings to reach at least 42 inches below grade. That rule is written for buildings, not fences, and no pool code sets a post depth — but it tells you what the ground here does, and the ground here moves.

The cycle is simple and relentless. Moisture gets into the soil, freezes, expands, lifts, thaws, and settles. A gate post set shallow moves with it. The hinge rises a quarter inch and the latch misses the strike by three-eighths. The gate swings, but the pawl skips over the strike plate instead of dropping into it. Nothing is broken. The barrier is just gone.

What we do differently on a pool gate: gate posts are set deeper than line posts and get concrete, because the gate post carries the whole leaf plus a person leaning on it. Hinges are through-bolted, not screwed into a post face that will shrink. And the latch is a gravity latch with enough travel to still engage when the post moves.

The ten-minute check:

1. Open the gate about six inches and let go. It should swing shut and latch on its own, every time, without a push.
2. Lift the gate at the latch end. More than a quarter inch of play means the hinges or the post have moved, and the latch is on borrowed time.
3. Look at the bottom gap. Ice, packed snow, and a drift hold a gate open a half inch, and a half inch is enough for the latch to miss.
4. Look at the strike plate with the gate closed. The pawl should be seated, not perched on the edge.

If it fails, the fix is usually an adjustable hinge or a new latch — under a hundred dollars in parts. If the post itself has heaved, it gets reset, and in frozen ground that waits for spring. Until then, treat that gate as open and use the other one.

## A Winter Cover Is Not a Barrier

A tarp winter cover keeps leaves and debris out of the pool. It does not hold weight, and it is not a safety device. That distinction matters more in Michigan than anywhere, because a covered pool looks closed and is not.

A safety cover is a different product: engineered to support an adult's weight, anchored into the decking, and rated to the ASTM F1346 standard. Mesh safety covers typically run $1,800 to $3,500 installed; solid covers $2,200 to $4,500; automatic track covers start around $10,000 and go up from there. A standard tarp cover is $200 to $600 and provides none of that.

Where a cover can legally stand in for a fence, it has to be the rated kind, and your department has to accept it — White Lake does not. Where it cannot, the cover is an additional layer on top of a fence, not a substitute for one. And a sagging tarp over twelve thousand gallons is arguably a worse hazard than open water, because a child who steps onto it goes under with the cover on top.

## Insurance, Resale, and the Attractive-Nuisance File

Most homeowners policies treat a pool as an attractive nuisance, and many condition liability coverage on a barrier configuration that sits a notch above the local code minimum. Worth a phone call before you build, not after: ask your carrier what it requires for the barrier, the gate, and the cover, get it in writing, and build to the stricter of the two standards — theirs or your city's.

Resale is the quieter version. A buyer's inspector will note a gate that does not self-latch, a propped gate, a missing door alarm, or equipment inside the clear zone, and those notes become negotiation credits far larger than the repair.

If you are selling — or buying a house with a pool — a written barrier check is worth having: measurements, gate operation, clear zone, door alarms, cover rating, and which code edition the department is enforcing. It is the kind of document that ends an argument.

## What You Can Do in November, and What Has to Wait for April

Frozen ground changes the schedule, and this is the week to sort the two lists.

**Do now, before the ground locks up:** measure the enclosure line and photograph it from outside, including the equipment pad and AC unit. Pull the permit application and the department's fence and gate detail sheet. Run the gate check and replace a latch or hinge while your hands still work outside. Fix the clear zone — firewood, bins, playset. Test a door alarm on every door that opens to the pool. Book a March estimate; good crews fill April first and cedar lead times tighten in spring.

**Wait for April:** setting posts. Posts set in frozen ground do not get a reliable footing, and a gate post set into frost is a gate post that moves in March, which puts you back at the top of this article. Panel work on an existing frame can sometimes be done over the winter; anything that involves digging waits.

Protecting what matters does not pause for the season. The pool is closed for five months; the barrier is on duty the whole time. Spend twenty minutes on it this week and it will be there in May.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Michigan require a fence around a swimming pool?

Yes. Michigan has no standalone pool-fence statute, but the residential code points to the International Swimming Pool and Spa Code, and Section 305 requires a barrier around any residential pool. Local departments enforce it, and most inspect the barrier before the pool is filled.

### How tall does a pool fence have to be in Michigan?

48 inches minimum, measured from grade on the side facing away from the pool and held for three feet beyond the barrier. Some Michigan communities require 60 inches — one Oakland County pool builder reports that in Bloomfield Hills, Birmingham, and Beverly Hills — so confirm before ordering posts.

### Which code edition applies to a Michigan pool right now?

The 2015 Michigan Residential Code, which sends pools to the 2015 ISPSC. A 2021 update was filed May 1, 2025, but a July 7, 2025 Court of Claims order paused it, and LARA says the 2015 editions remain in effect. Troy's building department confirms it.

### Do the horizontal rails on my fence have to face the pool?

Only when the tops of the rails are less than 45 inches apart. In that case the rails must be on the pool side and vertical spacing cannot exceed 1.75 inches. If the rails are 45 inches apart or more, they can face either direction and vertical spacing can go to 4 inches.

### Does a pool safety cover replace the fence requirement?

Only a cover rated to ASTM F1346, and only where your building department accepts it. White Lake Township does not accept a powered cover as the barrier for a pool. A standard tarp winter cover is not a safety device and never counts.

### Can the back wall of my house count as part of the pool barrier?

Yes, but every door from the house to the pool area then needs a compliant alarm, self-closing and self-latching hardware, or a rated powered cover. Alarms should meet UL 2017 — audible within seven seconds, 85 decibels at ten feet, deactivation switch at least 54 inches high.

### Why does my pool gate stop latching in the winter?

Frost heave moves the gate post a quarter inch and the latch misses the strike; Michigan's freeze-thaw cycle lifts and settles shallow footings all winter. Set gate posts deeper, use through-bolted hinges and a gravity latch with travel, and check the gate every few weeks.

### How much does a pool fence cost in Oakland County?

Removable mesh panels run about $15 to $35 per linear foot installed; a 6-foot board-on-board cedar fence runs $55 to $70 per linear foot including tear-out. A 90-foot backyard enclosure in cedar typically lands between $5,000 and $6,500 before gates, staining, and permit.

## The Bottom Line

The rules are not mysterious. Forty-eight inches measured from the outside, no four-inch openings, rails on the pool side when they are closely spaced, a gate that closes and latches itself with a release a child cannot reach, thirty-six inches of clear space around the outside, and a house wall that counts only if its doors are alarms.

What fails is not the code. It is the design that never got measured against it, and the gate nobody checked after the first hard freeze. Privacy you can see is worth having, but the barrier is the part that keeps its promise in February.
