# When the Leaves Come Down, So Does Your Privacy: Winter Sightlines and Year-Round Screening for Oakland County Yards
*Published: November 4, 2026 | By BF Fence*
---
## The Yard That Was Private in July

Here is the short answer, before we get into the how: if your backyard only feels private between May and October, you do not have privacy. You have foliage. And foliage in Oakland County runs on a schedule that has nothing to do with yours.

The fix is a four-part plan. Walk your property line in November and look at what you can actually see. Fix the gaps in your fence before you worry about its height. Put evergreens above the fence line where a second story can still see in. And measure from the snow line, not the grass line, because by January the ground your fence was built on is somewhere under a foot of snow.

That is the whole article. The rest is the detail that makes those four things work in Auburn Hills, Rochester Hills, Troy, Clarkston, and the rest of the county — the numbers, the code, and the reasons a fence that looked solid in July turns into a light show in February.

## Why November Is the Honest Month

Oakland County's median first fall frost lands in the first week of November. That is not a gardening statistic. It is the date the curtain opens.

Leaf drop happens in stages, and the stages matter. Ash and honey locust go early, sometimes bare by mid-October. Maples — the sugar maples that make this county look like a postcard in early October — are mostly down by the first week of November. Elms and walnuts follow. Then there is the exception that fools people: pin oaks and some red oaks hold their brown leaves through most of the winter. A yard ringed by mature pin oaks keeps a surprising amount of screening into January. A yard ringed by maples keeps none.

Two more things change in the same week. The sun drops. At our latitude, noon sun sits about 32 degrees above the horizon on November 4 and about 24 degrees at the winter solstice, against 71 degrees in late June. Low sun comes in almost flat, which means it reaches deeper into rooms, and the reverse is also true: from outside, a lit interior is easier to see into at 5:30 in the evening than at 8:00 in July. And the yard stops being used. The grill gets covered, the patio furniture gets stacked, and nobody walks the perimeter for five months.

That combination — no leaves, low light, lit windows, no one looking — is why the complaint calls start in late November and peak in January. "I didn't realize how much the neighbors could see." They could always see. The trees were doing the work.

So do the walk. Go out on a weekday evening after dark with the interior lights on and walk your property line slowly, all four sides. Then do it again in daylight from the highest point you can find — your deck, a second-story window, the top of your driveway. What you can see from out there is roughly what they can see from in here. Most people find one bad angle, not a bad yard, and one bad angle is a much cheaper problem.

## The Geometry of Being Seen

Privacy fails in exactly three ways: over the fence, through the fence, and around the fence. Height solves the first one, opacity solves the second, and layout solves the third. Most homeowners spend all their money on height, which is the one you can least control.

Start with eye level. A standing adult's eyes sit roughly 5 feet 2 inches to 5 feet 10 inches above whatever they are standing on. On flat ground on the other side of a 6-foot fence, that person cannot see your patio. Put them on a 3-foot deck and their eyes are at 8 feet 2 inches, and the arithmetic changes fast. A 6-foot fence six feet away from that deck leaves everything more than about 16 feet past the fence in plain view. Move that deck to 5 feet and the visible zone starts about 9 feet past the fence.

| Observer position | Eye height above grade | Ground visible past a 6-ft fence (fence 6 ft away) |
|---|---|---|
| Standing on flat ground | 5 ft 2 in – 5 ft 10 in | None — fence blocks the ground line |
| Standing on a 1.5-ft stoop | 6 ft 8 in | Beyond ~51 ft |
| Standing on a 3-ft deck | 8 ft 2 in | Beyond ~16 ft |
| Standing on a 5-ft deck | 10 ft 2 in | Beyond ~9 ft |
| Second-story window, sill at 14 ft | 14 ft+ | Nearly the entire yard |

Two lessons fall out of that table. First, a 6-foot fence — the standard side and rear limit across Oakland County — is a ground-level privacy tool. It is very good at blocking the neighbor standing at his grill. It is not a wall against a raised deck, and no amount of arguing with your fence will change that. Second, the height of the observer matters more than the height of the fence. When a neighbor builds a deck, adds a hot tub platform, or puts in an above-ground pool, your privacy changes without anyone touching your fence.

Which is why the second layer exists. A fence handles the first six feet. Evergreens, a pergola, a screen panel on a post, or a tall hedge behind the fence handle the sightline from above. We will get to that in detail.

Then there is the failure nobody photographs: around the fence. A solid 6-foot run between two houses still leaves the side yard open, and in most Oakland County subdivisions the side yard is exactly where the gap lives — a 4-foot walk gate, an AC pad, a downspout trench, or the strip between the fence end and the corner of the house. Stand in your neighbor's driveway and look down that strip. If you can see the patio, the fence is doing its job and the layout is not. The same thing happens at the back corners, where a fence that stops eight inches short of the lot line leaves a slot wide enough to see through at an angle, and along any slope, where the fence steps down and the top rail drops below eye level at the low end. None of those are height problems. They are layout problems, and layout is the cheapest thing on this list to fix.

The winter sun is worth one more minute, because it is the part nobody expects. At the winter solstice, a 6-foot fence at noon casts a shadow about 13.6 feet long. In late June, the same fence casts a shadow about 2 feet long. That is a difference you feel in January — a west-side fence that is a welcome shade block in August becomes a cold, dark patio in December. If you are planning a fence and you have a patio you want to use in winter, put the tall run on the north and west and keep the south open. Privacy and sunlight are both design decisions, and they can be made together.

## What the Ordinance Lets You Build — and What It Doesn't

Here is the constraint that shapes every privacy plan in this county: six feet is the ceiling for side and rear fences in Auburn Hills, Rochester Hills, Rochester, Troy, Novi, and Bloomfield Township. Front yards are stricter — anywhere from 30 inches to four feet depending on the city and the material. Corner lots carry a 25-foot sight triangle at intersections where nothing above 30 inches is allowed. We covered the section-by-section detail in our city-by-city fence rules guide, and it is worth reading before you sketch anything.

What matters here is how height is measured. It is measured from grade, and "grade" is not always the friendly number you had in mind. Auburn Hills measures fence height inclusive of any berm, so building up a mound of dirt to gain six inches of fence usually does not buy you six inches of anything. Lattice toppers count. Decorative post caps generally count. Trellis panels attached to the top of a fence count, unless your city's ordinance says otherwise — and you should assume it does until the building department tells you it doesn't.

There is also a threshold at seven feet. Under the 2015 Michigan Residential Code, fences under seven feet are exempt from the state building permit, but that exemption has nothing to do with your city's zoning permit, which you still need. Cross seven feet and you are into plan review and a building permit. And in most of these cities you cannot build a seven-foot side fence anyway, because the zoning cap is six.

So the honest conclusion is this: in Oakland County you cannot buy privacy with height. You can only buy it with opacity, placement, and layering. That is not a limitation to complain about. It is actually the cheaper way to solve the problem.

## Opacity Beats Height: Why Cedar Opens Up in January

A fence is not a wall. It is a few hundred pieces of wood that move.

Wood shrinks as it dries, and it shrinks most across the width of the board — the direction that matters on a fence. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory puts tangential shrinkage from green to oven-dry at roughly 5 percent for western redcedar and about 7.4 to 7.7 percent for the southern pines. Translated into fence boards: a 5.5-inch cedar picket losing about a sixth of an inch of width, and a pine picket losing closer to a quarter inch.

Now picture a standard side-by-side privacy fence — boards butted edge to edge on one face of the rails. On installation day in June, those boards are tight. Through the summer they dry, and each one gives up a sliver. By January, with the furnace running and relative humidity in the twenties, every one of those slivers is a gap, and a few hundred slivers is a fence you can see through at an angle. The wood is not defective. That is what wood does, and it is the single most common complaint we hear about a fence that looked perfect on day one.

| Layout | Sightline gaps | Finished look | Cedar per foot | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Side-by-side (stockade) | Open as boards dry, most visible at an angle | One face | Least | Boundary marking, budget builds |
| Board-on-board | None — overlap covers the seams | Both faces | Most | Privacy on a shared property line |
| Shadowbox (good neighbor) | Deliberate gaps, semi-private | Both faces | Middle | Airflow, softer look, pool areas |
| Horizontal with tight gaps | Small, seasonal | Both faces | Middle | Modern lines, newer subdivisions |

Board-on-board is the answer for anyone whose actual goal is privacy. The boards alternate across the rails and overlap, so the seam of one board sits behind the solid face of the next. When the cedar shrinks, the overlap absorbs it and the screen stays closed. Both sides look finished, which quietly solves the neighbor-relations problem too — nobody gets the "back" of the fence. The tradeoff is material: you are covering the same run roughly twice, so expect meaningfully more cedar per foot. Labor is comparable. You are paying for wood, not for difficulty.

Three smaller things decide whether a fence is actually opaque:

- **Board profile and grade.** Clear cedar with tight knots is more expensive and more consistent. A lower grade with large loose knots will have holes in it, and a hole is a sightline. If privacy is the point, do not buy the cheapest grade and hope.
- **Kiln-dried after treatment.** If you go with pressure-treated pine, look for KDAT on the tag. Kiln-dried-after-treatment lumber has already done most of its shrinking before it reaches your yard, so the gaps that open later are smaller.
- **Bottom clearance and gate fit.** Boards set within an inch of grade wick water and rot, and a gate that drags in July hangs open by an inch in February. Both create gaps at exactly the height where a dog, a child, or a sightline gets through.

There is a simple test, and it is the one we use on our own installs. After dark, turn on every light inside the house and have someone walk the property line on the outside. If they can see light through the fence, the fence is not private — it is decorative. Privacy you can see is privacy that holds up when the leaves are gone and the lights are on.

## Where the Fence Ends and Screening Begins

The second-story problem is real in this county, especially in the newer subdivisions where houses sit close and decks climb. A 6-foot fence cannot fix a 14-foot window. Layering can.

The pattern that works is the one we recommend most: fence for the first six feet, evergreens for the second six. The fence gives you an instant, solid, code-compliant base. The evergreens grow above it and take over the upper sightline over three to five years.

Two species carry most of that work here. Emerald Green arborvitae is the narrow one — roughly 12 to 15 feet tall at maturity and only 3 to 4 feet wide, which makes it the right choice for a tight side yard or a narrow strip behind a garage. Plant them 3 to 4 feet on center for a hedge, closer if you are buying larger stock and want a faster wall. Green Giant arborvitae is the bigger, faster one, commonly putting on 3 feet or more a year and reaching 30 to 40 feet if you let it. Space those 5 to 6 feet on center. Green Giant will screen a second-story window; Emerald Green mostly will not.

On the lake lots around Lake Orion and the wooded parcels out toward Clarkston, the math is different and usually easier. Those yards tend to have mature trees close to the property line already, so the January sightline problem is smaller and the work is mostly about closing the one gap the trees never covered — typically the side of the house facing a neighbor's driveway. In the tighter subdivisions of Troy and Rochester Hills, the opposite is true: the lots are narrower, the houses are taller, and the second-story view is the whole problem. Same county, different plan. Anyone who quotes you the same fence twice without walking the lot has not looked at it.

Two honest caveats. First, an evergreen screen is not a screen for about three years. If you need privacy this winter, the fence is the only part of the plan that works immediately. Second, deer browse arborvitae in Oakland County, and winter is when they do it. Green Giant holds up better than Emerald Green in most local experience, but nothing is deer-proof, and a hard winter with a hungry herd can set a screen back a season. If deer pressure is heavy on your lot, plan for protection or choose a different species.

Behind the fence, keep 2 to 3 feet of clearance. Arborvitae planted tight against a wood fence trap moisture against the boards, make it impossible to inspect or restain, and heave the soil as they grow. That clearance also lets air move, which is what keeps the fence above the rot line.

For smaller problems, the layering does not have to be a hedge:

- **A screen panel on posts.** A 6-foot by 4-foot cedar panel set on two posts, placed at the one angle that leaks, fixes a single sightline for a few hundred dollars.
- **A pergola or slatted overhead.** In a small Auburn Hills or Troy yard, overhead structure blocks the view from a window above without adding fence height at the property line.
- **Lattice or decorative panels inside the fence line.** Placed as a screen rather than a topper, they stay out of the height measurement and out of the ordinance argument.

One special case deserves its own paragraph: hot tubs. An outdoor spa is both a privacy problem and a code problem, and the code is not optional. Under ISPSC Section 305, an outdoor spa needs a barrier at least 48 inches above grade measured on the side facing away from the spa, with no opening that passes a 4-inch sphere, no more than a 2-inch gap at the bottom, and a self-closing, self-latching gate. The barrier has to sit at least 20 inches from the water's edge. A 6-foot privacy fence around the yard satisfies the height easily, but the inspection almost always fails on the gate hardware, not the fence. If the spa has a lockable safety cover meeting ASTM F1346, the barrier requirement is waived — but a cover does not give you privacy, and a hot tub in January is exactly when you want the screen. Plan for both.

## The Winter Ground Line: Snow, Frost, and What Moves

Everything above assumes the ground stays where you put it. In Michigan, it does not entirely.

Michigan's residential code requires exterior footings and foundation systems to extend 42 inches below actual grade, and the frost line is the reason. Fence posts are not foundations in the code's sense, but the physics is the same: a post set 24 inches deep in Oakland County clay is sitting in soil that freezes and heaves every winter, and heaving soil pushes things up and sideways. A post set below the frost line, in concrete or well-compacted gravel, does not move. That is the difference between a fence that is straight in year five and one that leans by year three — and it is also the difference between a fence that stays six feet tall and one that starts losing height at the corners.

Then there is snow, which is the most overlooked privacy variable in the entire conversation. A settled January snowpack in a shaded Oakland County backyard runs 12 to 24 inches deep, and it does not politely stop at the fence. It buries the bottom rail, it piles against the boards, and it raises the effective ground line for everything standing on it. A 4-foot fence with 18 inches of snow against it is effectively a 2.5-foot fence. A dog that has never jumped the fence in July will walk over a snowbank in February without thinking about it, and a neighbor standing on a plowed pile sees over a fence they could not see over in summer.

That has practical consequences you can plan for now:

- **Leave a snow storage strip.** Do not plant right up to the fence line on the side where the plow or the shovel throws snow. Two or three feet of clear ground keeps the drift off the boards and gives you somewhere to put the snow.
- **Mark the posts before the ground freezes.** Six-foot reflective stakes at every corner and both gate posts. Plow drivers hit fences in January, and a stake costs less than a post.
- **Clear the bottom rail after a big snow.** Snow packed against the bottom rail keeps the board wet for weeks, and wet wood at grade is where rot starts. Ten minutes with a shovel in January saves a board replacement in June.
- **Do not force a frozen gate.** Ice in the latch and frost heave in the hinge post will make a gate stick. Forcing it bends hardware and splits stiles. Clear the ice, let it move, and check the latch alignment again in March.

The freeze-thaw cycle also does a number on anything metal. Gate hinges, latches, and screws back out slightly over a winter of expanding and contracting. A ten-minute tighten-up in November — hinges, latch strike, post caps — prevents the slow sag that makes a gate drag by February.

## A November Walk-Around: Twelve Checks

This is the audit. Do it once before the ground freezes and once in March, and you will catch almost everything before it becomes a repair.

| # | Check | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Walk the line at night with interior lights on | Light through boards = opacity problem, not height |
| 2 | Look from your highest window and your deck | Upper sightline you cannot solve with fence height |
| 3 | Look from the street and from both neighbors' side yards | "Around the fence" sightlines at ends and gates |
| 4 | Measure board gaps at three heights | Shrinkage gaps, usually worst at eye level |
| 5 | Check for loose knots and holes | Cheap grade showing its cost |
| 6 | Push every post at the top | Movement means shallow or heaved posts |
| 7 | Check the bottom rail and board ends for soft wood | Rot starting where snow sits |
| 8 | Look for soil piled against the fence | Grade raised above the bottom rail |
| 9 | Open and close every gate | Hinge sag, latch alignment, freeze damage |
| 10 | Tighten hinges, latches, and post caps | Cheap prevention before freeze-up |
| 11 | Note where the plow throws snow | Snow storage and stake placement |
| 12 | Photograph the whole perimeter | The comparison you will want in March |

If checks 4, 5, and 7 all fail on the same run, you are not looking at maintenance. You are looking at a fence that was built to a price, and the honest advice is to replace that run rather than restain it.

## Between Now and Frozen Ground

The digging season in southeast Michigan usually runs into late November, sometimes into early December if the snow comes before the hard freeze and insulates the soil. Once frost drives more than a few inches into the ground, post holes stop being a practical proposition, and the season is over until March.

That leaves a window, and it is a useful one. Permits take time — several of the cities in this county want a plot plan and a final inspection, and two to six weeks is a realistic turnaround in a busy fall. Layout work, material ordering, removal of an old fence, and gate hardware can all happen in cold weather. Staining cannot: most stains want temperatures above 50 degrees for a day or two on both sides of application, which in this county means the job waits for spring. We would rather tell you that than sell you a winter stain that fails in April.

If you are planning for spring, November and December are when the schedule is still open. Fence crews in Oakland County fill up from February onward, and the homeowners who call in January are often booking into May. A November estimate costs nothing and puts you at the front of the line when the ground thaws.

On cost, the honest 2026 numbers: installed cedar privacy fencing in this market runs roughly $35 to $80 per linear foot depending on height, style, grade, access, and how much old fence has to come out. A 150-foot backyard is typically a $5,250 to $12,000 project. Board-on-board sits at the upper end because of the extra material. A 20-plant Emerald Green screen, installed, runs roughly $1,200 to $3,000 and takes three years to become a screen. Neither number is a quote — a quote requires seeing the yard, the grade, the access, and the property line.

What we will say plainly: a fence is not the cheapest way to get privacy. It is the only way to get privacy that works in February, on a Tuesday, with the leaves down and the lights on. That is what Built to last actually means in this climate — not the warranty language, but the fact that the screen is still there when the trees quit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is my backyard less private in the winter?

Because the screening you had was deciduous foliage, and it leaves. Oakland County's median first fall frost falls in the first week of November, and most maples are bare by then. Add lower sun angles that light up interiors in the late afternoon, and a yard that felt enclosed in July reads as open by Thanksgiving. Pin oaks and some red oaks hold their leaves through winter, which is why a few neighborhoods keep their screening longer than others.

### Does a 6-foot fence block a neighbor's second-story window?

No. A 6-foot fence blocks ground-level sightlines from flat ground, but from a second-story window at 14 feet nearly the whole yard is visible. From a 3-foot deck the ground beyond about 16 feet past the fence is visible. Above-fence screening — Green Giant arborvitae, a pergola, or a screen panel — is the only fix that does not require a height variance you will not get.

### Why does my cedar fence have gaps now that it's cold?

Wood shrinks as it dries, mostly across the width of the board. Western redcedar shrinks about 5 percent from green to oven-dry, which is roughly a sixth of an inch on a 5.5-inch picket; southern pine shrinks more, closer to a quarter inch. A side-by-side fence butted tight in June opens thin gaps by January as heated air dries the boards. It is not a defect, and board-on-board construction prevents it by overlapping the seams.

### Is board-on-board worth the extra cost for privacy?

If privacy is the actual goal, yes. Board-on-board overlaps the pickets so the seams are covered and stay covered as the wood moves, and both faces look finished. It uses noticeably more cedar per foot than a side-by-side fence, but the labor is comparable, so the premium is material rather than difficulty. Shadowbox looks similar but is deliberately semi-private and lets air and sightlines through.

### Do arborvitae lose their leaves in winter, and how close should I plant them?

Arborvitae are evergreen and keep their foliage year-round, which is exactly why they work as above-fence screening. Plant Emerald Green 3 to 4 feet on center for a hedge; plant Green Giant 5 to 6 feet on center for a taller, faster screen. Keep 2 to 3 feet of clearance from a wood fence so air moves and the boards can be inspected and restained. Expect about three years before a new screen is genuinely private.

### Can you install a wood fence in November in Michigan?

Usually, yes, until the ground freezes hard — often late November, sometimes early December if snow insulates the soil. Posts still need to reach below Michigan's 42-inch frost depth, so the work does not get easier as the season closes. Staining should wait for spring, when temperatures hold above 50 degrees long enough for the finish to cure.

### Does snow make a fence effectively shorter?

Yes, in practice. A settled snowpack of 12 to 24 inches raises the effective ground line, buries the bottom rail, and lets a dog or a person stand higher than they could in summer. That is why we mark posts with tall stakes, keep a snow storage strip clear of plantings, and clear packed snow away from the bottom rail after big storms.

### Does a hot tub need a code-compliant privacy barrier?

A spa needs a barrier under ISPSC Section 305: at least 48 inches above grade, no opening that passes a 4-inch sphere, no more than a 2-inch gap at the bottom, a self-closing and self-latching gate, and a barrier at least 20 inches from the water's edge. A spa with a lockable ASTM F1346 safety cover is exempt from the barrier requirement, but not from needing privacy. In our experience the inspection fails on gate hardware far more often than on fence height.

## Protecting What Matters, in Every Season

Privacy in Michigan is a seasonal illusion until you build it. The trees will do their part for six months and then quit, and the fence has to pick up the rest — opaque in January, above the snow line, layered where the second story looks down, and built below the frost line so it is still standing straight in year ten.
